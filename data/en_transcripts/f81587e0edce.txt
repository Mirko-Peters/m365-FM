When SAP designed Fiori, it settled on five ideas that every Fiori app is supposed to follow. They are worth knowing because they explain why Fiori looks the way it does. Three pieces sit behind almost every Fiori app. Once you can name them, the jargon around Fiori gets a lot less intimidating. The first is SAPUI5, the JavaScript framework behind most Fiori apps. In plain terms, it is the toolkit SAP uses to build web screens that behave the same way everywhere, on any browser and any device. Part of their toolkit is open source under the name OpenUI5, which means anyone can read the code and build with it. The second is SAP Fiori Elements. This is a low-code approach where developers describe what an app needs and the framework builds the screens from ready-made templates called floor plans. It cuts down on hand coding and it keeps apps consistent because they all start from the same proven patterns instead of a blank page. A developer still decides what data matters and how the app should behave, but the look and the layout come almost for free. The third piece is OData, the common language a Fiori app uses to ask the SAP backend for data. When you open a list of sales orders, the app sends an OData request, the SAP system answers, and the screen fills in with real numbers. OData is worth remembering because when a Fiori app loads slowly or shows nothing at all, The problem usually lives in the service behind the screen. Developers build and test these apps in a cloud workspace called SAP Business Application Studio, which comes with the templates and tools already set up. Put the pieces together, and the picture gets clearer, because SAPUI5 and Fiori elements build what you see, and OData feeds it with live data. The launchpad hands it to you at the right moment, and the colorful tiles are just the friendly face on a fairly ordinary set of moving parts. There's one more layer you will hear about, and it is the one nobody notices. In many setups, a Fiori front-end server sits between the apps and the SAP business system, handling the launchpad, the logins, and the routing of those OData calls. You rarely think about it when it works. You hear about it constantly when it does not because it decides whether the whole experience feels fast or slow. Now here is where the Microsoft side enters the picture, and the cleanest place to see it is at sign-in. In many companies, people already carry a Microsoft account for email, Teams, and documents, and that same account can be the one that opens Fiori. Microsoft EntraID, Microsoft's cloud identity service, can act as the identity provider for SAP Fiori using a standard called SAML for single sign-on. Setting it up is a two-sided job. On the Microsoft side, EntraID holds the certificate and the claims that describe who the user is. On the SAP side, the system is configured to trust EntraID and the two sides trade the details through metadata files. The payoff is simple and it matters. A user signs in once with their Microsoft account and lands in Fiori without typing a second password. Fewer passwords means fewer support tickets and people spend their energy on the job instead of the login screen. The security side gains as well because one place decides who gets access and that access can be reviewed or taken away in one spot. One limit is worth stating clearly and it trips up a lot of teams. Signing in is not the same as being allowed to do everything. Microsoft proves who you are and SAP still decides what each person can see and do once they are inside. Both sides matter and treating them as one thing is a common and costly mistake. This sign-in link is usually the first place a company connects its Microsoft world to its SAP world.
