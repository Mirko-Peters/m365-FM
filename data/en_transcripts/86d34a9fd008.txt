The mistakes beginners make. Most of the trouble with real time projects comes from a handful of assumptions, and each one is easy to avoid. Once you've heard it. The first mistake is assuming you need enormous amounts of data. Microsoft says the opposite in its own documentation, because your data doesn't have to flow at high rates or volumes for this to be worth doing. What matters is whether reacting to an event beats waiting for a report. The next one is treating this as a faster power BI report when the system is event driven, instead, a signal arrives and something happens in response. If a number that changes once a day is enough for your decision, a scheduled refresh still does the job better and it costs less to run. People also forget to touch the default retention and caching settings. A new database keeps data for three thousand six hundred and fifty days by default, and it caches it for the same period. Those are generous defaults, and generous defaults cost money, so decide how long you actually need the data. Another common myth is forgetting what it takes to run events. Streams need at least an F4 fabric capacity, and that capacity is shared across every fabric workload in your organization. One busy event house can affect the reports. Other teams depend on plenty of teams over engineer as well. If a no code connector already moves your data, you don't have to stand up a Kafka cluster just to prove you're serious. There's also the alert trap. Switching on notifications without checking them against your history is how teams end up ignoring their own alerts. Kql isn't SQL, and treating it like SQL will slow you down until you adjust the honest limits and the cost conversation. Every tool has edges, and knowing where they are is what separates a calm rollout from a stressful one. The first limit is that fabric capacity is a shared pool capacity. Units are the currency, and every workload in the capacity draws from the same account. When usage gets high, an event house throttles to protect itself. At the first level, queries slow down while ingestion keeps flowing at the second, both ingestion and queries pause, though no data is lost. At the worst level, data is held for a period and can eventually be lost. Fabric also added workspace level surge protection. If one workspace crosses a usage threshold over a rolling twenty four hour period, it can be blocked automatically until usage drops or someone removes the block. You can mark a workspace as mission critical to exempt it from those rules. There's a smaller behavior to plan for as well. An event house suspends itself when nobody is using it, which saves money. But the first query after a quiet spell can take a few seconds to wake up. If your process can't tolerate that pause, there's a setting that keeps it always on. Event streams carry their own limits. A single message can be up to one megabyte. Event data is kept for up to ninety days, and delivery is at least once, which is a polite way of saying you might see the same event twice, designed for duplicates, and you'll be fine. Storage is billed separately from capacity in a fast cash tier and a standard tier, and the caching policy controls which data gets the fast treatment. All of that leads to a decision test I find genuinely useful.
