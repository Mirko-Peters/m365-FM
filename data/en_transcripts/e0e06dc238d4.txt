The report sees a free resource, but production sees an incomplete team. Skills aren't just a headcount issue. A plant can have enough operators in total and still lack the right skill at the moment a constrained process needs it. One qualified person may need to cover several cells, and another... might hold certification for a material, a welding process, a test method, or a customer-specific procedure. Those limits can change with the product mix. A week dominated by standard products may run fine with the normal shift pattern, but bring in more complex variants, and the same staffing level becomes a problem because each variant pulls on a smaller pool of qualified people. The total labor hours may look fine, while the schedule starts waiting for a particular skill. That's not a people problem. It's a planning input. Plants often keep skill data somewhere. Training records, staffing plans, local matrices, or the knowledge of a production leader. Each source has a reason to exist. The issue comes when a planning system treats all staffed hours as equal, even though the work requires different levels of approval and experience. They aren't equal. A serious mixed feasibility check links an operation to the people or roles it needs. That doesn't mean assigning every person months ahead in a rigid schedule. In many factories, that would create its own problems. It means the planning logic knows that certain work can only run on specific shifts, with specific roles available under rules the plant actually follows. Consider inspection. A product family may run through machining without much trouble, then need a specialist inspection step before release. The equipment could have time and the parts could be ready, but if the qualified inspector isn't present until the next day, the order can't move. The work is complete physically, but not operationally. Safety adds another layer. Some operations require a defined staffing level, and a person may be technically trained but unable to work alone on a shift. Maintenance activity may require a lockout procedure, supervision, or a second approved role. These aren't exceptions. A planning model can wave a way to make a date fit. They're part of the factory. Cross-training can change the answer over time, and it's often discussed as a general improvement target. That's fair, but planning needs something more concrete. Which skill gap actually causes missed dates and which qualification, if added to a shift, creates a real alternative route or extends the usable window at a constrained operation? Those questions turn cross-training into an operational choice. Maybe the best response to a recurring mix problem isn't another machine. It might be qualifying a second setter for a high-pressure cell, training an inspector on a common product family, or changing shift coverage, so approved support exists when the work reaches that stage. You only see those options when people appear in the model alongside equipment. This needs care. Skill data can change, certifications expire, people take leave. A planning model shouldn't pretend it knows more than it does. It should show where a plan relies on a named qualification, a thin coverage assumption, or a gap that someone must resolve before release. That makes the trade-off visible early. Once you include shift coverage, a capacity figure starts to look less like one number and more like a set of real-time windows. Some support standard work, others support complex work, some can absorb disruption, and others can only keep stable production moving. That's how the factory operates. And even when the right machine, tool, and person line up, the order still can't start unless the inputs for that order are ready. Material constraints turn capacity into waiting time. You can have the machine, the fixture, and the qualified person ready, and still lose the slot because the material isn't there. That sounds basic, but here's what actually happens in practice. Material constraints get treated as a supply chain problem somewhere outside production planning. The reality is that a missing component changes the sequence a factory can run and leaves capacity sitting idle, waiting for work that can't start. Take a common assembly order, the plant holds most of the parts, labor is scheduled, and the assembly station shows open time. Then one purchased electronic module arrives two days late. The order can't start, even though every capacity report around that station still looks healthy. The hours exist, but the work doesn't. That's the gap most planning systems miss. Raw material creates the same problem in process industries and discrete manufacturing. A product may need a specific alloy, batch, coating, resin, or approved supplier lot. The system says you have stock, but it's the wrong specification for that order. Maybe it's below the required quantity stuck on quality hold or already allocated to another customer commitment. So you have inventory, but you don't have usable material. Material availability needs to mean more than something similar sits in the warehouse. It has to answer whether the right material in the right quantity with the right approval status can reach the operation before that operation starts. That time element changes everything. A delivery due late in the week might support the monthly plan, but it can't support an order that needs to start Monday to ship on time. The calendar doesn't care about your stock levels. Every order runs against two clocks. One tracks the production route, and the other tracks when material and components become usable. If those clocks don't meet, the order waits. And when it waits, the schedule may fill that open slot with another job, which then shifts the setup sequence, the queue, and the availability of the next operation. A late component can ripple through a factory even when it only affects one order. That ripple is invisible on a standard capacity report. Now, substitute rules matter more than most planners realize. In many plants, you can't simply swap one material for another just because the inventory system shows a similar description. The substitute may need engineering approval. It may work for one customer and not another. It may affect traceability, process settings, quality checks, or even the finished product certificate. A substitute is a controlled option, not an emergency guess. Treating it otherwise creates risk that no schedule can fix. Lot restrictions add more limits. Some products need material from one approved source. Some require matched lots, others can't use stock held for another order because of customer ownership, export rules, or a quality issue still under review. These rules may sit across ERP quality systems, warehouse processes, and local knowledge on the floor. If the planning logic can't see them, it schedules fantasy. And that fantasy becomes real when the line stops. There's also work in process, or WIP. An order may have material available for its first operation, but depend on a semi-finished part from another route before it reaches final assembly. If that upstream work slips, downstream capacity turns into waiting time. The assembly team may stay busy with other work, but the original delivery promise now depends on a chain that no broad capacity total can explain. That chain is where the real constraints live. This is why material status from ERP needs shop floor context before you release work. ERP can tell you planned receipts, stock records, purchase orders, allocations, and bills of material. All useful planning facts. But ERP alone may not know that a delivery arrived at the gate but hasn't passed incoming inspection, that the available lot sits in a blocked location, or that the upstream operation has produced quantity but not released it for use. Without that context, your schedule is a guess. The factory needs a usable material view. That view links each order to its required inputs, their approved status, their expected availability, and the point in the route where they matter. Then, a feasibility check asks a practical question. If we schedule this order in this slot, will every required input actually be ready? Not theoretically, not at some point in the week, ready when the operation starts. Sometimes the answer changes the sequence. A planner may bring forward another order that has complete material rather than holding a machine and team for work that can't begin. Sometimes it triggers a supplier call, a quality decision or a commercial conversation before the schedule turns into a daily negotiation. That's far better than releasing everything and hoping the parts arrive in the right order. Hope is not a planning strategy. Material constraints don't remove capacity. They strand it. They turn planned production time into gaps, extra set-ups, and work that waits in front of the next step. A feasible product mix needs the right demand on the right resources, but it also needs each order's inputs to become usable at the moment the route requires them. And once due dates enter the picture, even a plan with enough material and enough hours can still overload the factory at exactly the wrong time. Due dates create a different kind of overload. A weekly capacity plan can fit perfectly and still fail by Tuesday morning. Here's why. Customer demand doesn't arrive as an even stream across a week or a month. orders carry due dates, and those dates concentrate demand on the same shared resource during the same short window, even when the total hours for the full period look manageable. That's the part that catches most planners. Picture a planner reviewing the next five working days. The combined load on the constrained finishing cell sits below the weekly calendar. That sounds safe. But most of the orders that need that cell, promise, shipment, by Wednesday, while the available time after Wednesday, cannot recover those commitments. The week fits, but the first half doesn't. And the second half can't fix it. This is a different form of overload. It isn't always visible as more work than the factory can complete in total. It shows up as too much work hitting the same resource before the same deadline. A plan that looks green at week level can already contain an impossible cluster of work at day level. That cluster is the real bottleneck. Time buckets can hide that problem. When you load work by month, a late slot looks as useful as an early slot. When you load by week, Friday appears interchangeable with Monday. For an order that must ship Tuesday, those hours have completely different meaning. Due dates turn capacity into a time-phased problem, and time-phasing is what most planning systems ignore. A realistic check asks not only how much load each resource receives, but when each operation must happen for the order to complete on time. The first operation needs a slot early enough for the next operation to follow. Then inspection, packaging, or final release may need their own time. The order doesn't need capacity sometime this week, It needs a chain of capacity at the right points in the week. That chain can break even where total hours remain open. The break point is when you find out your plan isn't real. Imagine several orders for different customers, all due near the same date. They come from different product families and use different early steps. As they move through production, they converge at one shared test station. Each order looks reasonable on its own. Together they create a peak that the station cannot absorb before shipment. That's the load the planner needs to see before releasing work to the floor. Once the peak appears, people often try to fix it with urgency. A late order gets higher priority. Someone asks the team to squeeze it in. Another order gets moved aside. The schedule changes, the team changes direction, and the displaced order becomes tomorrow's emergency. Expediting doesn't create time. It changes who waits. And it usually makes things worse. Sometimes that choice is right. A customer commitment, a safety-related part, or a commercial decision may justify moving one order ahead. But a feasibility process should expose the cost of that decision. Which orders move later? Which resource absorbs the extra pressure? Does the new priority force another change in sequence? Are you protecting one due date by quietly risking three others? Without those answers, priority becomes noise. And noise doesn't ship orders. Simple dispatch rules can help in stable conditions. Earliest due date, for example, means work the order with the nearest due date first. That rule can reduce lateness in some situations because it gives the team a clear way to choose from a queue, but it doesn't solve every mix problem, and stable conditions are rare in most plants. If two urgent jobs need a long changeover between them, running them strictly by due date may waste scarce time and delay both. If an order reaches the resource before its material or prior operation is ready, its early due date doesn't turn it into executable work. If one job consumes a narrow test asset later in the route, the best sequence may depend on more than the date printed on the order. Due dates need context, just like capacity does. Context is what makes the difference between a schedule and a wish list. This doesn't mean planners should ignore due dates in favor of a perfect machine sequence. Customer promises matter. It means the plan has to show the real trade-off between delivery risk and the cost of getting there. A schedule can then support a proper decision. Protect the date, negotiate the date, change the order, or choose a different production path where one exists. That conversation is much easier before the work hits the floor. After that, you're firefighting. I'd also be careful with monthly service measures when testing product mix. A plant can ship most orders by month end and still create daily chaos over time and strained customer relationships because too many commitments fell into the same few days. The monthly result may look acceptable while the operating pattern remains unstable. Customers don't experience your average capacity, they experience their promised date. And they remember when you miss it. So use broad capacity to screen demand, then test the mix against time-phased load at the operations that control delivery. Look at the day, shift, or finite slot that the work actually needs. Not only the period where the planning report can place it. Once too many orders compete for the same early slot, the production cost starts appearing in the work that waits. And that cost is almost always higher than you think. Queue time is often the cost. Nobody planned. Here's the problem most manufacturers don't talk about. When too many orders all need the same early slot, the first thing you see is a queue. Work reaches an operation and just sits there because the resource, person, tool or process approval it needs is busy with something else. That wait often adds more lead time than the actual work, especially in a complex plant where an order moves through several handoffs and each one depends on a finite slot being free. Now, a queue isn't automatically bad. Some waiting exists in almost every factory because demand and process times don't arrive in a perfectly smooth pattern. The problem starts when the queue grows because the planned mix keeps feeding more work into a constrained step than that step can clear. Then waiting becomes the normal state. Picture a test area at the end of several production routes. Parts arrive from machining, assembly, and rework. The test team sees a full staging area, so it looks like there's plenty of work available. Meanwhile, upstream teams keep producing because their local schedules tell them to stay busy. Here's the thing. Those parts don't become shipments while they wait. They become work in process. WEP and WIP can create a false sense of progress because you've already consumed material, labor, and machine time. People can point to racks full of partly completed product, but the customer needs finished, released product, not a growing collection of orders parked before the same constrained operation. More WIP doesn't fix the flow, It often makes the problem harder to manage. Once a queue grows, the team has to figure out which order belongs to which customer, which job has a real due date risk, which parts need further checks, and which material or paperwork still needs attention. The physical search effort grows right along with the queue and priorities start to collide. A late order gets moved to the front because someone escalated it. That may protect one shipment, but every urgent order that jumps the queue pushes other work further back, and those displaced orders become the next set of escalations a day or two later. The queue turns into a priority auction, except nobody sees the full cost of winning. This is why a product mix can look manageable when you add up the total hours, but still damage delivery performance. The mix might create a heavy wave of work at one shared step. Upstream capacity keeps converting raw material into WIP, while the constrained step releases finished orders at its own pace. The plant feels busy. Lead time grows anyway. There's a simple idea from operations management called Little's Law. You don't need the formula for the practical point. When more work sits in a system and the completion rate doesn't rise with it, the average time work spends in that system increases. Put less formally, if you keep putting more cars into a traffic jam, the trip takes longer. That doesn't mean the answer is always to reduce WIP blindly. Some processes need buffers. Long lead time materials, batch processes, and normal variation can all justify planned inventory between steps. The point is to know why the buffer exists, how much it should contain, and whether it protects flow or simply hides an overload created by the wrong mix. A planned buffer supports the schedule. An accidental queue consumes it. This distinction changes the planner's job. Instead of releasing all orders that appear due soon, the planner can ask which orders should enter the system now without flooding the operation that controls completion. That might mean holding work upstream, changing the release order, or protecting limited capacity for the products that need it most. Holding an order can feel uncomfortable. People often read it as inactivity, especially when an upstream machine has open time. But starting work early only helps when the next steps can absorb it in time. If the order will wait for days at the constraint, early release can tie up cash, floor space, and attention without improving shipment. The best release decision isn't always the earliest release. It's the release that supports a feasible path through the factory. That requires a view of the order route, the current queue, the due date, and the finite capacity ahead. Without that view, teams do what they can with local information, and the usual response is to keep equipment running, which leads to another uncomfortable point. Local efficiency can look good while flow through the factory gets worse. Local OEE can support a bad mix. There's a habit in manufacturing that causes trouble here. When delivery performance slips, people often look first at machine utilization and OEE, or overall equipment effectiveness. That makes sense up to a point. OEE helps you see whether a resource loses time through availability losses, slower running, or quality losses. If a machine stops often, runs below its expected rate, or produces reject parts, you need to understand that. But OEE answers a resource question. It doesn't answer the product mix question, Picture a plant where each department has a clear target to keep its machines busy. The cutting area keeps cutting, the machining area keeps machining, assembly keeps building, everyone can report strong utilization, and several resources may even improve their OE during the month. Meanwhile, finished orders still leave late. How can that happen? Because the work being produced may not match the work that needs to flow. An upstream cell can run efficiently on the easiest available orders, even if those orders will wait behind a constrained process later in the route. The cell looks productive. The factory gains more WIP and no extra shipments. That isn't a failure by the people running the cell. They're often doing exactly what their local measures ask them to do. The problem comes from treating local activity as proof of system output. They're related, but they're not the same. A factory doesn't earn delivery performance when an individual operation finishes. It earns delivery performance when the full order completes, passes the required checks, and ships when promised. So a machine can have a strong OEE number while the product mix creates poor flow. Imagine a broad machining group that can process many product families. The team sees a gap in the schedule and runs the available work that needs the least changeover. That choice may improve machine time and reduce setup loss. It can even appear sensible from the perspective of that machine. Yet the jobs they choose may feed a downstream process that already has too much work, while more urgent orders wait for a different machining step. The local machine stays busy. The constrained path receives more work it can't clear. The orders that matter for shipment remain incomplete. That's the conflict. OEE remains useful because it helps production teams improve the performance of equipment. A poor availability pattern at a shared resource can reduce what the schedule can realistically promise. Slow cycles can change the load. Quality losses can reduce usable output. You need that information. But OEE cannot decide which product should run next. It has no built-in understanding of customer dates, product routes, shared fixtures, material readiness, or the current sequence at another process. It can tell you a machine lost time. It can't tell you whether recovering that time will improve finished order throughput for the planned mix. Those are different decisions and they need different views. For product mix planning, bring the views together. Look at the constraint load created by the actual orders. look at flow through the required operations, look at delivery risk by due date, then use OEE to understand whether the resource performance assumptions inside that plan are credible. That puts each measure in the right place. A planner might see that a test process controls shipment for a certain product family. The team can then ask whether its planned capacity reflects actual performance, including common stops and quality losses. If the answer is no, the model needs a better assumption. If the answer is yes, extra activity upstream may simply create more waiting work. This is also why keep everything running, can become an expensive rule. When the mix is wrong, full local utilization can produce more handling, more space pressure, more searches for parts, and more arguments about which order deserves priority. The plant spends effort managing work that shouldn't have entered that part of the flow yet. A busy factory can still miss dates. I'm not suggesting idle machines should become the goal. That would be just as simplistic. The goal is to use resources in a way that supports the orders the factory needs to finish, under the limits that govern the current mix. Sometimes that means an upstream resource runs a different order. Sometimes it means the resource prepares for a later job. Sometimes it means the team accepts a short gap because releasing more work would only overload the next shared step. That can feel counterintuitive when a utilization report turns red, but a red utilization indicator may be less harmful than racks of product waiting for the one operation that controls shipment. The plan needs to judge work by its effect on flow delivery and the current constraint, not only by whether every asset stayed active. There's one more complication. Even a well-sequenced mix can change after production starts, because the planned route assumes parts move through it without unexpected quality loss. Yield, rework, and scrap distort the plan. Here's the problem I keep seeing. A schedule looks clean on paper when every routing time adds up nice and neat. But then, quality shows up that week, and suddenly the plan starts losing capacity it never accounted for. Nobody planned for that. Most planning standards assume a normal path. A part moves through its operation, passes inspection, and goes to the next step. And sure, you have to assume that You can't plan every order as if failure is guaranteed. But you still need an honest range around that assumption. So here's what changes. Some product families need more inspection than others. A new variant might need extra checks. A tight tolerance can mean more measurement, adjustment, or first-piece approval. And a customer-specific configuration might require a test sequence that standard work never touches. The product mix changes the quality load. That's the simple truth. Picture two weeks with the same planned hours on a machining cell. First week, the cell runs proven, repeat parts, everything smooth. Second week, it runs more complex parts that need extra checks after machining and more detailed testing before release. The machine load barely changes, but inspection and test suddenly face a very different kind of demand. And that changes whether the whole order finishes on time. Now, rework adds another path that standard routing treats as a rare exception. When a part needs correction, it might return to an earlier operation, wait for a specialist review, need a new test, or consume time on the same constrained resource that the original plan already loaded. The part doesn't vanish just because it failed the first time, It stays in the factory, competes for material handling and attention, and it can eat up scarce production time again. If the mix includes products with a higher chance of rework, a plan based on first-pass routing alone can look comfortable while the real week gets crowded. But I'm not saying you should slap a fixed rework penalty on every order and call it done. Rework patterns differ by product, process, material, and current conditions. A broad average might still hide the same problem, just with the more official number attached to it. Use what the factory already knows. If a product family regularly needs extra test time, Model that demand where it happens. If certain variants carry a known risk of a return loop, run scenarios with a reasonable allowance and make the assumption visible. If the data is too weak to support a precise estimate, say so rather than pretending the schedule has certainty it doesn't have. A plan handles an identified uncertainty far better than an unspoken one. Scrap creates a related issue, though it behaves differently. A reworked part consumes more process time. A scrapped part wastes material and the capacity already spent before anyone decides it can't ship. Then the factory might need to build a replacement, adding new work exactly when the schedule already feels tight. Scrap isn't just a finance number you record after the shift ends. For a constrained operation, scrap can remove capacity twice. First, the resource spends time producing the failed part. Then it might need to spend more time producing a replacement. If the product needs scarce material, the replacement may also wait for supply, turning a quality issue into a delivery issue. That link matters when the plant decides which mix to accept or release. Quality holds create another kind of delay. A batch might finish its physical work, but remain blocked while someone reviews a result, investigates a deviation, checks traceability, or waits for a disposition decision. The order looks nearly complete in one system, but it can't move to shipment. Production sees finished parts, the customer still sees nothing. When the mix includes more regulated products, new products, or products with tight inspection rules, those hold points need to sit in the feasibility view. You can't plan away every possible hold, but the plan should know that a route includes a release step, and that the release has a real lead time or limited review capacity. Otherwise, the factory plans to ship parts before it has permission to ship. In practical terms, separate stable facts from uncertain performance. Stable facts include required inspections, test operations, approval steps, and known rework routes. Performance assumptions include expected first pass yield, likely rework demand, and the chance that a hold takes longer than normal. Both belong in the discussion, but they don't carry the same confidence. So when the mix shifts toward products with more quality exposure, planners need to see the effect before release. They need to know whether the inspection team can absorb the load, whether replacement demand threatens a constrained process, and whether the promised date leaves enough room for the route to handle normal variation. That's not pessimistic planning. It's planning for the factory that actually produces the parts. And after quality alters the route, another question follows. How much of the planned machine time can you genuinely expect to survive the week? Reliability changes, effective capacity. Let's talk about effective capacity, because that's another place where the plan gets distorted. Planned capacity often assumes a resource will behave like its calendar. If the machine has eight scheduled hours, the plan treats those eight hours as available, maybe reduced by a standard efficiency factor. But production rarely gets a week that clean. A machine can stop. A process can drift. A product change can need extra warm-up, adjustment, or cleaning. Maintenance can find wear that needs attention before it turns into a larger failure. None of that means the plan failed. It means the plan needs to account for how the equipment actually behaves. Here's the distinction. Effective capacity is the time you can reasonably expect to use for the work in front of you. That's not the same as rated speed multiplied by calendar hours. Rated speed usually comes from stable conditions with the right material, a known setup, and no unexpected interruptions. A real schedule includes product changes, operator handovers, normal stops, plant maintenance, and the occasional problem nobody could predict. The question isn't whether uncertainty exists. It always does. The question is whether your planning process treats uncertainty as part of the production system or hides it behind a clean capacity number and waits for the shop floor to absorb the difference. Now, product mix changes this more than many people expect. A machine might run one family of products for days with few issues, then behave completely differently when the schedule moves toward a more demanding family. Maybe the new material creates more toolware. Maybe the product needs tighter settings and more checks. Maybe each changeover leaves the machine less stable for the first part of the run. Reliability can depend partly on what you ask the resource to produce. Picture a shared machining cell. The planner sees room in the calendar and brings forward a group of complex parts. On paper, their standard runtime fits. In practice, the parts need frequent tool checks, longer adjustments, and more maintenance intervention because the process puts extra stress on a component already close to its service limit. The machine doesn't simply lose time. The planned mix changes the chance and shape of that lost time. That's why maintenance and planning need a more direct conversation. Preventive maintenance doesn't compete with production the same way an unplanned stop does. It protects the resource from a bigger disruption later, but that doesn't make the timing easy when urgent orders arrive. If maintenance needs a four-hour window on a constrained resource, that window belongs in the feasibility check. Skipping it might create a short-term slot for an order, but the schedule should show the assumption clearly. We are taking a known maintenance risk to protect the state. Sometimes leadership may choose that risk. Often, once they see the likely downstream effect, they'll choose another option. A plan should support that choice, not quietly bury it. Now, I know it's tempting to solve this with one universal availability percentage. Take every machine's calendar, remove a fixed allowance, and call the remainder realistic capacity. That can be useful as an early rough screen. But it can also create false confidence. A fixed allowance doesn't distinguish a stable product from a difficult one. It doesn't know whether the resource faces repeated changeovers, whether a tool reaches its life limit during the planned batch, or whether a maintenance task must happen before a product can run safely. It turns different operational conditions into one average number. The factory still has to live with the differences. A better approach uses scenarios. Start with the normal operating case based on recent and credible performance for that process and product family. Then test the disruption case that production recognizes as plausible. A maintenance intervention, a slower start after a changeover or reduced output from a resource with a known issue. You don't need to predict the exact minute of a stoppage. You need to know whether the mix can absorb a credible loss of time without turning every customer commitment into an escalation. If one modest disruption causes multiple orders to miss their dates, the plan has very little room in it, even if the main capacity report stays green. That's a useful finding before release. So reliability doesn't mean planning for disaster every week. It means separating hopeful capacity from capacity that can survive normal factory conditions. When the product mix puts extra strain on a shared resource, the realistic question isn't whether the machine has hours on its calendar, it's whether those hours will still exist when the work reaches the machine. Utilities and plant services can become constraints. Here's something that often gets overlooked. There's another form of capacity that sits completely outside the main production schedule. The machine, tool, material, and team may all be ready, but the plant service that supports the process simply doesn't have enough room to handle the load. And that's where the plan starts to break down. Now here's why that actually matters in practice. Some product families consume shared services in very different ways, and the mix can push those services past a safe or practical limit long before the main work centers even look full on the report. The constraint shows up somewhere nobody was watching. Take heat treatment. It's a classic example. A furnace doesn't work like a machining center with a simple hourly slot for every part. Instead it has a chamber size, a recipe, a heat cycle, a cool-down period, loading rules and sometimes restrictions on which materials can run together. So the furnace calendar can look wide open while the actual batch space has already disappeared. You just can't see it from the schedule. Consider a plan that includes several orders all needing the same heat cycle before shipment. Each order looks manageable by itself, but put them into the same week and the oven becomes a shared constraint. You can't just add another order because the nominal machine capacity report still shows open hours elsewhere. The batch process sets the pace, not the machine time. Compressed air creates the same kind of problem, especially when several high-demand processes run at the same time. A plant may have enough equipment capacity for a group of machines, but not enough stable air supply to run every air-intensive operation together without affecting pressure, process quality or other production areas. And that limit never appears in an ERP routing. Nobody flags it. Power demand works exactly the same way. Some plants manage peak electrical load because large equipment like ovens, welders, chillers or test rigs draw heavily at certain times. A product mix that shifts toward energy-heavy work changes the loading pattern across the day, even if the total production hours don't change. The order book may fit perfectly, but the utility window may not. Cooling water, steam, vacuum, clean room capacity, extraction and wastewater treatment also control what work can run at the same time. These aren't background details you can ignore. For the processes that depend on them, they are production resources. Picture a plant with several coating operations. Each booth may have a free operator and a clear schedule. Yet the extraction system only supports a limited number of booths at full use under the plant's safety rules. Running everything at once doesn't create more throughput. It creates a condition the plant shouldn't run. Safety rules define capacity too. Sometimes the restriction comes from the environment rather than a physical limit. process needs a controlled temperature range. A clean room supports only a fixed amount of activity before cleaning or monitoring is needed. Certain materials can't run together because of contamination risk, fire risk, or emissions rules. Those conditions shape the feasible mix in ways that never show up on a capacity report. And this gets missed because plant services often belong to separate teams. Production owns the order sequence. Engineering owns the process rules. Facilities manages utilities. Environmental health and safety teams set operating limits. Each group sees its own piece of the problem. The plan needs to connect those parts before the week begins, not after the schedule has already broken. Now, the good news is you don't need to build a full physics model of every utility system just to improve mix planning. Start with the shared services that repeatedly force last-minute changes. If heat treatment capacity drives delivery, model the furnace recipes, batch rules, and calendar. If peak power constrains certain work, model the approved operating windows. If clean room access limits product release, treat that capacity as finite. use the limits that actually alter the decision. There's a useful distinction here. A machine resource tells you whether a process can perform work. A plant service tells you whether the plant can support that process while other work runs. Both conditions must hold at the same time. One without the other doesn't produce a finished order. This can also change investment discussions. A plant appears to need another production machine when the actual constraint sits in cooling, extraction, test power, or oven batch capacity. Buying the machine might increase local activity while the shared service still limits output. That's an expensive way to discover where the constraint lives. So when you test a product mix, widen the definition of capacity. Include the services, batch windows, environmental rules, and safety limits that decide whether several orders can run together. The resource that controls the plan may not look like production equipment at all. And when that constraint stays invisible, its cost rarely lands in one obvious place. The hidden cost shows up across the factory. When a bad product mix hits an unseen constraint, the cost doesn't arrive as one neat line in a finance report. A better mixed feasibility process So here's a better approach to mix feasibility. You start before anyone freezes the weekly schedule, and instead of treating one forecast as a promise, you treat it as one demand scenario among several the factory might need to handle. That sounds like a small change, but it completely changes the conversation. Instead of asking whether the plan fits a single forecast, you ask what happens if demand shifts toward a difficult family, an urgent order arrives, or a customer changes the required date. Planning turns into a test of choices, not a defense of one number. You start with firm demand, then layer in what's likely possible or still under discussion, and you keep those categories clear because nobody benefits when a tentative sales opportunity quietly enters the production plan and later gets treated as a customer commitment. For each scenario, you check the production facts that decide whether work can flow. Current route, planned process times, resource calendars, setup needs, tool availability, skills by shift, and material assumptions. You're not verifying every master data field in the company, just the facts that can change the answer to the decision in front of you. A feasibility process needs explicit assumptions. Record a material delivery date as uncertain if it is. If a route can use an alternate machine only after engineering approval, don't count that machine as open capacity until that approval exists. And if a standard time no longer matches the product revision, flag it before the order creates a problem on the floor. The goal is visible reasoning, not false precision. Next, run a finite check before you promise a date or release the work. Finite means each order consumes time on a real resource within a real calendar, competing with all other orders for that resource, and the system respects the conditions you've chosen to model. But the result should not just say yes or no. A useful result tells the team where the pressure lands, when it lands, and what that pressure does to the rest of the plan. Perhaps the new demand fits if a batch runs in one campaign, or it fits only if a qualified setter covers a later shift. or perhaps it misses because one test step becomes overloaded for two days, even though the main production area still looks open. Those are very different decisions. When you compare scenarios, don't reduce everything to total machine load. Compare constraint demand over time, likely flow time, delivery risk, work in progress, and the expected margin effect, because the choice with the highest apparent revenue may produce a poor operational result if it creates late committed work, extra overtime, or a queue that takes weeks to clear. You need to see the trade-offs together. Now that also means the process can't belong only to planning. The planner may own the schedule, but sales owns part of the demand decision, production knows whether the sequence is workable, supply chain knows the material risk behind the dates, and finance can frame the cost and return. Each group needs the same starting model, even when they reach different conclusions. Picture an exception meeting around a late customer request, where instead of debating whose capacity number is right, the group sees a few feasible paths and the conditions attached to each one. accept the order and delay lower priority work, accept part of it with a later balance, use an approved outside process, or change the sequence and accept more setup effort. Then someone with the right authority chooses. That human decision remains part of the process. A system can calculate that one option protects due dates while another protects margin, but it can't decide what the business should value most in a specific commercial situation unless leaders have already set that rule. It stops judgment from operating without a clear view of what each choice costs in capacity, time, and risk. Don't rebuild the whole plan for every minor event, but don't wait until the weekly meeting when a known change has already broken the schedule. The process should match the decision speed you need. Capture the orders already waiting, already running, or due to arrive at the constraint during the horizon. And then you can test a proposed mix against the work the factory has already promised to complete. Start with a small set of rules. Perhaps some variants require a specific fixture, the resource needs a changeover between product groups, or one shift cannot run a certain test without qualified cover. Add the rules that repeatedly change the plan, not every possible detail that might exist somewhere in documentation. A model earns trust by explaining the decisions people face. Historical testing helps here. Take past weeks where the product mix created a visible queue or caused delivery dates to move, feed the known order mix into the model using the rules and dates that applied at the time, then compare the result with what production experienced. Did the model point to the same constrained resource, show pressure in roughly the same period, and identify which variants competed for the limited time? You shouldn't expect a perfect replay because shop floor work includes interruptions, judgment calls, and changes that nobody recorded cleanly. But if the model consistently misses the problem people remember, don't add a dashboard and call it done. Find out which planning fact is absent or wrong. Maybe standard times need work, the tool rule never entered the routing, or the resource calendar treats an unavailable shift as open. Those gaps tell you where better data can change an actual decision. That's a much better reason to improve data than simply because a field exists. There's a human side to this as well. Bring the planner, the production lead, and the people closest to the selected constraint into the first model review, because they'll often spot an assumption that looks harmless in a system, but creates trouble in the real process. Listen carefully when someone says, that only works if... That phrase usually points directly to a constraint worth modeling. Don't try to win the first project by proving that every order can be scheduled automatically. Win it by helping the team answer one recurring question with less guesswork, less manual checking, and fewer surprises after release. If the model can show why one mix fits and another does not, it has already changed the conversation. From there, expand only when the next constraint genuinely affects the decision, and the scope can grow with evidence rather than turning into a long data project that loses contact with the production problem it started with. Ownership, data quality, and change control. Here's the real challenge with building a planning model that actually works in practice. Once it starts influencing real decisions on the floor, somebody needs to own each piece of it, not in that vague sense where everyone owns the data. because we both know that just means nobody can fix it when a planner spots a problem on a Tuesday morning. You want a named owner for every planning fact. Engineering owns product routes, approved alternates, and the date when an engineering change becomes active. Production owns the practical rules around setups, resource use, and what a process can actually handle. Tooling, maintenance, quality, workforce. Each team needs a clear scope for the facts they control. Now, that doesn't mean one person has to manually maintain every record. It means people know who can confirm a rule, who can approve a change, and who has to act when the schedule depends on data that doesn't match what's happening on the floor. Take a standard process time. It starts as an engineering estimate, and then production learns through repeated runs that a newer variant actually takes longer at a certain operation. If that difference changes which product mix can fit, the team needs a real path from observation on the shop floor to reviewed planning data. Otherwise the planner just keeps compensating in private and nobody knows the model is drifting. The feedback loop has to be simple enough to use under pressure. An operator, team lead, or scheduler should be able to flag that a setup duration, routing, tool condition, or resource rule no longer fits the work. The right owner reviews it, checks the evidence, and either changes the model or explains why the current rule still holds. A complaint without a resolution path just becomes another note in someone's inbox. Engineering changes need extra care because a routing rarely changes in isolation. A new product revision might use a different material, require a new inspection step, add a tool, or permit an alternate machine under specific conditions. The planning model needs to know not just the new route, but when it applies and which open or future orders it affects. A route marked as approved isn't enough when the new fixture won't arrive until next month. Version control gives the team a way to trace those decisions. When a feasibility result changes, people should be able to ask which routing version, calendar, setup rule, and resource state shaped that answer. That doesn't mean turning planning into a paperwork exercise. It means you can explain why the answer changed instead of restarting the argument from scratch. Traceability protects both the planner and the floor. It also helps to separate constraints from assumptions. A qualified person required for a regulated process is a hard constraint. A setup expected to take four hours based on recent work is an assumption. Material on a quality hold is a hard constraint until release. While a supplier delivery date might be a planning assumption with a stated confidence level, those categories should not blur together. When leaders review a product mix scenario, they need to see where the plan is actually blocked and where it depends on a judgment call. If a scenario works only because a supplier meets an uncertain date or because an experienced setter stays late, that needs to appear clearly in the result. A feasible plan with hidden assumptions is just a polite form of surprise. Data quality also needs a practical standard. You don't need every field completed to build a useful model. You need the data that changes a production decision to be accurate enough, current enough, and owned by someone who can answer for it. That puts the attention in the right place. Fix unused attributes with poor data when they become relevant. But if the calendar for a constrained resource repeatedly misstates available time, deal with that first. And if tools appear available in a system while they sit in repair, the mix decision will keep failing no matter how tidy the rest of the data looks. is about fitness for the planning decision, governance often sounds like a monthly meeting with a slide deck and a long list of actions. That's not the kind of governance this work needs. It needs everyday discipline, a clear owner, a visible change, a review path, and a way for execution to correct planning facts. The model stays useful only when it stays connected to the work. As the scope grows, this discipline becomes even more important. Without it, a planning model gradually turns into another static record of how people once believed the factory operated. With it, the model can keep pace with new products, changed routes, repaired tools, skill changes, and the normal mess of production. The aim isn't perfect data. It's a planning model that changes when the factory changes, before the next capacity report tells everyone they're green again. Common ways mix planning goes wrong. Once you start putting ownership and change control around the model, a few recurring mistakes become easier to spot. Most of them don't come from careless people. They come from planning shortcuts that work often enough until the mix changes and exposes the assumption underneath. One mistake is treating average capacity as a delivery commitment. A monthly report can show that a work center has room overall, while the actual orders compete for the same narrow time window, the same process step, or the same setup sequence. Average load answers a screening question, but it doesn't answer whether individual orders can finish on their promised dates. Think about a resource with open time late in the week, while most of the mix needs it before Wednesday. The weekly total might fit, but the customer dates don't. If someone accepts demand based only on that weekly number, Production inherits a problem that no amount of calendar arithmetic can solve. Time matters as much as hours. Another mistake is assuming an alternate resource solves an overload. On paper, a second machine may carry the same operation code. That can be useful, but it doesn't mean the product can move there today. The alternate route might need a fixture that isn't free, a program that hasn't been released, an operator with specific qualifications, or quality approval for that product revision. Sometimes it can run there, but at a different rate with more scrap risk, or only after a change that disrupts another planned job. An alternate resource is an option only when its conditions are true. Teams also release more work when queues start growing. It feels like a sensible response because the floor needs work, and managers don't want people or machines waiting. Yet when work already waits at the shared constraint, releasing more of the same mix usually adds more work in progress without increasing completed orders. The queue gets deeper, and priorities become louder. That behavior often comes from local measures. An upstream area sees open time, so it keeps producing. The constrained operation is sees more material arriving than it can process. Supervisors then spend their day sorting, moving, and escalating while the schedule loses any stable order. The factory stays busy, but delivery still slips. Then there's the mistake of talking about AI before you have usable factory constraints. Someone asks for a co-pilot to recommend the best mix or a machine learning model to predict the schedule. Those tools may help later, but they can't repair a missing root rule, an unknown fixture limit, or a calendar that claims a skilled operator is available when they aren't. The system needs a model before it needs a clever interface. If the model doesn't state which products can run on which resources, under what conditions, then an AI tool can only make a polished guess. A confident explanation of an impossible plan remains an impossible plan. The language may sound better than the old spreadsheet, which is not the same thing as improving the decision. There's some dry irony there. We'll ask generative AI to explain the factory before we've explained the factory to ourselves. The last mistake is measuring a planning project by the number of system connections. Teams may connect ERP data, MES events, maintenance records, and machine signals then declare progress because more data now flows into one place. Data movement isn't the outcome. The test is whether a planner can make a better choice before releasing work. Can they see that an accepted order consumes the last usable slot at a constrained process, test an alternate route, and see the tool, skill, and approval conditions attached to it, and give sales a date that reflects the actual production path? If those questions still require phone calls, private workbooks, and a meeting to reconcile conflicting numbers, the integration work hasn't reached the decision yet. That doesn't mean the project failed. It means the next task is clear. Move from connected records to governed production logic, then judge the work by fewer avoidable schedule changes, clearer exception decisions, and a plan that the floor can recognize as real. The planner's job changes once that model exists. They no longer spend all their time proving why the plan can't work. They can show the choices and let the business decide which consequence it's willing to own. The planner's decision changes when the model exists. Think about it this way. It's Tuesday afternoon. Sales just dropped a late demand increase for a high margin product family, and the customer wants delivery inside the current planning window. Without a detailed mix model, the conversation starts with a capacity report. Someone spots unused hours on a machine group and says the plant can handle it. The planner knows that answer is probably wrong, but proving why takes half a day of calls, checks, and manual changes. And that's if they're fast. Now, with the model in place, the planner starts somewhere else entirely. They add the proposed order to the current plan with its actual product revision, route, due date, quantity, material status, every condition attached to its process steps. Then the system tests the consequence against work already committed. Maybe the order fits, but only if it uses the shared test resource during a narrow slot on Thursday, and that pushes two existing orders beyond their promised dates. or perhaps it fits through machining, but the required fixture is already tied up with another commitment and can't support parallel work. So the answer becomes specific, and the planner now compares real options rather than arguing from a single capacity total. They can accept the full order, defer it, accept only part, use a permitted substitute if product and customer rules allow, send a process step to an approved outside supplier, add a shift, or change the sequence and accept the extra setup time. Each option carries a cost and a condition. For example, adding a shift might create enough time at the constraint. but only if qualified people can cover it and the material arrives on time. Outsourcing might protect internal flow, but it adds cost and introduces another supply risk. Deferring the request may protect existing commitments, though sales could lose an opportunity the business wants to pursue. Nobody gets a free option, that's the whole point. A good model also exposes the downstream effect. It doesn't stop at the first overloaded resource, It traces the proposed work through the entire remaining process, showing which orders lose their place, which delivery dates move, which tools become unavailable, and where the plan depends on uncertain conditions. That changes the tone of the meeting. Instead of production saying no and sales challenging the number, the entire team sees the same choices. They ask whether the customer will accept a split delivery, decide whether overtime is justified, protect a prior commitment and negotiate a later date, or accept the risk openly if the order carries a wider commercial reason. But here's the thing. The decision still belongs to people. A planner shouldn't become the person who decides which customer matters more. And a scheduling engine shouldn't quietly choose between a contract, a long-term relationship, and a high-margin urgent request, just because someone set a weight in a model months ago. The model informs the choice. It doesn't own it. So the result needs to show its assumptions alongside its recommendations. If the order fits only if a supplier meets a planned date, say it. If it depends on a setup duration drawn from limited history, show it. If the system lacks current tool status, don't bury that gap behind. a green status. Uncertainty needs a place in the decision. I think that's where many planning projects either earn trust or lose it. People don't expect a system to know everything, but they do expect it to admit what it knows, what it assumes, and what it cannot confirm. A model that shows its limits gives experienced planners room to apply judgment. Say the planner presents two feasible paths– One protects the new order, but creates a delivery risk for a committed customer. The other protects current dates, but requires the new customer to accept a later delivery. The system shows the resource demand, sequence impact, and stated assumptions for both. A commercial leader then makes a commercial choice with operational facts in front of them. That's a better use of technology than asking a dashboard to declare there is capacity, or asking AI to produce a confident answer from partial context. The planner moves from defending a schedule to framing a decision that people can understand and own. and the factory receives a plan with an explicit choice behind it. Not another urgent order that arrives on the floor with no one willing to explain what it displaced. Available capacity is not a feasible product mix. A capacity report still has a place. It can screen demand, show broad pressure, and tell you where to look first, but it cannot promise that a proposed product mix will actually run. Feasibility depends on the product, the process it needs, the resource and time available, and the conditions around that work, in other words, the constraints that turn nominal hours into actual production choices. When you test those conditions before release, you reduce schedule churn before it reaches supervisors, operators, and customers. You give sales and operations a shared way to discuss what a new order will displace. A green capacity report can still describe an impossible production week. So if you're working through this in your own plant, I'd like to hear how you test product mix feasibility before you commit a customer date. That's where the rubber meets the road, and it's the place where a good model earns its keep.
