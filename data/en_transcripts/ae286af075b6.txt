What a calculation group actually is. A calculation group is a special table inside your model that holds reusable rules, and each rule is called a calculation item. The important part is that a calculation item can be applied to whatever measure is on screen. So instead of writing one year to date, measure for sales, another for orders and another for profit, you write the rule once, then you point it at any of them in a report, a calculation group shows up as a table with a single visible column. That column is the thing people drag into a visual or a slicer. Put it in a matrix and each calculation item becomes its own column of numbers. Put it in a slicer and the user flips between year to date, last year and the plain total, while the layout stays exactly the same. So you write one pattern and it works for sales, for orders, and for anything you pointed at. Add a tenth measure to your model next month, and the rules you already built apply to it with no extra work at all. Inside the group, one item usually stays plain. It simply returns the measure exactly as it is. So a report that ignores the group still shows the normal number. That plain item becomes your safe default. The group works like a menu of ways to look at any measure, and the report chooses from that menu. The one function that does the heavy lifting. All of this rests on a single function called selected measure. The name is long, but the idea is short. It stands in for whatever measure is currently in your visual. So a rule can be written once and still fit every measure you throw at it. When you write a calculation item, you take the selected measure and wrap something around it. If you want last year, you compare the selected measure to the same period a year earlier. If you want a running total, you add the selected measure up from the start of the year. The measure itself never changes and only the wrapper does. A few related functions help at the edges. Once you see that the rest of the feature is mostly bookkeeping, you decide what the wrapper does, and power BI handles swapping in the right measure for each cell of your report. They don't work on implicit measures, which are the quick auto aggregations power BI creates. If your model leans on implicit measures today, you'll need to replace them with explicit ones before a calculation group behaves the way you expect. Where it gets tricky with precedence and common mistakes, everything so far sounds clean, and the sharp edge is the one most beginners never hear about until it costs them. It's called precedence. When a model has more than one calculation, group precedence decides which one wraps the other, and a higher precedence group gets applied more broadly across the calculation. You set it as a number on each group, and the higher number is applied later, wrapping around whatever the other group produced. Microsoft's documented example makes this concrete. Say you have a time intelligence group and a second group that turns a total into a daily average. If time intelligence has the higher precedence, the year to date treatment lands on both the top and the bottom of that fraction. The daily average becomes a year to date daily average. That's the correct behavior. And it's also why the order matters so much. The trap is this get precedence wrong and you get wrong numbers with no error message at all. Nothing turns red, nothing warns you, and the report simply shows a figure that looks reasonable and isn't the one you meant when you add a second group. Test the interaction on purpose instead of trusting it. That's why teams write their precedence choices down, because a short note in the model beats a guess six months from now. The most common real mistake is stacking a calculation group on top of measures that already contain time intelligence of their own.
