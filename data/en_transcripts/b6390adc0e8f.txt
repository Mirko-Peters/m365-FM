There are four seats and power BI names them viewer, contributor, member and admin. Picture them as a single ladder with the least power on the bottom rung and the most at the top. They stack in a clear order, and the two seats in the middle are where most of the confusion lives. A viewer can look at everything in the workspace and interact with it, and that's the entire job. They open reports, click through the pages, and they can subscribe to a report. So a fresh copy lands in their inbox on a schedule. What if you can't do is change a single thing? A contributor can do everything a viewer can, and they can also build. They create and edit reports, work on the semantic models underneath them, and delete content the team no longer needs. What they can't do is share that work outward or publish the app that wraps the workspace into something a wider audience can open. A member takes the next step up and adds sharing to the mix. Members can share items with the people around them, update the workspace app, and add other people at the same level or lower, and admin can do all of it. And an admin is also the only person who can delete the workspace or change someone else's role. When you see a single name listed as the owner of a workspace, that person is almost always sitting in the admin seat. One detail about these roles is easy to miss and it shapes everything else. A role applies to the whole workspace, not to a single report. There's no switch that lets someone edit this dashboard while only reading that one, because the seat covers everything inside the room. What each role can and cannot do. Now that the ladder makes sense, the useful part is where the rungs sit close together. The gaps between them matter far more than the names. An admin can let contributors update the app, which helps with publishing, and it still doesn't turn them into people who can give out access. There's one more practical perk in this seat. A contributor can connect a data refresh to an on premises gateway, which is how a lot of companies pull numbers from a server that lives inside their own building. Members can share what they build, update the app, and add people to the workspace. The wrinkle is worth spelling out. A member can add a new person, but a member can't remove anyone and a member can't change an existing person's role. Here's the idea that clears up most of the confusion around power BI access. A workspace role and row level security are two different locks, and they guard two different things. The workspace role answers whether a person can get into the room and open what's inside. Row level security answers which rows of data that person can see. Once a report is open, you can hand someone the viewer role and still let them see only their own regions numbers. Because the two controls stack on top of each other, power BI checks three layers in order before anyone reaches data. First, it checks whether the person can sign into the organization's identity at all. Then it checks whether they can get into the power BI or fabric service. Finally, it checks whether they're allowed to do the specific thing they're asking for on that specific table or file, a person has to clear all three. This is why access can still fail after you've granted a role, and why a role by itself never hides data inside a report. Row level security is set up inside the semantic model, usually in power BI desktop. By defining roles and rules on the tables, those rules travel with the model. So the same report shows different rows to different people depending on who opens it. Microsoft's own guidance makes the split concrete to enforce row level security. For the people who browse a workspace, you use the viewer role, the role gets them through the door, and the row rules shape what they see once they're inside. Skip the row rules and lean on the role alone, and every viewer sees every row. The two tools also fail in different ways. Get the role wrong and the wrong person walks into the room.
