The mistakes beginners make knowing the walls is one thing, and knowing the potholes people fall into is another. These are the mistakes that catch beginners in their first few weeks, and every one of them is easy to avoid once you have seen it. The biggest one is building a flow under a personal account and then treating it like a company asset. A flow runs as the person who owns it. If that person changes roles or leaves, the flow can weaken or break entirely. The practical habit is to decide early who should own a flow. Your team relies on a close cousin is the broken connection flows connect to your apps through connections that can expire or fail. When that happens, the flow just stops working until someone notices. If a flow matters, it deserves a periodic check because a silent failure is worse than a loud one. Sharing a flow is another spot where people get tangled. You can share a flow with a teammate, but they may still need their own connection to the apps it touches. That's why a shared flow sometimes works for one person and fails for another. Agree on who owns the connections before you roll a flow out to the whole team. Another common trap is testing only the happy path. You try the flow with perfect data. It works and you call it done. Then real life sends an empty field, a missing file, or a name spelled differently. And the flow stumbles. Good builders test the ugly cases on purpose before trusting a flow with real work. There's also the trap of building too much at once. It's tempting to design a flow with a dozen branches and every edge case in mind. Those are the flows that break in confusing ways and are painful to fix. A tiny flow that sends one message reliably beats a grand one nobody trusts. Two small habits save a lot of pain later. Give your flows clear names because a list of twelve flows called flow one is useless six months down the road. Write down in plain words what each flow is meant to do, so the next person can understand it without opening every step. The last mistake is more about mindset. People rush to automate a process they've never actually run by hand, so they automate the wrong steps and lock in a mess. Do the task manually once or twice and write down the real steps. Then automate what you wrote and grow it only when the small version proves itself. Dodge those mistakes and you're in good shape.
