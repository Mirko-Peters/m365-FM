Lean and Six Sigma are two improvement approaches with different targets. Lean removes waste and improves the flow of work. Six Sigma reduces variation and defects in a process. This article is for operational excellence leads who must decide when to use which, and when to combine them. It explains both, the DMAIC cycle, a decision guide, a worked example with numbers and the mistakes that make programmes compete instead of cooperate.

## Lean vs six sigma: what is the core difference?

[ASQ](https://asq.org/quality-resources/six-sigma) summarizes the contrast in one line: lean focuses on waste reduction, while Six Sigma emphasizes variation reduction. The two answer different questions.

- **Lean asks:** where does the work wait, move or repeat without adding value? The aim is a faster, simpler flow.
- **Six Sigma asks:** why does the result differ from the target, and how do we make the process stable and capable? The aim is fewer defects.

The tools differ too. According to ASQ, lean uses less technical tools such as kaizen and visual controls, while Six Sigma uses statistical data analysis, design of experiments and hypothesis tests. Neither is better. They fit different problems.

Both are process improvement methodologies, and both appear in lean manufacturing programmes next to each other. A Six Sigma certification, such as a Green Belt or Black Belt, shows training in the method.

A short comparison in words: lean works on the **time** a product spends in the system. Six Sigma works on the **quality** of what the process produces. A plant with long queues and few defects needs lean first. A plant with fast flow and many defects needs Six Sigma first. Most plants have some of each.

## What does lean focus on?

The Lean Enterprise Institute describes [lean thinking and practice](https://www.lean.org/lexicon-terms/lean-thinking-and-practice/) as a five-step thought process: define value from the customer's view, map the value stream, create flow, let the customer pull value and pursue perfection. The common thread is waste: any step that the customer would not pay for.

In practice, lean teams work close to the gemba, the real place of work. They watch the process, map the flow, remove waiting, reduce batch sizes and make problems visible. They rely on the people who do the work. The method of choice is [kaizen](https://www.m365.fm/entity/kaizen/), the Japanese word for improvement through small, repeated steps. The tools are simple on purpose: boards, markings, standard work and short cycles.

## What does Six Sigma focus on?

ASQ calls Six Sigma a method that provides organizations tools to improve the capability of their business processes. It focuses on reducing process variation and enhancing process control. The stated metric for Six Sigma quality performance is 3.4 defects per million opportunities, accounting for a 1.5-sigma shift in the mean.

The ASQ page also describes the people. Key practitioners are trained in advanced statistics and project management and are called Black Belts. Black Belt training is described as four weeks of instruction over four months, with projects worked on between sessions. Other belt levels, such as the Green Belt, also exist. A certification shows training in the method, but it is the project results that count.

The central idea is that variation causes defects. If you measure a process, find the sources of scatter and control them, the outcome becomes predictable. This is a data-driven approach: you need reliable measurements, not opinions.

## How does the DMAIC cycle work?

[ASQ describes DMAIC](https://asq.org/quality-resources/dmaic) as a structured approach for improving existing processes that fall short of performance standards or customer expectations. It has five phases.

1. **Define.** Set the problem and the goals, identify the customers and their requirements and agree on a project charter.
2. **Measure.** Document the actual process, validate the measurement systems and establish baseline performance with reliable data.
3. **Analyze.** Identify the critical inputs, the drivers of variation and defects, to find the root cause.
4. **Improve.** Evaluate and test solutions, and optimize the process. Design of experiments and kaizen events are common tools.
5. **Control.** Set up mistake-proofing, long-term measurement and reaction plans. Write standard procedures and confirm that the process stays capable.

ASQ says the method is meant for incremental improvement of an existing process. For new products or a full redesign, it points to a different method called DMADV. The fifth phase matters most. Many projects find a solution and then lose the gain, because nobody holds it in place.

## When should you combine lean and Six Sigma?

ASQ describes Lean Six Sigma as a fact-based, data-driven philosophy of improvement that values defect prevention over defect detection. It adds that implementations often start with lean to reduce waste, then apply more technical Six Sigma tools if process problems remain.

That order makes sense. Waste hides problems. Large stocks, long queues and rework loops mask the real variation in the process. Once lean has removed the clutter, the remaining problems are easier to see and measure. Then Six Sigma tools can find their root cause.

Use these questions as a guide. They are a planning aid, not a rule from a standard.

- **Is the main problem waiting, stock or a slow flow?** Start with lean.
- **Is the flow fast but the results scatter around the target?** Use Six Sigma tools.
- **Do both appear?** Remove the obvious waste first, then measure.
- **Is the cause unknown after a first look?** Measure first, with DMAIC, before you change anything.
- **Is the team new to improvement?** Start with the simple tools. Add statistics when they are needed.

For the long run, tie both to one system of [continuous improvement](https://www.m365.fm/entity/continuous-improvement/): one list of problems, one rhythm of review, and one way of recording results.

## Example: one cell, two problems

This is an illustration with made-up numbers.

A cell makes 5,000 units per week. 4% of them need rework, and the lead time through the cell is 6 days, of which 5.5 days are waiting.

- Defects per week: 5,000 × 4% = 200.

**Lean step.** A map shows that units wait 1.5 days in front of the inspection station. The team smooths the flow and the wait falls from 1.5 to 0.5 days.

- New lead time: 6 − 1 = 5 days.

**Six Sigma step.** The remaining defects scatter. The team uses DMAIC. It measures the process, finds that one machine setting drifts between shifts and controls it with a standard check. The defect rate falls from 4% to 1.5%.

- Defects per week: 5,000 × 1.5% = 75.
- Defects avoided: 200 − 75 = 125 per week.
- If each rework costs 30 currency units, that is 125 × 30 = 3,750 units per week.

For comparison, assume 5 defect opportunities per unit. Defects per million opportunities fall from 200 ÷ (5,000 × 5) × 1,000,000 = 8,000 to 75 ÷ 25,000 × 1,000,000 = 3,000. The Six Sigma level of 3.4 is far away, which is normal. The aim is not the label but the result. These numbers are assumptions. Measure your own process.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating them as rivals.** The programmes serve the same goal. Competition between a lean team and a Six Sigma team wastes effort.
- **Using statistics on a messy process.** If waste and chaos dominate, a statistical project will drown in noise. Clean up first.
- **Ignoring the people.** Both approaches need operators to take part. Belts and certificates do not replace the knowledge of the team.
- **Measuring without acting.** A thorough measurement phase that never leads to a change helps nobody.
- **Forgetting control.** Without standards and monitoring, the gain fades. Hold it in place.
- **Chasing labels.** A belt level or a sigma value is not a result. Track the effect on delivery, quality and cost.
