Operational excellence vs lean is not a contest. Lean is a way of thinking and a set of practices for creating value with less waste. Operational excellence is the wider goal of consistently better business performance, and lean is usually its main toolkit. Lean answers "how do we remove waste from this flow?" Operational excellence answers "how do we run the whole plant so that results stay good?" This article is for operational excellence managers who need a clear definition for colleagues, a plan, or a budget discussion.

## What is lean?

The Lean Enterprise Institute (LEI) describes lean as "a way of thinking about creating needed value with fewer resources and less waste". It is also a practice of continuous experimentation. Lean starts with the customer: what do they value, and what problem do they need solved? It keeps going while value is imperfect and waste remains. You can read the full wording on the [LEI page on what lean is](https://www.lean.org/explore-lean/what-is-lean/).

LEI also stresses a moral side. Lean rests on respect for the people involved: customers, employees, suppliers and communities. That matters for the comparison below, because it shows that lean is not only a cost tool.

Lean thinking is simple to state, but it takes steady practice. In daily practice, [lean management](https://www.m365.fm/entity/lean-management/) uses tools such as value stream mapping, standard work, pull systems and visual management. Each tool targets waste, which lean calls muda, the Japanese word for activity that uses resources but adds no value for the customer. Lean manufacturing applies these tools on the shop floor.

## What is operational excellence?

There is no single OpEx definition, and sources differ. The [Juran Institute](https://www.juran.com/?p=4644) defines operational excellence as "the goal of improved business performance". It says OpEx usually means using lean techniques to eliminate waste and lower production time and costs, and that it looks beyond cost reduction and process improvement toward long-term, sustainable growth.

The Shingo Institute offers a more detailed picture. Its model groups guiding principles into cultural enablers, continuous improvement, enterprise alignment and results, and it states that "the foundation of an enterprise is culture". You can see the structure on the [Shingo Model page](https://shingo.org/model). Programs also borrow from quality management approaches such as total quality management (TQM). In that view, [operational excellence](https://www.m365.fm/entity/operational-excellence/) is a management system with a purpose, methods and expected behavior, not a single program.

A working definition for your own plant: operational excellence is the consistent delivery of safety, quality, delivery and cost results through a management system in which people at every level improve the work.

## Operational excellence vs lean: the key differences

The two overlap a great deal, so look at where they differ.

- **Scope.** Lean focuses on how value flows and where waste hides. Operational excellence covers the whole operation, including strategy, planning, the supply chain, leadership routines and results.
- **Nature.** Lean is both a mindset and a toolkit. Operational excellence is a goal and a management framework that organizes the tools.
- **Methods.** Lean uses flow, pull and waste tools. Operational excellence can combine lean with Six Sigma, project management and other methodology, depending on the problem.
- **Time horizon.** A lean project can finish in weeks. An operational excellence system is meant to run for years.
- **Culture and leadership.** Lean values respect for people. Operational excellence adds an explicit demand on leaders: align goals, build daily management and model the behavior you ask for.
- **Measures.** Lean often tracks lead time, inventory and flow. Operational excellence tracks those too, plus the wider organizational results such as safety, quality and customer satisfaction.

One more clarification: operational excellence vs lean is sometimes confused with lean vs Six Sigma. Six Sigma is a data-driven method for reducing variation and defects. Juran notes that the two began as separate approaches and were later combined. Lean Six Sigma is the name of the combined toolkit.

## How do they work together with continuous improvement?

Both depend on [continuous improvement](https://www.m365.fm/entity/continuous-improvement/), the ongoing effort to improve in small steps. Juran treats continuous improvement and operational excellence as closely linked but distinct: continuous improvement keeps producing improvements, while operational excellence adds long-term direction and a culture in which employees improve the flow of value.

Think of it as three layers:

1. **Lean tools** find and remove waste in a flow, such as a long queue between two machines.
2. **Continuous improvement routines** (kaizen events, daily problem solving, suggestion systems) keep the work going after the first project.
3. **The operational excellence system** sets goals, reviews results, and makes sure leaders and measures support the routines.

Without layer 3, projects pile up and fade. Without layers 1 and 2, the system has nothing to run. A plant that wants lasting results needs all three.

## How do you explain the difference to your leadership team?

Executives often use the two terms as if they meant the same thing. A short, structured approach helps you make the case:

1. **Start with the customer.** State what customers value and need: delivery, quality and price. Late orders damage the customer experience, so start there. Both lean and the wider system serve customer needs, and this keeps the talk away from jargon.
2. **Name one problem.** Show a concrete flow problem, such as a long queue or late orders, with workflow data from your own plant.
3. **Show the tools.** Explain which lean tools address that problem and how long a first improvement will take.
4. **Show the system.** Explain which routines will keep the flow and the quality stable, such as daily boards, problem-solving rules and leader visits to the floor.
5. **Ask for a decision.** Ask for time and people for the first improvement, and agree how you will report the result.

This order works because it moves from the problem to the solution to the system, and it avoids a long debate about definitions.

## Example: choosing the right approach

This example uses assumed numbers to show how a manager might decide.

A plant makes a family of machined housings. A product takes 12 working days from order release to shipment. A study of the routing shows 4 hours of actual machining and handling. On 8-hour days, 12 days equal 96 hours, so touch time is 4 ÷ 96 = about 4.2% of lead time. The other 95.8% is waiting.

- **Diagnosis:** Waiting dominates. This is a flow problem, so lean tools fit: map the value stream, reduce waste such as waiting, limit work in progress, and level the load. Such waiting is a clear sign of inefficiency in the flow, and the customer feels it as late delivery.
- **Scrap rate:** Suppose scrap is stable at 2% across the period. That is not the main issue now, so a Six Sigma project is not the first move.
- **System question:** If three earlier lean projects here gave gains that faded within a year, the cause is probably missing daily management and leader routines. That is an operational excellence question, not a tool question.

The answer in this case is both: lean tools for the queue, and an OpEx system so the new flow holds.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating them as rivals.** Companies lose time debating the label when they could be solving a flow problem.
- **Calling a set of lean projects "operational excellence".** Without goals, daily management and leader routines, results fade.
- **Starting with a framework and no problem.** A large program without a visible win loses attention fast.
- **Copying a maturity model or scorecard without adapting it.** Use it as a guide for discussion, not as a checklist.
- **Ignoring respect for people.** Both lean and OpEx stall when staff feel improvement is done to them.
- **Measuring only cost.** Cost cuts that damage quality or safety are not excellence.
