5S is a workplace organization method with five steps: sort, set in order, shine, standardize and sustain. This article is for production and team leaders who want a 5S routine that still works a year from now, not a clean-up day that fades within a month. It explains the five steps, a rollout plan, how to audit the result and a worked example with numbers. It also lists the mistakes that make most programmes fail.

## What is 5S, and why does it matter in a plant?

The Lean Enterprise Institute defines [5S](https://www.lean.org/lexicon-terms/five-s/) as a set of five related Japanese terms, each beginning with an S sound, that describe workplace practices supporting visual control and lean production. In plain words: everything you need is easy to find, everything you do not need is gone, and the area is clean enough that problems show.

That sounds trivial, but a typical workspace hides a lot of waste. Operators walk to look for a gauge. A fixture sits behind three pallets. A leaking hose is not noticed because the floor is always dirty.

ASQ lists the benefits that a [5S programme](https://asq.org/quality-resources/lean/five-s-tutorial) can bring: improved safety, higher equipment availability, lower defect rates, reduced costs, increased production agility and flexibility, improved employee morale and better asset utilization. The page gives no measured figures. Treat these as hypotheses and measure your own result, as the example below shows.

## What do the five S's mean?

Each S has a Japanese name. Sources translate them slightly differently, so you will see "set in order" in some places and "straighten" in others. The meaning is the same.

1. **Seiri, sort.** Separate needed items from unneeded ones, such as tools, parts, materials and paperwork, and remove the unneeded. Be strict: if you cannot name the job an item serves this month, it does not belong at the workstation.
2. **Seiton, set in order.** Arrange what remains so that, in the words of the Lean Enterprise Institute, there is "a place for everything and everything in its place". Frequently used items go closest to the point of use. Mark each place with a label, an outline or a shadow board.
3. **Seiso, shine.** Clean and wash. On the shop floor, cleaning is also inspection: when you wipe a machine, you find leaks, loose bolts and worn parts early.
4. **Seiketsu, standardize.** Maintain the cleanliness that results from regularly performing the first three S's. This means written standards, photos of the target state and a clear owner for each area.
5. **Shitsuke, sustain.** Build the discipline to perform the first four S's every day, so that the state does not slide back.

Two notes from the same source help to avoid confusion. Some practitioners add a sixth S for safety. And Toyota traditionally uses only four S's and drops shitsuke, because its daily, weekly and monthly audits of standardized work make it redundant. The lesson for you: sustaining needs something concrete, such as a routine of audits. Good intentions are not enough.

## How do you roll out 5S step by step?

Do not start in the whole plant. A pilot area teaches you what works in your culture, and a visible success is the best argument for the next area.

1. **Pick one pilot area and an owner.** Choose a cell with a willing team leader and a visible problem, such as long searches for tools. Name one person responsible.
2. **Get the team involved.** The people who work in the area know where the clutter is. Explain the aim in one sentence: less searching, fewer hazards, easier work.
3. **Take a baseline.** Photograph the area from fixed positions. Measure one number, for example the minutes per shift that operators spend searching for tools or material.
4. **Sort.** Walk through the area with the team. Tag everything you are unsure about with a red tag and move it to a holding area. After a set period, return, reassign or discard what nobody claimed.
5. **Set in order.** Decide where each remaining item lives. Place frequently used items at hand, label shelves and drawers, and mark floor positions for carts and pallets. Make it obvious to a newcomer where each item goes and where it is missing.
6. **Shine.** Clean the area together, including machines. Treat every leak, crack and loose part as a finding and log it for maintenance. Then agree on the cleaning routine: who cleans what, when and with which tools.
7. **Standardize.** Write the rules on one page per area: the target state as a photo, the cleaning plan, the owner and the audit rhythm. Use the same labels and colours across areas, so that a person moving between cells understands each one.
8. **Sustain.** Build the check into the day. A five-minute routine at the end of each shift, a weekly audit and a visible score on the team board are enough to start.

## How do you audit and sustain 5S?

Most 5S programmes fail after the clean-up, not during it. ASQ quotes a Quality Progress warning that many companies implement only the first three steps and that, unless the standardize and sustain steps are formalized, the initiative is doomed to failure. The fix is a simple 5S audit that is done often and visibly.

The State of Iowa's published [5S audit checklist](https://dom.iowa.gov/resource/lean-resources/5s-audit-checklist) is organized in the same five categories: remove all unnecessary materials and equipment, make it obvious where items belong, clean everything, establish policies and procedures to keep the first three in place including monitoring, and provide training and daily activities. That structure is a good template for your own checklist.

Here is a design that works in practice. It is a suggestion, not a standard:

- **Questions.** Write three to five yes-or-no questions for each S. For example, for sort: "Is there anything at this station that is not used for the current job?" For set in order: "Does every tool have a marked place, and is it in that place?"
- **Scale.** Score each question 0 for no and 1 for yes. Add the points for each S and show the five scores on a simple chart.
- **Rhythm.** The area team checks daily in five minutes. The team leader audits weekly. Once a month, a person from another area audits. A fresh pair of eyes sees clutter that the local team no longer notices.
- **Response.** A low score is not a reprimand. It starts a short action with an owner and a date.
- **Display.** Put the scores and the photos of the target state on the team board, next to the other daily measures.

## How does 5S connect to lean and continuous improvement?

5S is often the first lean tool a plant uses, and it prepares the ground for the others. In a tidy, marked workplace, abnormal conditions are visible: a missing tool, a pallet in the wrong place, a puddle under a machine. Visible problems can be solved. Hidden ones cannot.

The method also teaches the habit behind [kaizen](https://www.m365.fm/entity/kaizen/). Kaizen is the Japanese word for improvement through small, repeated steps by the people who do the work. A team that changes the place of one tool because it saves ten seconds is practising it. The standards you write in the fourth S give those improvements a baseline: every change starts from the current standard and ends with a new one.

This is also why 5S belongs in the plan for [continuous improvement](https://www.m365.fm/entity/continuous-improvement/) and not in a one-off project. A project ends. A routine continues. If the audit results appear on the same board as quality and delivery, the plant manager sees 5S as part of normal management and not as a campaign.

## Example: search time in a machining cell

This is an illustration with made-up numbers.

A machining cell has six operators on each of two shifts. A short time study shows that each operator spends about 15 minutes per shift looking for tools, gauges and fixtures. The team runs a 5S pilot. They remove tools that nobody uses, give each remaining tool a marked place on a shadow board and add a daily five-minute check.

After four weeks, the same study shows about 5 minutes of searching per shift.

- Saving per operator and shift: 15 − 5 = 10 minutes.
- Saving per shift in the cell: 6 operators × 10 minutes = 60 minutes.
- Saving per day with two shifts: 120 minutes, or 2 hours.
- Saving per week of five days: 10 hours.

The daily check costs 6 operators × 5 minutes = 30 minutes per shift, or 60 minutes per day. So the net gain is 60 minutes per day in this illustration, or 5 hours per week. That is still a positive balance, and the cell also finds two oil leaks while cleaning. These numbers are assumptions. Measure your own baseline before you start, and your own result afterwards.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating 5S as a clean-up day.** A single big event produces a clean area for a few weeks. A routine produces a state that lasts.
- **Stopping after three steps.** Sorting, setting in order and cleaning are visible. Standardize and sustain are not, and they are the steps that keep the result.
- **Doing it to the team instead of with the team.** If leaders tidy and label an area without the people who work there, the labels will not match the real work.
- **Throwing away too fast.** Use a holding area for unclear items, and check with maintenance and quality before you discard anything.
- **No owner and no audit.** An area without a named owner has nobody to hold the standard.
- **Auditing without action.** A score that never leads to a fix teaches the team that the audit does not matter.

## How software can help

Software is not the core of 5S. A tidy bench does not need an app. But software can make the routine easier to hold. A digital audit form on a tablet saves paper and lets the team leader attach a photo to each finding. A shared action list shows which findings are still open, and a trend of audit scores by area shows where the routine is weakening. Use such tools only when the paper version already works. A digital checklist for a habit that does not exist yet will not create the habit.
