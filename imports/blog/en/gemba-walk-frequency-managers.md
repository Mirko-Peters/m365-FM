There is no official rule for gemba walk frequency. Lean sources describe walks as a regular habit, often daily for the management team, but no standard fixes the minutes. This article is for operational excellence managers who need to set a cadence for several levels of leaders and justify it to management. It shows what the Lean Enterprise Institute says, a practical cadence by role, how to build it into leader standard work and how to check that your rhythm works. *Gemba* is Japanese for "actual place", the place where work is done.

## Gemba walk frequency: what the sources say, and what they do not

Start with what is documented. In the Lean Enterprise Institute article [Standard work for lean leaders](https://www.lean.org/the-lean-post/articles/standard-work-for-lean-leaders-one-of-the-keys-to-sustaining-performance-gains/), Joe Murli describes gemba walks as one of five elements of leader standard work. They are conducted daily by the management team, he writes, following a regular route at a standard time, possibly with a checklist. The article gives no specific walk length and no share of the manager's day.

The Lean Enterprise Institute's lexicon entry on [leader standard work](https://www.lean.org/lexicon-terms/leader-standard-work/) lists gemba walks among five day-to-day tools, together with reflection meetings, responding to andons, creating accountability and mentoring people. It does not say how often walks should happen.

So the honest answer has two parts. Lean literature treats the walk as a routine, and the daily rhythm belongs to the management team as a whole. Beyond that, the exact frequency for each role is a decision for your plant. Be careful with articles that state "walk twice a week" as a rule. They give an opinion, not a standard.

## Daily or weekly: setting a cadence by role

A daily gemba walk and a weekly gemba walk answer different needs. The first keeps a team's problems small and visible. The second lets a leader see change over time. A practical split, based on common lean practice and not on a standard, looks like this:

- **Supervisors and team leaders: daily, 10 to 15 minutes.** They own the area and see problems first. Their walk checks that standard work is followed and that yesterday's problems are handled.
- **Plant and department managers: weekly, 30 to 45 minutes.** They follow one flow from start to finish, ask about causes and review open actions.
- **Operational excellence managers: weekly or every two weeks, in rotating areas.** Their walks look for patterns across areas and check that the routine of other levels holds.
- **Senior leaders: monthly, with a clear purpose.** Their presence shows interest and helps to clear obstacles that area managers cannot remove.

A short [gemba walk](https://www.m365.fm/entity/gemba-walk/) can still bring valuable insights if it has a clear question, and regular walks help leaders to foster open communication and to identify areas for improvement. Treat the minutes as starting points. A walk that you cancel every second week is worse than a shorter one that always happens. The aim is a rhythm that people trust.

## Put the walk into leader standard work

A rhythm survives only if it is written down. The Lean Enterprise Institute describes leader standard work as a way to shift managers from being the primary problem solvers to building the problem-solving ability of their people. The lexicon also calls it "kaizen for management". An *andon* is a signal that a problem has appeared, and leader standard work includes responding to it quickly, because problems are chances to learn.

To put walks into leader standard work:

1. **Fix a time and a route.** "Tuesday 10:00, final assembly to shipping." Murli's description of a regular route at a standard time is the model.
2. **Block the time in the calendar.** Treat it like a customer meeting.
3. **Name the focus.** One product family, one problem or one question per walk.
4. **Add the follow-up.** After each walk, 10 minutes for actions. At the start of the next one, review them.
5. **Review the routine monthly.** Look at what was done, what was cancelled and why.

Leader standard work also protects against the common failure that managers fill their day with meetings and fire-fighting. It makes the walk the default, not a gap-filler.

## What frequency changes on the shop floor

Frequency decides what a walk can do. A rare walk gives a snapshot. A regular walk lets leaders observe processes firsthand and build a deeper understanding of how work changes between shifts, products and weeks. They see an inefficiency before it becomes habit, and they notice when a bottleneck moves from one station to another.

Frequent, short contact also changes the relationship. When leaders engage with employees on the shop floor again and again, frontline workers see that the leader comes back. That supports employee engagement and morale, because people learn that a conversation about a problem leads somewhere. Over months, repeated walks help to identify opportunities and find root causes. This is how a culture of continuous improvement grows in lean management: through repeated contact between the people who lead and the people who do the work, followed by action. Process improvement and better productivity depend on that action. A walk alone does not produce them.

## How to know if your frequency is right

Two simple measures tell you whether the cadence works:

- **Walk adherence:** completed walks divided by planned walks. If adherence is below about three quarters for several weeks, the plan is too ambitious or other work crowds it out. The threshold is a rule of thumb, not a standard.
- **Action closure:** actions closed divided by actions raised from walks. If walks produce no actions, they may be too long, too general or too rare.

Add a qualitative check. Ask team leaders every quarter: "Do walks help you?" Their answer matters more than any ratio. If walks feel like inspections, frequency is not your problem. Fix the behaviour first.

## Example: the time cost of three cadences

This is an illustrative example with made-up numbers. An operational excellence manager compares three cadences for one plant manager with a 40-hour week, which is 2,400 minutes.

- **Daily, 20 minutes:** 5 × 20 = 100 minutes per week. Share of the week: 100 ÷ 2,400 = **4.2 percent**.
- **Weekly, 45 minutes:** 45 minutes per week. Share: 45 ÷ 2,400 = **1.9 percent**.
- **Every two weeks, 90 minutes:** 45 minutes per week on average. Share: **1.9 percent** as well.

The weekly and the two-weekly cadence cost the same time. The difference is how fast problems are noticed and how often the team sees the manager. She proposes weekly walks for the plant manager and daily walks for supervisors. She also tracks adherence for eight weeks: 6 of 8 planned walks were done in the first month, which is 75 percent. The two missed walks were cancelled because of a customer audit, so she moves the walk to Thursday morning, which is quieter.

The numbers do not prove the right cadence. They show the cost of each option, so that management can agree on a plan with open eyes.

## Common mistakes

- **Copying a frequency from a book or blog.** No source gives a rule. Test and adjust.
- **Starting with daily walks for all managers.** The routine collapses in week three. Start weekly and add only when it works.
- **Counting hours instead of results.** Ten walks that produce no actions are not a success.
- **Cancelling when things are busy.** Busy weeks are when problems appear. Shorten the walk, but go.
- **Leaving out the team.** If people do not know the rhythm, each walk is a surprise.
- **Having no review.** Check the cadence every month and change it when the data say so.
