The most common value stream mapping mistakes are mapping too much, mapping without action, relying on memory instead of observation and leaving out the people who know the flow. This guide is for continuous improvement managers who want maps that lead to change. It draws on practitioners who have seen maps go wrong, explains how to avoid each mistake, and ends with a worked example of how one guessed number changes the whole picture.

## What are the most common value stream mapping mistakes?

[Value stream mapping](https://www.m365.fm/entity/value-stream-mapping/) draws the material and information flow of a product family, from customer to supplier, so that you can see waste and plan a better flow. It is a core tool of lean manufacturing. The method is simple on paper, and that is why teams skip steps, and most VSM mistakes follow from that. Several practitioners have listed what goes wrong:

- Severino Abad of the Instituto Lean Management lists eight mistakes in his Planet Lean article [Avoiding the most common mistakes with VSM](https://www.planet-lean.com/articles/lean-value-stream-mapping). They include blurring reality and assumptions, using weak data, leaving out key people, getting lost in detail and separating mapping from action.
- Jeff Pierce lists nine failures in [Value Stream Mapping Gone Wrong](https://cmcrossroads.com/article/value-stream-mapping-gone-wrong), among them an inappropriate team, no metrics, analysis paralysis and maps with no action.
- Tom Voss groups his lessons under scope, people, data and follow-up in [Value Stream Mapping: Lessons from the Front](https://www.industryweek.com/lean-six-sigma/value-stream-mapping-lessons-front).

The lists overlap a lot. The next four sections cover the mistakes that appear on all of them: scope, action, data and people.

## How do you avoid mapping too large?

VSM pitfalls often start with scope. Abad says that trying to map everything at once causes analysis paralysis, and that teams should focus on the process they chose to change. Pierce makes the same point: when a team maps everything, it gets lost in detail.

How much detail is right? The sources give different numbers because they look at different levels:

- Christoph Roser of AllAboutLean, in [Practical Tips for Value Stream Mapping](https://www.allaboutlean.com/vsm-pitfalls/), sees a sweet spot of 4 to 15 process boxes, says more than 30 is a waste of time and limits the map to one A3 sheet. If it does not fit, it has too much.
- Voss says your improvement goals should set the detail. A plant-level map focuses on overall lead time. A work-cell map needs task times in seconds and discrete inventory counts. His rule of thumb is 20 to 30 steps for the primary flow, and he notes that adding detail later is easier than consolidating steps.

Both authors warn against too much detail. Voss adds that too much detail hides the problems, that too little hides the details that matter, and that the map should make bottlenecks visible.

Mapping all value streams of a plant at once is the same mistake at a larger scale. A practical rule: pick one product family and one question, such as "where does the lead time go?" Draw each process step that answers the question, with its cycle time, changeover time and stock. If the map grows beyond one sheet, you are probably mapping several value streams, and each deserves its own map.

## How do you avoid mapping without action?

A map that ends in a drawer is the costliest of the value stream mapping mistakes. Voss writes that a value stream map is not a standalone deliverable. It is the starting point for a plan, and the plan must be executed. He limits each value stream plan to about six months, after which the future state becomes the new current state and the cycle repeats. Pierce is just as direct: maps alone do not improve the value stream, so design the future state and implement the changes.

Abad adds a point about momentum. A good map is alive and reflects process changes over time. Companies that use one early map to inform people let it become obsolete. Using the map only to explain how work is done does not reward the effort the team spent gathering information at the gemba, the place where the work happens.

These steps keep the map tied to action:

1. **Write a one-page plan** with each improvement, an owner and a date, before the mapping workshop ends.
2. **Assign one person to each improvement area.** Voss recommends a project manager per area.
3. **Run [kaizen](https://www.m365.fm/entity/kaizen/) events after the map.** Kaizen means improvement in small steps. Pierce advises mapping first to set the plan and targets, and then running events to implement specific improvements.
4. **Publicize early wins.** Voss notes that employees who help turn the map into real improvements are more willing to join the next mapping workshop.
5. **Redraw the map** after each round of change.

## How do you avoid weak data and the wrong team?

Both mistakes make the map describe a plant that does not exist.

**Data.** Abad finds it hard to separate how things happen from how people say they happen and how they should happen. The gap widens when teams use guesses instead of observation. When real information is missing, teams use averages or old numbers, which makes takt time and OEE unreliable. Roser agrees. He says you should draw the current state on the shop floor, because a map drawn elsewhere shows how things should be, and that you should verify information yourself instead of only asking employees.

Voss makes a helpful point for teams with gaps: poor or incomplete data is itself a flow impediment, so treat data gaps as findings. He also suggests validating current state data with time observations, inventory counts and current quality metrics. Pierce puts it as a need for accurate, current cycle times. Estimated or outdated measures lead to poor analysis.

**People.** Pierce recommends a cross-functional team that understands the work and has the authority to make changes, including leaders. Otherwise the team may map processes that nobody in the room can change. Abad adds that informal experts who shape the flow should help define the current state. Without them, the map misses how the process really works. Voss suggests keeping the same team updating the map over time, so the process stays relevant.

## Example: how one guessed number changes the picture

This is an illustrative example with made-up numbers, not a measured case. A team maps five steps of one product family. Demand is 200 units per day, and a working day has 25,200 seconds. The processing time is 200 seconds per unit, and 120 seconds of it is value-added.

**From memory.** The team says each step has about one day of stock. That gives 5 days of waiting, or 5 × 25,200 = 126,000 seconds. Lead time is 126,000 + 200 = **126,200 seconds**. The value-added ratio is 120 ÷ 126,200 = about **0.095 percent**.

**From a count.** On the floor, someone counts the stock in front of each step: 400, 300, 300, 200 and 200 units. That is 1,400 units. At 200 units per day, the waiting time is 7 days, or 7 × 25,200 = 176,400 seconds. Lead time is 176,400 + 200 = **176,600 seconds**. The value-added ratio is 120 ÷ 176,600 = about **0.068 percent**.

**What the difference means.** The real lead time is 50,400 seconds, or 2 working days, longer than the team believed. That is about 40 percent more than the remembered figure. Suppose the team sets a future state target of half the remembered stock, 2.5 days. Against the true 7 days, that target needs a cut of 4.5 days, or about 64 percent, not 50 percent. A team that plans for 50 percent will miss its target without knowing why.

The count took less than an hour. The guess cost the team a wrong target.

## Common mistakes

Beyond scope, action, data and people, these mistakes come up in the sources:

- **Assuming what the customer values.** Pierce advises talking to customers or their representatives. Without this, you cannot judge which steps add value.
- **Using maps for tactical fixes.** Pierce suggests mapping the whole value stream for strategic alignment, and using process-level mapping for deeper dives into specific areas.
- **Breaking the information flow rules.** Roser says each process should have one information flow, and that an information flow should not end at inventory, because inventory cannot act on information.
- **Forcing factory symbols on office work.** Abad notes that offices and projects have less linear flows. He suggests swimlane diagrams to show which department owns each activity.
- **Making one large redesign.** Pierce favors small, incremental changes: make a set, learn the impact, then make the next set.
- **Limiting participation.** Abad warns that restricting mapping to certain functions gives a partial view of reality and can create conflict.
