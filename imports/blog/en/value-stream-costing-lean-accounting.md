Value stream costing tracks revenue and cost for a whole product family, from order to delivery, and reports a profit for that stream. It replaces the habit of working out a standard cost for each product and judging departments by variances. This article is for plant managers who work with finance on lean accounting. It explains why standard costs conflict with lean flow, how a value stream P&L works, a worked example, and how to change over without losing the trust of finance. It is general information, not accounting advice, so involve your finance team and auditors.

## What is value stream costing?

Art Byrne, retired CEO of The Wiremold Company, describes the idea in an [LEI article on lean accounting](https://www.lean.org/the-lean-post/articles/ask-art-what-sets-lean-accounting-apart-from-traditional-accounting). When a plant is organised into cells, it gets clear visibility of the labour and material for each product made in them. Overhead is tracked at the product family or value stream level, down to gross margin. Byrne says that allocating overhead to individual products is pointless.

The result is a profit and loss statement per value stream, often called a value stream P&L. It shows what the stream earns and spends in the period, with actual figures, in terms that production people can read.

This is the cost side of [value stream mapping](https://www.m365.fm/entity/value-stream-mapping/) and of value stream management as a routine. The map shows how material and information flow, with cycle time and lead time for each step. The value stream P&L shows what that flow earns. Both belong to lean thinking: define customer value, then look at the whole stream, not at single departments.

## Why do standard costs clash with lean?

Standard costing sets a cost for each product from estimated labour hours and overhead rates. Actual results are compared with the standard, and the differences show as variances. Byrne makes four points in this article and in a [second LEI piece on standard costing and takt time](https://www.lean.org/the-lean-post/articles/ask-art-what-happens-when-standard-cost-accounting-meets-takt-time/):

- Results appear as a long list of variances against budget assumptions that were themselves estimates, so problems are hard to trace to a root cause.
- Detailed product costs rest on complex estimates that few outside finance trust, yet finance still uses them to set prices.
- Absorption costing rewards building inventory. Overhead deferred into stock makes the current month look better, so managers may build products with no demand to hit targets.
- Inventory is where lean expects waste to hide, so a system that rewards more of it works against lean manufacturing.

A [third LEI article](https://www.lean.org/the-lean-post/articles/dealing-with-standard-costing-in-lean-organizations/), by a former CFO who now consults on lean accounting, adds that variances drive mass-production behaviour such as building inventory and buying in bulk. They should not serve as performance measures in a lean company. It also notes that inventory reduction leaves overhead under-absorbed, which can understate profit for a period until inventory stabilises.

That is the conflict. Lean wants less inventory, small batches and flow at takt time, the pace set by customer demand, so that teams can eliminate waste across the supply chain inside the plant. Standard costing rewards the opposite.

Byrne gives an example in the takt time article. A company raised its inventory turns from about 3 to 6 while its non-cash earnings fell, because overhead that had sat in stock was now charged to the period. He adds that in his experience the traditional approach prevails in most companies, more than 80 percent of the time. Treat these as one practitioner's observations, not survey results.

## What does a plant gain from value stream costing?

Lean accounting is not a cost-cutting trick. It is a way to make the numbers match what the shop floor is trying to do. Four effects show up in plants that make the change:

- **Decisions follow the flow.** A shorter lead time and less inventory show up as better results, so teams are not punished for doing the right thing.
- **Productivity is read per stream.** You see output per person or per cell for the whole product family, not per machine.
- **Continuous improvement gets a scoreboard.** Each improvement event can be tied to a change in the stream profit, which helps to reduce costs where the stream actually spends them.
- **The current state becomes visible in money.** On the current state map you already show lead time and inventory. The stream P&L adds revenue and cost to the same page, so the map, the future state plan and the accounts tell one story.

None of this removes the need for good data. A value stream P&L is only as sound as the product family boundaries behind it, so define the family first.

## How does a value stream P&L work?

The method below is a simple structure of my own. Lean accounting authors describe richer versions, and your finance team will adapt it.

1. **Define the stream.** Use the product family. Say which products, cells and people belong to it.
2. **Take the revenue.** Sales of the family's products in the period.
3. **Subtract direct material.** Purchased material and components used by the stream.
4. **Subtract stream conversion costs.** The people, machines and space that work only for the stream.
5. **Subtract other stream costs.** Tooling, maintenance and logistics that can be assigned to the stream without an allocation key.
6. **Read the stream profit.** The result is the stream's profit before shared plant costs.
7. **Handle the shared costs once.** Costs that serve several streams, such as the building or central functions, are kept at plant level or split by a simple, stable rule.

Value stream capacity matters too. In lean accounting this is how much of the stream's capacity, in people and equipment, is used and how much is free. Free capacity is not a cost to hide but a choice to make: sell more, or use it for improvement.

## Example: one stream, one inventory cut

This example is invented, with round figures. It is not a measured case.

Stream A has revenue of 4.8 million in the year. Materials are 2.0 million, conversion costs of the cell are 1.5 million, and other stream costs are 0.4 million. The stream profit is 4.8 minus 2.0 minus 1.5 minus 0.4, which is 0.9 million, or 18.75 percent of revenue.

Now the plant cuts inventory in stream A from 1.2 million to 0.6 million by smaller batches and pull. Under absorption costing, assume that 20 percent of the inventory value is overhead. The 0.6 million reduction releases 0.6 times 0.2, or 0.12 million of overhead into the P&L in that year. Reported profit under standard costing falls by 0.12 million, even though the plant has freed 0.6 million of cash.

The value stream P&L reports the real costs of the year. The finance team shows both numbers side by side and explains the 0.12 million gap in plain words. The plant manager sees what happened to cash, lead time and profit at the same time.

## How do you change over?

Byrne recommends that the plant issues a lean P&L alongside the standard P&L, and switches once people are comfortable. He says this can take about six months. He also notes that finance teams often resist, and that their resistance is a major obstacle.

The former CFO adds a cautionary point: standard costing is still needed for inventory valuation under GAAP or IFRS. Once inventory falls to roughly 30 to 60 days, the valuation risk drops and a simpler method becomes possible. The author calls the full change a medium- to long-term goal.

A practical path:

1. Ask finance to join the value stream review each month.
2. Build the value stream P&L for the pilot stream from existing ERP data.
3. Run it next to the standard reports for at least two quarters.
4. Explain differences, for example the effect of inventory cuts, in plain words.
5. Simplify standard costing step by step as the lean reports gain trust.

## Common mistakes

- **Dropping standard costing without finance.** Reporting rules still apply.
- **Allocating overhead down to each product.** That brings back the problem.
- **Mixing up streams and departments.** The P&L belongs to the product family.
- **Judging a plant manager on absorption.** Inventory reduction will then look like a loss.
- **Hiding the shared costs.** Say how you split them and keep the rule stable.
- **Skipping the explanation.** Without a plain-language bridge, a profit dip after an inventory cut raises alarms.
