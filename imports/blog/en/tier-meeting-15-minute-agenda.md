A tier meeting is a short, standing daily meeting at a board where a team reviews yesterday's result, today's plan and its open problems. Meetings are arranged in tiers, so what one team cannot solve goes up to the next level the same day. This article is for production managers who want a working 15-minute agenda for tier 1 and clear rules for the tiers above it. It covers the structure, the agenda, a worked timetable and the mistakes that kill the routine.

## What is a tier meeting?

A tier meeting is one level of a daily management system. The same idea is also called a daily stand-up meeting in manufacturing, a huddle, or simply the shop floor meeting. Kaizen Institute describes [daily management](https://kaizen.com/insights/daily-management-manufacturing-plant/) as a connected routine of four tiered meetings, each feeding the next, held at a fixed time every day and run on a visual board.

The tiers are:

- **Tier 1: frontline teams.** They review the previous day's results against target, today's plan and new problems.
- **Tier 2: supervisors.** They take the problems that tier 1 could not solve.
- **Tier 3: department managers.** They handle issues that cross teams, and department KPIs.
- **Tier 4: plant leadership.** They act on plant-wide problems and resources.

You will also meet the names tiered management system, tiered daily management and daily management system. They all describe the same stack of short meetings, supported by visual management at every tier. The tier 1 team is the frontline team, and its meeting is the first meeting of the day.

The point of tiers is speed. A problem that a team cannot fix, such as a missing material or a broken fixture shared by two cells, does not wait for a weekly review. It moves up the same day with a named owner, and the answer returns to the team that raised it.

## What does the board show?

Each tier meets at a visual board. A common format is an SQDC board, short for safety, quality, delivery and cost. It shows performance against target so that anyone can see what is on track and what is not. The same layout at every cell makes the boards easy to read for visitors and leaders.

For a tier 1 KPI board, keep a short list:

- Safety: incidents and open hazards
- Quality: defects or first-time quality, plan versus actual
- Delivery: units made against plan, per hour or shift
- Cost: one measure the team can influence, such as downtime minutes
- An action list with problem, owner and due date

Use [shop floor management](https://www.m365.fm/entity/shop-floor-management/) as the umbrella idea: the tier meeting is the daily rhythm inside it. Each tier tracks only a small set of measures that tie to business priorities, and a measure that is off target gets an action, an owner and a follow-up date.

## The 15-minute tier 1 agenda

Hold the meeting at the board, standing, at the start of the shift. Here is a time budget you can use as a starting point. It is a suggestion, not a standard. Adjust it to your team size.

1. **Minutes 0 to 2: safety.** Any incident, near miss or hazard since the last meeting. Name the owner of each open item.
2. **Minutes 2 to 6: yesterday against plan.** Read the numbers aloud: planned, actual, gap. Name the largest gap and its main cause in one sentence.
3. **Minutes 6 to 9: today's plan.** Check people, material, machines and known risks for the shift. Ask: what could stop us today?
4. **Minutes 9 to 13: new problems.** Add each problem to the action list with an owner and a due date. Decide which ones the team can fix and which ones go up to tier 2.
5. **Minutes 13 to 15: actions and close.** Review old actions. Close the finished ones, reset or escalate the stuck ones. End on time.

The Catalyst Connection article on [tiered meetings in manufacturing](https://blog.catalystconnection.org/tiered-meetings-in-manufacturing-the-operating-rhythm-that-protects-execution) gives a similar daily huddle agenda: the prior day's safety performance, quality defects, delivery performance, abnormalities, and whether the target was met, with an immediate countermeasure if it was not.

## How do tiers 2 and 3 work?

Tier 2 and tier 3 follow the same pattern with a different scope.

- **Tier 2** meets after the tier 1 meetings of its teams, so it hears today's issues. The supervisor brings the escalated items and the board numbers. The group decides who acts and by when. Typical topics are staffing gaps, material shortages and equipment downtime that cross team boundaries.
- **Tier 3** meets after tier 2. It reviews department KPIs, issues that need other departments such as maintenance, quality or logistics, and anything that needs a decision above supervisor level.
- **Tier 4**, where it exists, handles what the plant leadership alone can decide: investment, resources and plant-wide problems.

Each tier needs an escalation rule. Write it down: what goes up, to whom, and how the answer comes back. The Catalyst Connection article puts it well: "Escalation must lead to response." If teams raise issues and see no reaction, they stop raising them.

## Example: one morning across three tiers

This is an illustration with made-up numbers.

A plant starts its first shift at 6:00. The tier 1 meetings run from 6:05 to 6:20 at three cells. Cell B reports 168 units made against 200 planned yesterday, a gap of 32 units or 16%. The cause is 40 minutes lost to a missing fixture. The team cannot make a new fixture, so the team leader escalates it with the part number and the needed date.

Tier 2 meets at 6:30 for 15 minutes. The supervisor of the machining area hears the cell B issue and two others. Maintenance, who sit at the table, take the fixture repair as owner with a due date of the next morning. Tier 2 sends one issue, a recurring material shortage across two cells, up to tier 3.

Tier 3 meets at 7:00. The department manager contacts logistics about the shortage and sets a date. By the tier 1 meeting the next day, cell B reports that the fixture is back. The team leader closes the action. The loop from problem to answer took 24 hours, and the team saw it.

## How do you start your first meeting?

Start small and keep the first month simple.

1. Pick one cell with a willing team leader.
2. Set up a board with four measures and an action list.
3. Fix a time and a place, stand at the board and use the agenda above.
4. Let the supervisor attend for the first weeks to remove obstacles on the spot.
5. Start tier 2 only when tier 1 runs without support, and escalate at least one real issue.

For continuous improvement, review the recurring items after a few weeks. A problem that appears on the board three times is a candidate for a proper problem-solving session with the right people.

## Common mistakes

- **Solving problems in the meeting.** A 15-minute meeting becomes 45. Assign the problem and move on. The Catalyst Connection article lists detailed problem solving in daily huddles as a typical mistake.
- **Turning the meeting into a report.** If people read numbers and nobody acts, the meeting is a ritual.
- **Escalation without response.** Issues pile up at tier 2, and teams stop raising problems.
- **Too many measures.** A board with 15 KPIs hides the one that matters.
- **Stand-up meetings that turn into sit-down discussions.** Standing keeps the meeting short.
- **Skipping meetings when busy.** The busy days are when the board matters most.
- **Leaders who do not attend.** If managers never show up at the board, the team concludes it is not important.
