A value stream map update is the act of changing the map so that it matches the stream as it is now, and the rules that tell you when to do it. This article is for continuous improvement managers whose maps were drawn in a workshop and then left on a wall to age. It gives seven triggers, three levels of update, rules for versions and ownership, a review rhythm, and an invented example that follows one map through a year.

## Why does a value stream map go stale?

A value stream map, the result of [value stream mapping](https://www.m365.fm/entity/value-stream-mapping/), is a picture of the flow of material and information through a product family at one moment. Streams change. A new fixture shortens a changeover, demand rises, a supplier changes its delivery rhythm, or a step is moved to another line. Each change makes some box, arrow or number on the map untrue. After a few of them, the map stops matching the floor, and people stop trusting it.

The Lean Enterprise Institute (LEI) workbook [Learning to See](https://www.lean.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/01/Learning-to-See-part1.pdf) is clear on the principle. It says that as your future state becomes reality, a new future-state map should be drawn, and that this is continuous improvement at the value stream level, because there must always be a future-state map. It also says to fine-tune the future-state map as implementation progresses and not to get hung up on getting every detail perfect.

So a map is never finished. It is a lean tool for improvement, and an old map is only a record.

## When does a value stream map update become necessary?

Use these seven triggers. The list is my own working set, built on the workbook's principle, and your plant may add others.

1. **The future state is reached.** The workbook's trigger: draw a new future-state map.
2. **Demand or takt time changes.** Takt time is the pace set by customer demand: available time divided by demand. If demand moves by more than you can absorb, the number of operators and the pacing change.
3. **Equipment or layout changes.** A new machine, a new cell or a moved process step changes cycle times, handoffs and material flow.
4. **The product mix changes.** A new variant can change the routing or the changeover pattern, and it may move a product to another family.
5. **A supplier or customer changes.** A new delivery rhythm, order pattern or packaging rule changes the information flow and the inventory.
6. **Metrics drift from the map.** If lead time, inventory or delivery in your weekly review differ from the map's data boxes by more than a threshold you set, the map is wrong or the stream has changed.
7. **A new owner takes over.** The workbook suggests using maps to help ensure an effective hand-off when production managers change. A hand-off is a good moment to check that the map is true.

## Which three update levels should you use?

Not every change needs a new map. This three-level scheme is my own convention.

- **Level 1: refresh the numbers.** Update the data boxes and the timeline from your measures, for example monthly. No change to the structure.
- **Level 2: edit a step.** A process step, a buffer or an information link has changed. Edit that part of the current-state map, and note the change within two weeks.
- **Level 3: redraw.** Draw the current state again and a new future state with a cross-functional team. Do this when the future state is reached, or when demand, mix or layout change a lot.

Write the rule down: which trigger leads to which level, who decides and by when. Without it, the map is updated when somebody has time.

## Who owns the map, and how do you track versions?

The stream owner owns the map. The owner does not do all the drawing. Team members who know a step edit it, and the owner keeps the single history. For each map, record:

- **Version number and date.** For example version 1.2, 12 March.
- **Owner.** One name.
- **Change note.** One line: what changed and why.
- **Data source.** Where the numbers come from and when they were taken.
- **Who walked the flow.** A map without a walk is a guess.

Keep old versions. Comparing version 1 with version 3 shows how the stream moved: which steps disappeared, how the lead time changed, and which waste was removed. Print the current-state and future-state maps at A3 size at the board, and mark changes there, so that the board and the file stay the same.

## What review rhythm works?

Link the map to the routines you already have. This is a planning suggestion, not a standard:

- **Weekly:** the stream review looks at the measures. If a measure moves unexpectedly, ask whether the map still matches.
- **Monthly:** refresh the data boxes (level 1) and check open changes. The [LEI ebook on value stream mapping](https://email.lean.org/hubfs/ebook%20files/eBook-Top5-VSM-final.pdf) notes that Toyota reviews takt time for each process monthly, with tweaks every ten days, which shows that different numbers can have different review cycles.
- **Quarterly:** walk the whole stream with the team, compare it with the map, and decide on level 2 edits.
- **Yearly or at the future state:** level 3 redraw and a new plan.

## Example: one map through a year, invented numbers

This example is invented. The numbers are not from a real plant.

In January the team draws version 1 of the current-state map for a product family. Lead time is 12 days, and a changeover at the bottleneck takes 60 minutes.

In March a new fixture cuts that changeover to 20 minutes. This is a step change. The team edits the data box and the note on the map within two weeks, and saves version 1.1 (level 2).

In June demand rises by 25 percent, from 300 to 375 units a day. Available time is 27,000 seconds a day, so takt time falls from 27,000 divided by 300, or 90 seconds, to 27,000 divided by 375, or 72 seconds. The bottleneck cycle time is 80 seconds, which was fine at a takt time of 90 and is too slow at 72. The map shows the bottleneck at the new takt time, and the team adds an operator and edits the map (level 2, version 1.2).

In September lead time reaches 8 days and the future-state goals are met. The workbook's trigger applies, and a cross-functional team draws a new current state and a new future state (level 3, version 2). The old maps stay on file. Comparing version 1 and version 2 shows lead time down from 12 to 8 days, which is a reduction of one third.

## What if your plant has many value streams?

With several value streams, an update schedule is a workload question. Rank the value streams by revenue, as for the roadmap, and keep the monthly refresh for the top streams, and the quarterly walk for the rest. Use one template for every map, so that team members and leaders can read any of them quickly, and use the same data boxes in each: cycle time, lead time, inventory, yield and takt time.

The update is also the moment to identify areas for improvement. When you compare the current state with the last version, look for steps that gained waiting, handoffs that moved, and non value added work that crept back. That is process improvement at the level of the whole stream, not of one workstation.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating the map as a one-off.** A map without an update rule becomes wall art.
- **Redrawing everything every time.** Use the three levels, so that small changes cost little.
- **No owner.** If everybody may edit, nobody keeps the history.
- **Updating from the desk.** Walk the flow, and compare the map with what you see.
- **Overwriting old versions.** You lose the proof of progress.
- **Perfect details first.** The workbook warns against getting stuck on details; a roughly right map in use beats a perfect one in a drawer.
