# Total Productive Maintenance (TPM) and OEE: 8 Pillars, Losses, OEE Calculation, and Rollout

*Updated: November 2026 · Reading time: about 45 minutes (quick start: short answer, "Key takeaways", the worked OEE example and the checklists)*

> **Short answer:** Total productive maintenance (TPM) is a plant-wide system for keeping equipment able to do its job, with operators, maintenance, engineering and management all taking part. It was developed in Japan from American preventive maintenance; Nippondenso, a Toyota supplier, won the first PM Prize for it in 1971 [2][3]. TPM attacks six big losses (breakdowns, setup and adjustment, minor stops, reduced speed, defects and rework, startup losses), and overall equipment effectiveness (OEE = availability × performance × quality) measures how much of the planned time is left after those losses [6][8].

> **Key takeaways**
> - TPM is built on eight pillars, with 5S as the foundation. JIPM, the body that awards TPM prizes, treats four of them (focused improvement, autonomous maintenance, planned maintenance, quality maintenance) as mandatory because they act directly on losses and OEE [1].
> - OEE is the scoreboard of TPM, not its goal. The value is in the loss breakdown: minutes lost to each of the six big losses, in Pareto order.
> - Worked example in this guide: an injection molding machine at 87.1 % availability, 88.0 % performance and 96.0 % quality runs at 73.6 % OEE. After a year of TPM work on all six losses it reaches 84.3 % and makes 563 more good parts per day.
> - The 85 % "world-class OEE" figure is widely attributed to Nakajima but could not be traced to a primary passage; Ljungberg found OEE "only around 55 per cent" across about 20 cases [9]. Benchmark yourself against your own baseline.
> - Autonomous maintenance runs in seven steps, from initial cleaning to self-management [26][3]. Operators clean, inspect, lubricate and tighten; they do not take over repairs.
> - In the US, OSHA's lockout/tagout standard draws the line: normal production and routine minor servicing with effective alternative protection are outside 29 CFR 1910.147; cleaning inside guards or clearing jams in the danger zone usually is not [14].
> - A realistic rollout takes years, not months. Plan a pilot line in the first six months and stage gates before each expansion.
> - Before you buy a machine, calculate TEEP: it shows how much calendar time your existing equipment still has.

## What is total productive maintenance (TPM)?

[Total productive maintenance](https://www.m365.fm/entity/total-productive-maintenance/) (TPM) is a set of practices that keeps every machine in a production process able to perform its required tasks. The Lean Enterprise Institute (LEI) describes it that way and credits Denso in the Toyota Group with pioneering it [6]. The LEI reads the word "total" in three ways [6]:

1. **Total participation:** operators, maintenance technicians, line managers, manufacturing engineers and quality experts all take part.
2. **Total productivity of equipment:** the target is every loss, summarized as six major losses (downtime, changeover time, minor stops, speed losses, scrap and rework).
3. **Total life cycle:** maintenance practice changes with the equipment's stage, from design and installation through mature operation to replacement.

In plain terms: in a traditional plant, production runs the machine and maintenance fixes it when it breaks. In a TPM plant, the operator keeps the machine clean, inspected and lubricated and spots abnormalities early; maintenance spends its time on planned work, root causes and the skills operators do not have; engineering designs new equipment so that it is easy to maintain; and management measures the losses and removes the obstacles.

As a maintenance strategy, TPM differs from other maintenance strategies mainly in who does the maintenance work. Equipment maintenance becomes a shared job of production and maintenance, and operators take ownership of their equipment. The TPM principles behind it are short: prevent rather than repair, involve everyone, and judge the program by the losses on the production equipment, not by the number of maintenance activities completed.

### What TPM is not

- **Not a maintenance department program.** If only maintenance attends the kickoff, it is preventive maintenance with a new name.
- **Not "operators do the repairs."** Operators take over routine care and first detection. Skilled diagnostics and repairs stay with trained technicians.
- **Not a software purchase.** A CMMS or an OEE system helps once routines exist; it does not create them.
- **Not a headcount reduction project.** Programs announced or perceived that way lose operator trust in the first month (see the [mistakes section](#common-tpm-mistakes)).

### Benefits of total productive maintenance

The benefits of total productive maintenance come from a few mechanisms. The size of each depends on your starting point, so measure it on your own baseline:

- **More uptime:** fewer breakdowns and unplanned stops, because operators find leaks, loose bolts and worn parts before they fail.
- **Fewer quality issues:** equipment in basic condition produces fewer production defects and less startup scrap (quality maintenance).
- **Longer asset life:** cleaning, inspection and lubrication slow down deterioration. The EPA lists longer equipment life, fewer defects and fewer spills, leaks and upset conditions among the effects of well-maintained equipment [13].
- **A safer working environment:** a clean machine with visible abnormalities is easier and safer to work on, and the safety, health and environment pillar checks every change (see [TPM and safety](#tpm-and-safety-lockouttagout-ergonomics-and-the-operators-voice)).
- **Skills:** one-point lessons and the education and training pillar close knowledge gaps between operators and maintenance staff.

The EPA estimates that TPM can raise equipment efficiency rates by 15 to 25 percent within three years [13]; treat that as orientation, as the [business case section](#the-business-case-what-an-oee-point-is-worth) explains.

### TPM vs. preventive maintenance vs. predictive maintenance

These terms are often mixed up in search results. The table separates them.

| Approach | Who does the work | Trigger | Typical goal | Relationship to TPM |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Reactive (breakdown) maintenance | maintenance | failure | restore function | what TPM tries to reduce |
| Preventive maintenance (PM) | maintenance | calendar or usage interval | prevent known failure modes | part of the planned maintenance pillar |
| Predictive / condition-based maintenance | maintenance, reliability engineers, increasingly sensors and analytics | measured condition (vibration, temperature, oil analysis, current) | act just before failure | a technique inside planned maintenance; see the planned [predictive maintenance and IIoT guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/predictive-maintenance-iiot/) |
| Total productive maintenance | everyone, operators included | daily routines plus planned and condition-based work | zero breakdowns, zero defects, zero accidents caused by equipment, and the loss data to get there | the management system that holds the techniques together |

TPM grew out of preventive maintenance. A 2014 paper in the journal of the Japanese Society for Quality Control traces Japanese TPM back to American preventive maintenance and describes the plant-wide version as a later development of it [7].

### TPM vs. TQM and lean

TPM, total quality management (TQM) and lean share Japanese roots and many tools, but they look at the plant from different angles. TQM starts from the product and the customer, lean starts from the flow of value, and TPM starts from the equipment. In practice they meet: a stable machine is the precondition for flow and pull in [lean manufacturing](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/lean-manufacturing/), and a capable machine is the precondition for quality. McKone, Schroeder and Cua studied 97 plants in three countries and found that TPM implementation was explained more by managerial factors that plant management controls, namely just-in-time, TQM and employee involvement, than by country or industry [16]. TPM rarely survives in isolation from the rest of the operating system.

## History: from US preventive maintenance to Nippondenso and JIPM

The short history is consistent across sources; the dates differ by a few years depending on whom you ask.

| Year | Event | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 1951 | Seiichi Nakajima, after studying American preventive maintenance, introduces productive maintenance in Japan | [2] |
| late 1960s | Nippondenso first uses the term "total productive maintenance", with the slogan "productive maintenance with total employee participation"; one account dates its development to 1969, another describes Nakajima developing TPM between 1950 and 1970 | [3][2][5] |
| 1971 | Nippondenso receives the PM Prize (Distinguished Plant Award) from the Japan Institute of Plant Maintenance (JIPM), the first winner with the TPM method; many people date the origin of TPM to this year | [3][2] |
| 1982 | Nakajima publishes *TPM tenkai* (JIPM, Tokyo) | [4] |
| 1988, 1989 | English editions: *Introduction to TPM* (1988) and *TPM Development Program* (1989), both Productivity Press | [4][35][36] |
| 2015 | Nakajima dies on April 11, at age 96 | [2][4] |
| FY2024 | JIPM revises its TPM award system; Category A and B merge into the Award for TPM Excellence | [1] |

**Seiichi Nakajima (1919 to 2015)** is usually called the father of TPM. *Maintenance Technology* describes him leading the work at Nippondenso as part of JIPM and credits him with establishing the PM Awards, today's TPM Awards [2]. The Emperor of Japan awarded him the Medal with Blue Ribbon for his work on improving manufacturing through TPM [4].

**JIPM and the TPM Awards** still shape what "real" TPM means. Under the system that applies from fiscal year 2024 (old rules allowed through FY2026), JIPM awards five levels: the Award for TPM Excellence, the Award for Excellence in Consistent TPM Commitment, the Special Award for TPM Achievement, the Advanced Special Award for TPM Achievement and the Award for World-class TPM Achievement [1]. Companies outside Japan compete for them too; Yamaha Motor, for example, lists its JIPM award recipients from 1995 to 2016 on its corporate site [37].

**Why the history matters for a US plant:** TPM's starting point was American preventive maintenance [7][2]. What Japan added was the shift from a specialist function to plant-wide participation, the loss accounting behind OEE and a structured path for operators to take ownership.

## How TPM and OEE fit together

[Overall equipment effectiveness (OEE)](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/manufacturing-kpis/) is the measure Nakajima built into TPM to show how much of a machine's planned time produces good parts at ideal speed [8][10]. It splits the gap to perfection into three factors. An OEE score of 100 % means that a piece of equipment, a single machine or a production line, made only good parts, at maximum speed, with no stop time [22]. The OEE formula is:

> **OEE = Availability × Performance × Quality**

| Factor | Formula | Losses it captures |
|---|---|---|
| Availability | run time ÷ planned production time | breakdowns, setup and adjustment |
| Performance | (ideal cycle time × total count) ÷ run time | minor stops, reduced speed |
| Quality | good count ÷ total count | defects and rework, startup losses |

Muchiri and Pintelon's widely cited review defines the factors the same way: availability as operating time relative to loading time, performance as theoretical cycle time times actual output relative to operating time, and quality as good output relative to total output [8]. ISO 22400-2:2014, the international standard for KPIs in manufacturing operations management, uses the same logic with its own time terms [11][12].

The three factors collapse into one shorter formula that is worth memorizing because it doubles as a data check [22]:

> **OEE = (good count × ideal cycle time) ÷ planned production time**

**How the two relate:** TPM is the improvement system; OEE is its scoreboard. A plant can measure OEE without doing TPM (and many do), but then the number usually becomes a reporting exercise. A plant cannot do TPM properly without some form of loss measurement, because focused improvement and planned maintenance need to know which loss is biggest. Ljungberg made that point in 1998: implementing TPM requires measuring the size of each type of loss so that resources go where they matter, and many companies look only at major breakdowns while missing minor stops and speed losses [9].

**Where this guide stops and the KPI guide starts:** this article owns the TPM side of OEE: losses, the improvement practice and the program metrics. The [Manufacturing KPIs](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/manufacturing-kpis/) guide covers OEE in the context of all plant KPIs, including how to roll OEE up across lines correctly and a tested Power BI/DAX model. Both use the same definitions.

## The six big losses (and the 16 losses)

Nakajima assigned equipment losses to six categories, two per OEE factor [8]: availability losses (downtime due to equipment failures and setups), performance losses (minor stops and reduced speed) and quality losses (defects and startup scrap). The names vary slightly between sources; the table uses the common English terms with Nakajima's wording in parentheses.

| # | Loss (Nakajima's term) | OEE factor | Typical examples | First countermeasure | TPM pillar that owns it |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Breakdowns (equipment failure) | availability | hydraulic leak, motor failure, tool breakage | restore basic condition, root cause analysis, planned maintenance | planned maintenance, autonomous maintenance |
| 2 | Setup and adjustment | availability | mold or die change, first-piece trial and adjustment, material change | [SMED](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/smed-changeover-reduction/) (planned guide), standard settings | focused improvement |
| 3 | Minor stops (idling and minor stoppages) | performance | jammed part, blocked chute, sensor trip, operator clears and restarts | abnormality tags, small fixes, mistake-proofing | autonomous maintenance, focused improvement |
| 4 | Reduced speed | performance | machine runs below its ideal rate "because it runs better that way" | find the real reason for the slowdown; maintain ideal cycle time master data | focused improvement, quality maintenance |
| 5 | Defects and rework (quality defects and rework) | quality | scrap and rework during stable production | quality maintenance (conditions for zero defects) | quality maintenance |
| 6 | Startup losses (reduced yield during startup) | quality | scrap after a mold change, warm-up, first parts of a shift | standardized startup, shorter stabilization | quality maintenance, early equipment management |

**Where minor stops end and breakdowns begin** is a definition you have to set yourself. No standard fixes a binding threshold. The EPA describes stoppage losses as frequent downtime "from zero to 10 minutes in length" [13]; the OEE vendor Vorne describes small stops as typically under five minutes and not requiring maintenance personnel [21]. Pick one threshold (two or five minutes are common choices), write it into your OEE definition sheet and never change it silently, or your performance and availability trends will jump for no physical reason.

**Planned downtime is not one of the six losses.** Breaks, scheduled meetings, planned maintenance windows and "no orders" are excluded from planned production time before OEE is calculated. That is a convention, and it is the one place where plants most often game the number (see [limits](#limits-and-criticism-of-tpm-and-oee)).

### The 16 major losses

JIPM-tradition literature goes beyond equipment. The Kaizen Institute presents 16 major losses in three groups, covering losses of equipment effectiveness, of people's work efficiency and of production resources such as material, energy and tooling [15]. The six big losses are the equipment subset that OEE measures. The other ten cover losses around the machine, such as people's time, material, energy and tooling. They matter once OEE work has matured, because a plant can raise OEE while wasting labor and energy around the machine. [unverified: the individual names of the 16 losses differ between sources and were not checked against Nakajima's or JIPM's primary text, so they are not listed here.]

## How to calculate OEE: worked example with the six big losses

The example follows one machine through one day. All numbers are illustrative, and every step can be checked against the others.

**The machine:** IMM-12, a 300-ton injection molding machine with a two-cavity mold. Ideal cycle time 30 seconds per shot, so 15 seconds (0.25 minutes) per part. The plant runs three eight-hour shifts.

### Step 1: planned production time

| Item | Minutes |
|---|---|
| Calendar time (one day) | 1,440 |
| minus planned breaks (3 × 30 min) | −90 |
| minus planned maintenance window | −30 |
| **Planned production time** | **1,320** |

### Step 2: availability

Recorded downtime during planned production time:

| Event | Loss category | Minutes |
|---|---|---|
| Hydraulic hose leak, 2nd shift | breakdown | 45 |
| Heater band failure, 3rd shift | breakdown | 25 |
| Mold change A to B, incl. adjustment | setup and adjustment | 50 |
| Mold change B to C, incl. adjustment | setup and adjustment | 50 |
| **Total downtime** | | **170** |

Run time = 1,320 − 170 = 1,150 minutes.
**Availability = 1,150 ÷ 1,320 = 87.1 %.**

### Step 3: performance

At 0.25 minutes per part, 1,150 minutes of run time could produce 1,150 ÷ 0.25 = 4,600 parts. The counter shows 4,048 parts.

**Performance = (0.25 × 4,048) ÷ 1,150 = 1,012 ÷ 1,150 = 88.0 %.**

Running at full speed for the whole run time, the machine would have needed only 1,012 minutes for the 4,048 parts. The gap is 1,150 − 1,012 = 138 minutes. The machine log splits it: 61 minor stops add up to 84 minutes, and a slowed-down cycle (31 to 32 seconds instead of 30 for several hours after the second mold change) costs the remaining 54 minutes.

### Step 4: quality

Of the 4,048 parts, 85 were scrapped during startup after the mold changes and the morning start, and 76 were rejected during stable running (short shots and flash). Good parts: 4,048 − 85 − 76 = 3,887.

**Quality = 3,887 ÷ 4,048 = 96.0 %.**

Reworked parts count as defects here, consistent with TPM logic and with the [Manufacturing KPIs](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/manufacturing-kpis/) guide. Some definitions, for example in Kang et al.'s KPI hierarchy, count reworked parts as good [12]; decide once and do not compare plants that use different rules.

### Step 5: OEE

**OEE = 0.871 × 0.880 × 0.960 = 73.6 %.**

**Cross-check with the short formula:** 3,887 good parts × 0.25 minutes = 971.75 minutes of fully productive time; 971.75 ÷ 1,320 = **73.6 %**. If the two results differ, your input data are wrong (usually the ideal cycle time or the counter).

### Step 6: the loss waterfall

The total says little. The minutes per loss say where to start.

| Stage | Minutes | Share of planned time |
|---|---|---|
| Planned production time | 1,320.0 | 100.0 % |
| − breakdowns | 70.0 | 5.3 % |
| − setup and adjustment | 100.0 | 7.6 % |
| = run time | 1,150.0 | 87.1 % |
| − minor stops | 84.0 | 6.4 % |
| − reduced speed | 54.0 | 4.1 % |
| = net run time | 1,012.0 | 76.7 % |
| − startup losses (85 × 0.25) | 21.25 | 1.6 % |
| − defects and rework (76 × 0.25) | 19.0 | 1.4 % |
| = **fully productive time** | **971.75** | **73.6 %** |

**Reading the waterfall:** setup and adjustment (100 minutes) is the largest single loss, followed by minor stops (84) and breakdowns (70). The first TPM projects on IMM-12 are therefore a SMED workshop on the mold change and an autonomous maintenance pilot aimed at the minor stops, with planned maintenance taking over the hydraulic system. Quality losses look small in minutes (40 minutes together), but they also cost material and resin, which the minutes do not show.

### Step 7: the same machine after a year of TPM

Assume the following results after twelve months of focused improvement, autonomous maintenance and planned maintenance (illustrative targets, not a promise):

| Loss | Before (min) | After (min) | Main countermeasure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Breakdowns | 70 | 30 | hydraulic hose replacement plan, heater band checks in the CILS standard |
| Setup and adjustment | 100 | 60 | SMED: preheated molds, standard clamp heights, quick connectors |
| Minor stops | 84 | 50 | abnormality tags, chute redesign, part-present sensor cleaned daily |
| Reduced speed | 54 | 40 | restored cooling water flow, cycle time standard enforced |
| Startup scrap | 85 parts | 50 parts | startup standard with process window, first-shot checklist |
| Defects | 76 parts | 60 parts | quality maintenance matrix for mold temperature and holding pressure |

Run time = 1,320 − 30 − 60 = 1,230 minutes; availability = 1,230 ÷ 1,320 = 93.2 %.
Net run time = 1,230 − 50 − 40 = 1,140 minutes; total count = 1,140 ÷ 0.25 = 4,560 parts; performance = 1,140 ÷ 1,230 = 92.7 %.
Good parts = 4,560 − 50 − 60 = 4,450; quality = 4,450 ÷ 4,560 = 97.6 %.
**OEE = 4,450 × 0.25 ÷ 1,320 = 84.3 %**, up 10.7 points, with 563 more good parts per day from the same machine and the same shifts.

Whether those 563 parts are worth money is a separate question, answered in the [business case section](#the-business-case-what-an-oee-point-is-worth).

### Five OEE calculation rules that keep the number honest

1. **One time model for everyone.** Write down what is excluded from planned production time (breaks, meetings, planned maintenance, no orders, trials) and apply it to every machine. ISO 22400-2 offers a template time model [11].
2. **Ideal cycle time is the design or best demonstrated rate, not the standard used for costing.** A generous standard inflates performance and can push it above 100 %, which is a data error by definition [22].
3. **Count at the right point.** Total count where the machine produces; good count after the quality decision. Parts scrapped later in the process belong to that later step unless the cause is traced back.
4. **Calculate from sums, never average percentages.** For a week, a line or a plant, add up good count × ideal cycle time and planned production time, then divide. The [Manufacturing KPIs](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/manufacturing-kpis/) guide shows why averaging distorts the result.
5. **Analyze at least a week.** A single shift is noise. Zepf recommends analyzing at least one week (10,080 minutes) before drawing conclusions [24]; Vorne recommends collecting at least two weeks of data before choosing a focus [21].

## TEEP, loading, MTBF and MTTR

OEE answers "how well do we use the time we planned to run?" Two other questions need other metrics.

### TEEP: OEE against calendar time

Total effective equipment performance (TEEP) relates OEE to all calendar time [10]:

> **TEEP = OEE × loading**, with **loading = planned production time ÷ calendar time**

**IMM-12, per day:** loading = 1,320 ÷ 1,440 = 91.7 %; TEEP = 73.6 % × 91.7 % = **67.5 %**.

**IMM-12, per week (five production days):** loading = 5 × 1,320 ÷ 10,080 = 65.5 %; TEEP = 73.6 % × 65.5 % = **48.2 %**.

**What TEEP is for:** capacity and capital decisions. Before you approve a second molding machine, TEEP shows that IMM-12 is idle or losing time for more than half of the week's calendar time. Weekend shifts, planned maintenance in the existing windows and the OEE improvements above are all cheaper than a new press, provided demand, labor and the union contract allow it. Some web pages give OEE and TEEP the same formula; that is wrong, because the time base differs.

### OOE and other variants

Some sources add operations equipment effectiveness (OOE), which uses operating time including some planned downtime as its base, and research literature lists several further variants for lines and plants (for example overall line effectiveness or overall plant effectiveness) [8]. Definitions of these variants differ between authors and vendors. If you use one, define the time base in writing.

### MTBF and MTTR: breaking down the breakdown loss

Availability lumps breakdowns together with setups. Planned maintenance needs the breakdowns separately, as frequency and duration:

> **MTBF (mean time between failures) = run time ÷ number of breakdowns**
> **MTTR (mean time to repair) = total breakdown time ÷ number of breakdowns**

**IMM-12, one week:** run time 5 × 1,150 = 5,750 minutes; 14 breakdowns totaling 350 minutes.
MTBF = 5,750 ÷ 14 = **410.7 minutes** (about 6.8 hours).
MTTR = 350 ÷ 14 = **25.0 minutes**.
Availability from breakdowns alone = MTBF ÷ (MTBF + MTTR) = 410.7 ÷ 435.7 = **94.3 %**. The remaining gap to OEE availability (87.1 %) comes from setups.

**How TPM reads the two numbers:** a short MTBF points to deterioration, poor basic conditions or design weaknesses (autonomous maintenance and planned maintenance). A long MTTR points to diagnosis, spare parts, access or skills (planned maintenance, training, early equipment management). The Society for Maintenance & Reliability Professionals (SMRP) publishes more than 70 maintenance and reliability metrics with definitions, formulas, sample calculations and best-in-class targets in its Best Practices (7th edition); use those definitions if you want to benchmark with other SMRP members [19]. SMRP's Body of Knowledge, organized in five pillars (business management, manufacturing process reliability, equipment reliability, organization and leadership, work management), is a useful map of the skills a planned maintenance pillar needs [20].

## What is a good OEE? Benchmarks and their limits

This is one of the most searched questions, and most answers repeat one figure.

**The 85 % figure.** Many pages call an OEE score of 85 % "world-class OEE" for discrete manufacturing. Vorne, for example, calls 85 % world class, 60 % "fairly typical" and 40 % "not uncommon" for manufacturers starting out [21]. The 85 % is regularly attributed to Nakajima and is often built from 90 % availability, 95 % performance and 99.9 % quality (0.90 × 0.95 × 0.999 = 85.4 %). [unverified: the original passage in Nakajima's books could not be located; the [Manufacturing KPIs](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/manufacturing-kpis/) guide reached the same result.] The English Wikipedia article on OEE states that 85 % is not the right target in many cases, for example when not all losses are captured or when an industry's quality requirements are higher [10].

**What research found.** Ljungberg's study of about 20 cases found OEE "only around 55 per cent", with performance losses as the largest contributors, and suggested that OEE is lower for processes that are new to a company [9]. Vendor OEE benchmarks built from their own customer data rarely disclose their method; treat them as vendor claims [34].

**How to set a target instead:**

| Method | How it works | IMM-12 example |
|---|---|---|
| From your own baseline | measure cleanly for at least two to four weeks, then target a fixed share of the gap per year | baseline 73.6 %; close half the gap to 90 % in year one: target approx. 82 % |
| From demand | OEE needed = demand × ideal cycle time ÷ planned production time | 4,200 good parts per day × 0.25 ÷ 1,320 = 79.5 % |
| From the loss Pareto | target the minutes you can realistically remove from the top two losses | 40 minutes of setup and 34 minutes of minor stops = 74 minutes = +5.6 points |
| "Best of the best" | Vorne describes a "best of the best" goal [21]; a common version multiplies the best availability, best performance and best quality the machine has each reached in any shift of the measurement period | 93 % × 93 % × 98 % = 84.8 % |

**Three facts that make cross-plant comparison risky:**

- OEE behaves like a product of percentages and penalizes uneven factors: 20 % × 80 % = 16 %, while 50 % × 50 % = 25 % [10].
- Plants define planned time, ideal cycle time and quality differently, so the same machine can show a 15-point difference under two definitions.
- A continuous-process plant running 24/7 and a high-mix job shop with many changeovers have structurally different OEE levels.

Use OEE to compare the same asset over time. Use TEEP and the loss waterfall to compare assets for investment decisions.

## The 5S foundation and the 8 pillars of TPM

The usual picture of the TPM model is a temple: a foundation of [5S](https://www.m365.fm/entity/5s/), the eight pillars of total productive maintenance and a roof labeled with the goals (zero breakdowns, zero defects, zero accidents). Vorne's summary of the goals is a good one-liner: no breakdowns, no small stops or slow running, no defects and no accidents [21].

**Why 5S comes first:** you cannot see a leak, a loose bolt or a worn belt on a machine covered in oil, chips and dust. Sort, set in order, shine, standardize and sustain create the conditions under which abnormalities become visible. In TPM, "shine" becomes cleaning as inspection, which is the bridge to autonomous maintenance step 1. The planned [5S and visual management guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/5s-visual-management/) covers the method in detail.

### The eight pillars at a glance

JIPM lists the eight pillars in its award guidance as focused improvement, autonomous maintenance, planned maintenance, quality maintenance, initial (early equipment) management, education and training, efficiency of administrative and indirect departments, and safety, health and environment [1]. It states that the first four act directly on loss reduction and OEE and must always be implemented; early management and the administrative pillar can be left out of the assessment where an organization lacks those functions [1].

| # | Pillar (Japanese) | Purpose | Typical owner | First deliverable |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Focused improvement (kobetsu kaizen) | remove specific losses with structured problem solving | cross-functional team led by engineering or CI | loss Pareto and first [A3](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/lean-manufacturing/a3-problem-solving/) (planned) |
| 2 | Autonomous maintenance (jishu hozen) | operators keep basic conditions and detect abnormalities early | production, supported by maintenance | initial cleaning and tag board on the pilot machine |
| 3 | Planned maintenance | move maintenance from reactive to planned and predictive work | maintenance | criticality ranking and PM plan for the pilot asset |
| 4 | Quality maintenance (hinshitsu hozen) | set and keep the equipment conditions that produce zero defects | quality and process engineering | quality maintenance matrix (condition, standard, check) |
| 5 | Early equipment management | design and buy equipment that is easy to run, clean, maintain and change over | engineering and purchasing | maintainability checklist for the next purchase |
| 6 | Education and training | close the skill gaps that the other pillars expose | HR / training with maintenance | skills matrix for operators and technicians |
| 7 | Safety, health and environment | zero accidents and zero environmental incidents caused by equipment | EHS | hazard review of all autonomous maintenance tasks |
| 8 | Office (administrative) TPM | remove losses in support processes such as spare parts purchasing, planning and order handling | administrative departments | loss map of one support process |

The Kaizen Institute uses the same eight pillars and gives each its own step model (nine steps for focused improvement, six for planned maintenance, eight for early equipment management and five stages for training) [15]. The step names differ between consultancies; the logic does not.

### Quality maintenance

Quality maintenance links product defects to equipment conditions. The core tool is a matrix: for each critical quality characteristic (for IMM-12, for example, short shots), the team lists the equipment conditions that influence it (mold temperature, holding pressure, nozzle condition, material moisture), sets a standard range for each, defines who checks it how often and records the result. When a condition drifts out of its range, the team corrects it before a defect appears. Quality engineers in IATF 16949 or AS9100 environments will recognize the logic of a control plan; the difference is that the matrix is kept at the machine and its checks are built into the operators' CILS and the planned maintenance tasks. This turns quality control from inspecting parts into controlling conditions, which is the same idea as [poka-yoke](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/lean-manufacturing/poka-yoke/) (planned) applied to the machine.

### Early equipment management

Most maintenance problems are designed in. Early equipment management feeds the plant's experience (which bolts loosen, which filters are impossible to reach, which changeovers take too long) into the specification of new equipment and into the acceptance test. Practical tools: a maintainability and cleanability checklist in every request for quotation, a "maintenance prevention" log of design weaknesses found by autonomous maintenance teams, and an acceptance run where OEE, changeover time and cleaning time are measured before the purchase order is closed.

### Education and training

Each pillar creates skill gaps. Autonomous maintenance needs operators who understand lubrication, fasteners, pneumatics and hydraulics at an inspection level; planned maintenance needs technicians who can do failure analysis and condition monitoring. A skills matrix with four levels (knows the theory, can do it with help, can do it alone, can teach it) per person and per skill makes the gaps visible. The planned guide on [manufacturing workforce skills](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/operational-excellence/manufacturing-workforce-skills/) covers skills matrices and Training Within Industry (TWI) Job Instruction, which suits one-point lessons well.

### Safety, health and environment

The SHE pillar makes sure that the other pillars do not create new hazards (operators working closer to moving parts) and that equipment improvements reduce existing ones. The EPA points out the environmental side of TPM: well-maintained equipment produces fewer defects, lasts longer and has fewer spills, leaks and upset conditions [13]. The US-specific safety rules are covered in their own section [below](#tpm-and-safety-lockouttagout-ergonomics-and-the-operators-voice).

### Office TPM

Office TPM applies loss thinking to support work. Typical losses in a TPM context: a technician waits two hours for a spare part because the purchase requisition sits in an approval queue, a planned maintenance job is cancelled because the planner did not get the production schedule, or a machine waits for a work order. Mapping one of these support processes with [value stream mapping](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/value-stream-mapping/) is often the quickest win.

## Autonomous maintenance (jishu hozen): the seven steps

Autonomous maintenance is the pillar that most clearly distinguishes TPM from classic maintenance. Operators take over the basic care of their equipment in seven steps. The names follow the JIPM-standard sequence as presented in recent practitioner literature [26]; Ginder, Robinson and Robinson describe the same seven stages as "levels" [3].

| Step | Name | What operators do | Exit criterion (audit before moving on) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Initial cleaning and inspection | clean the machine thoroughly, find and tag every abnormality (leaks, loose parts, damage, missing labels, contamination) | all tags written, most closed or scheduled; operators can explain what they found |
| 2 | Eliminate sources of contamination and hard-to-access areas | remove the causes of dirt and leaks; make inspection points reachable (covers, windows, guides) | cleaning time measurably reduced; contamination sources listed with countermeasures |
| 3 | Tentative cleaning, lubrication and inspection standards | write the first CILS standard: what, how, how often, how long, with what | standard can be done in the time allowed; tasks fit the shift |
| 4 | General inspection training | learn the machine's subsystems (fasteners, lubrication, pneumatics, hydraulics, electrics, drives) and inspect them | operators pass a skill check per subsystem; new tags come from inspection, not just cleaning |
| 5 | Autonomous inspection | operators run their own inspection routine using the improved standards and checklists | inspection checklist owned by operators; breakdowns from neglected basic conditions near zero |
| 6 | Standardization | extend standards to the area around the machine: materials, tools, quality checks, data recording | workplace standards documented and kept; visual controls in place |
| 7 | Autonomous management (self-management) | teams set their own targets, analyze their losses and improve continuously | team runs its own loss Pareto and improvement plan with little supervision |

Some programs add a "step 0" for preparation: safety training, the team charter and a clear agreement on which tasks operators will and will not do.

### CILS: the daily routine

The visible output of steps 3 to 5 is a short daily routine of clean, inspect, lubricate and tighten (CILS; some plants say CILT, with T for tighten). A good CILS card for IMM-12 fits on one page, takes about 10 minutes at shift start and has a photo for each point. In the IMM-12 time model, the three daily CILS rounds (3 × 10 minutes) run inside the 30-minute planned maintenance window, so they are excluded from planned production time; if your plant runs CILS while the machine is scheduled to produce, it is an availability loss and must be recorded as such.

| # | Point | Method | Standard | Tool | Time | Frequency | Who |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Hydraulic oil level | sight glass | between min and max marks | none | 0.5 min | every shift | operator |
| 2 | Hydraulic hoses at clamp unit | visual, dry glove check | no wetness, no abrasion | flashlight | 1 min | every shift | operator |
| 3 | Mold cooling water flow | flow indicator | green zone (marked) | none | 0.5 min | every shift | operator |
| 4 | Tie bar lubrication | grease gun, 2 strokes | per lubrication chart | grease gun (color-coded) | 2 min | weekly | operator |
| 5 | Part-present sensor | wipe lens | clean, LED reacts | lint-free cloth | 0.5 min | every shift | operator |
| 6 | Safety gate interlock | functional test with machine in setup mode | machine stops when gate opens | none | 0.5 min | every shift | operator |
| 7 | Heater band connectors | visual (from outside guard only) | no discoloration | flashlight | 1 min | weekly | operator; maintenance if abnormal |

### Abnormality tags (F-tags)

During step 1 and every day after, operators hang a tag on every abnormality they find. These are not the red tags of 5S, which mark items to be sorted out of the workplace; TPM tags mark defects on the equipment itself. Two colors are common: one for issues the operator can fix, another for those that need maintenance. Each tag gets a number, a short description, the date and the finder's name, and it is logged on a board or in the CMMS. Two numbers make the system work: tags found per week (detection) and tags closed per week (response). If tags stay open for weeks, operators stop writing them; a closure target such as "operator tags within 7 days, maintenance tags within 30 days" keeps the system credible. [unverified: the closure targets are a practitioner rule of thumb, not a standard.]

### One-point lessons

A one-point lesson (OPL) is a single-page instruction on one topic, usually with a photo or sketch and fewer than 50 words of text: "how to check the hydraulic oil level", "what a worn V-belt looks like". OPLs are written by technicians or experienced operators, taught at the machine in five to ten minutes and signed off by each trainee. They are the cheapest training format in TPM.

### What operators should not do

Autonomous maintenance has limits, and they should be written into the team charter:

- no work inside guards or in hazardous energy zones without the plant's lockout/tagout procedure and the required authorization
- no electrical work beyond what qualified persons are allowed to do
- no changes to safety devices, interlocks or software parameters
- no repairs that need diagnosis; operators tag, maintenance fixes

Writing these limits down protects the operators, keeps maintenance on board ("they are not taking our jobs") and gives the union or employee representatives a clear basis for agreement.

## Planned maintenance: from firefighting to a system

Planned maintenance is the maintenance department's pillar. Its goal is to move work from unplanned to planned and from time-based to condition-based where that pays off, so that technicians spend their skills on prevention and root causes.

### A six-step sequence

The Kaizen Institute describes planned maintenance in six steps [15]; the sequence below is a common practical version.

1. **Evaluate equipment and rank criticality.** Score each asset for safety, quality, delivery and cost impact of a failure, and for failure frequency. Start with A-critical assets, typically bottleneck machines (see the planned [theory of constraints guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/theory-of-constraints/)).
2. **Restore and reverse deterioration.** Fix what autonomous maintenance and breakdown history reveal; there is no point planning maintenance on a machine in poor basic condition.
3. **Build an information system.** Asset register, failure history and work orders in a CMMS; breakdown codes that map to failure modes, not just "repaired".
4. **Build a time-based maintenance system.** PM tasks with intervals, procedures, parts and durations; scheduling coordinated with production planning.
5. **Build a condition-based (predictive) maintenance system.** Vibration, thermography, oil analysis or motor current where the failure mode gives a warning and the asset is critical enough.
6. **Evaluate and improve.** Track MTBF, MTTR, planned maintenance percentage and cost; review PM tasks that never find anything and failures that PM did not prevent.

### Maintenance strategy per failure mode

| Failure behavior | Example on IMM-12 | Best strategy |
|---|---|---|
| wear with a predictable life | screw and barrel, check ring | time- or usage-based replacement, measured wear |
| gives a measurable warning | hydraulic pump bearing (vibration), motor (current, temperature) | condition-based monitoring |
| random, no warning, cheap and fast to replace | indicator lamp, small fuse | run to failure with spare part on hand |
| hidden failure (only visible when needed) | safety interlock, emergency stop | functional test at fixed interval (in the CILS card) |
| caused by poor basic conditions | contamination in hydraulic oil, loose fittings | autonomous maintenance standard |

### Maintenance cost: what the data say

Hard US numbers on maintenance cost are scarce. A 2018 NIST report found that US manufacturers spent about $50 billion on outsourced maintenance and repair in 2016 according to Census data, a figure that excludes internal labor and materials [18]. The same report cites a Swedish survey in which downtime costs amounted to 23.9 % of total manufacturing cost and downtime took 13.3 % of planned production time, and an estimate that industrial maintenance costs 5.5 % of company turnover, with a range of 0.5 % to 25 % [18]. NIST itself notes that the literature rarely provides the data needed for a US-level estimate [18]. Treat any single "cost of downtime per hour" figure with care; it depends entirely on whether the machine is a bottleneck.

## Focused improvement: attack the biggest loss first

Focused improvement (kobetsu kaizen) is TPM's problem-solving pillar. Small cross-functional teams of four to six people [21] take one specific loss, usually the top bar of the loss Pareto, and remove it with a structured method.

**The method:** most plants use a [PDCA](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/lean-manufacturing/pdca-cycle/) (planned) or A3 format, and DMAIC from [Six Sigma and root cause analysis](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/six-sigma-root-cause-analysis/) (planned) for chronic, statistically complex losses. TPM adds a few loss-specific tools:

| Tool | Use in TPM |
|---|---|
| Loss Pareto in minutes | choose the theme; convert quality losses into minutes (count × ideal cycle time) so all losses compare |
| 5 whys | sporadic breakdowns and minor stops with a traceable cause chain |
| P-M analysis (phenomenon and mechanism) | chronic losses with several interacting causes; starts from the physical phenomenon and lists every equipment condition that could contribute [unverified: method origin in JIPM literature not checked against a primary source] |
| SMED | setup and adjustment losses |
| Fishbone (Ishikawa) diagram | structuring possible causes before data collection |
| Minor stop study | observe the machine for a full shift, record every stop under the threshold with cause |

**Worked example on IMM-12: minor stops.** The minor stop study shows 61 stops per day. Of these, 38 are parts hanging on the ejector side of the chute, 12 are part-present sensor trips caused by a dusty lens and 11 are miscellaneous. The team adjusts the chute angle and adds a guide rail (38 → 9 stops), puts sensor cleaning into the CILS card (12 → 2) and leaves the rest for now. Result: 61 → 22 stops, average 1.4 minutes each, so minor stop loss falls from about 84 to about 31 minutes per day. That is better than the 50 minutes assumed in Step 7 above; keep the conservative figure in the plan until the result holds for a month.

**Rule for the team:** one loss, one team, a fixed end date of 8 to 12 weeks, and a result measured in minutes on the waterfall. If a project runs longer, split it.

## TPM and safety: lockout/tagout, ergonomics and the operator's voice

TPM moves operators closer to the inside of their machines. In the US, that puts autonomous maintenance squarely in the scope of OSHA rules. An injury during a cleaning task can stop a TPM program, and rightly so.

### Where 29 CFR 1910.147 applies

OSHA's standard on the control of hazardous energy (lockout/tagout) "covers the servicing and maintenance of machines and equipment" [14]. Three points matter for TPM:

1. **Normal production operations are not covered.** Servicing during normal production is covered only in specific cases, for example when an employee must remove a guard or place part of the body into the point of operation [14].
2. **The minor servicing exception.** Minor tool changes and adjustments and other minor servicing activities are excluded if they are "routine, repetitive, and integral to the use of the equipment for production", provided the work is done using alternative measures that give effective protection [14].
3. **Procedures, training and inspections.** Energy control procedures must be developed, documented and used; authorized employees must be trained to recognize hazardous energy sources, affected employees instructed in the purpose and use of the procedure, and the procedures inspected at least annually [14].

**What this means for CILS tasks:** sort every autonomous maintenance task into one of three groups before step 1 starts.

| Group | Examples | Control |
|---|---|---|
| A: outside the danger zone, machine running or stopped normally | sight-glass checks, visual hose checks from outside the guard, reading gauges, wiping a sensor lens that is outside the guard | normal production; job safety analysis |
| B: minor servicing that is routine, repetitive and integral to production | clearing a part from an accessible chute with a tool, adjusting a guide during production | only with documented alternative measures that give effective protection, and only if the task truly meets the exception [14] |
| C: anything inside guards or in a danger zone, or non-routine | initial cleaning inside the clamp area, lubrication points behind guards, tightening inside the machine | full lockout/tagout; operators trained as authorized employees for these tasks or the task stays with maintenance |

Initial cleaning (step 1) almost always contains group C tasks. Plan the lockout procedures and the authorized-employee training before the first cleaning day, not after.

### Ergonomics

Cleaning and inspection add tasks to the operator's shift. Check them for awkward postures, reaching and lifting, which OSHA lists among the ergonomic risk factors behind musculoskeletal disorders [30]. Step 2 of autonomous maintenance is the right place to fix this: making inspection points reachable helps the machine and the operator at the same time.

### Unions, employee committees and respect for people

- **Contracts and job classifications.** In unionized plants, moving tasks between production and maintenance classifications can be a bargaining issue. Agree the task list from the "what operators should not do" section with the union before the pilot.
- **Employee committees.** TPM steering groups and pillar teams involve hourly employees. In the US, standing committees that deal with terms and conditions of employment can raise questions under Section 8(a)(2) of the National Labor Relations Act; the *Electromation* case (7th Circuit, 1994) is the reference [29]. Keep pillar team charters focused on equipment, quality and safety, and have employment counsel review them.
- **Time.** CILS and tag handling need paid time in the schedule. A program that expects operators to do autonomous maintenance "in between" teaches them that management does not mean it.
- **Credit.** Tag counts and closed tags are team achievements. Never use them to rate individuals.
- **A seat at the table.** Put hourly operators and technicians on the TPM steering group and on every pillar team, not only supervisors. They know where the machine leaks.

## TPM implementation roadmap: 0 to 36 months

The Kaizen Institute describes a three-phase TPM roadmap of 6 to 12, 12 to 24 and about 12 months [15]; Vorne's practical start uses five steps (pilot area, restore the equipment, measure OEE, address the major losses, introduce proactive maintenance) [21]. The roadmap below combines them with stage gates, so that a total productive maintenance program grows only as fast as the routines on the shop floor hold. Durations are a planning guideline. |---|---|---|---|

| 1. Pilot | 2–8 | one pilot machine (a constraint or a machine with visible pain), 5S, autonomous maintenance steps 1–3, first focused improvement project on the top loss, planned maintenance for the pilot | pilot OEE trend up for 8 weeks; CILS done on 90 % of shifts; tag closure on target |
| 2. Expand the core pillars | 8–20 | roll out to the line or area; autonomous maintenance steps 4–5; criticality ranking and PM system for A-critical assets; training pillar with skills matrix | at least 3 areas at step 3; planned maintenance percentage rising; MTBF up on A-assets |

## TPM KPIs: how to measure the program

OEE is the result. A TPM program also needs process KPIs that show whether the routines are actually running. The definitions below are common practice; for benchmarking with other plants, use the SMRP Best Practices definitions [19] or ISO 22400-2 where it covers the KPI [11].

| KPI | Formula | What it tells you | Level |
|---|---|---|---|
| OEE | good count × ideal cycle time ÷ planned production time | overall effect of the six losses | machine, line |
| Loss minutes per category | minutes per loss (quality losses × ideal cycle time) | where to focus; the most useful TPM chart | machine |
| TEEP | OEE × loading | unused calendar capacity | machine, plant |
| MTBF | run time ÷ number of breakdowns | reliability | asset |
| MTTR | breakdown time ÷ number of breakdowns | maintainability, response, skills | asset |
| Planned maintenance percentage | planned maintenance hours ÷ total maintenance hours | how much maintenance is under control | maintenance |
| PM compliance | PM work orders done on time ÷ PM work orders due | discipline of the planned maintenance system | maintenance |
| Maintenance backlog | planned but not yet done work in crew-weeks | resourcing | maintenance |
| CILS adherence | shifts with completed CILS ÷ shifts scheduled | autonomous maintenance discipline | team |
| Tags found / tags closed per week | counts | detection and response | team |
| Average tag age | days open | credibility of the tag system | team, maintenance |
| Autonomous maintenance step level | highest audited step per machine | program maturity | area |
| Skills matrix coverage | qualified people per critical skill | training pillar | area |
| Maintenance cost per unit | maintenance cost ÷ good units | cost effect | plant |
| Equipment-related safety incidents | count, and as part of the OSHA recordable rate | SHE pillar | plant |

**Leading and lagging:** CILS adherence, tag closure and PM compliance are leading indicators; they move first. OEE, MTBF and maintenance cost are lagging. A board with only lagging KPIs will show nothing for months and then lose management attention. The [Manufacturing KPIs](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/manufacturing-kpis/) guide explains the tiered KPI structure; the [Shop Floor Management](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/shop-floor-management/) guide shows how these numbers enter the daily [tier meeting](https://www.m365.fm/entity/tier-meeting/).

## The business case: what an OEE point is worth

Finance teams often reject TPM numbers because the benefit is calculated as "OEE points × output × price". That shortcut is only correct under one condition.

### Case 1: the machine is a constraint and demand exists

If IMM-12 limits the plant's output and customers would buy more, each good part adds its contribution margin. Assume a selling price of $1.20 per part and variable cost (resin, energy, packaging) of $0.70, so a contribution margin of $0.50.

- One OEE point on IMM-12 = 1 % × 1,320 minutes ÷ 0.25 minutes = **52.8 good parts per day**.
- The improvement from 73.6 % to 84.3 % = 563 good parts per day × $0.50 = **$281.50 per day**.
- At 250 production days: **$70,375 per year** in additional contribution, from one machine.

The [theory of constraints guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/theory-of-constraints/) (planned) explains why an hour gained at the bottleneck is worth the output of the whole plant for that hour.

### Case 2: the machine is not a constraint

If IMM-12 has spare capacity and demand is fixed, the 563 extra parts are worth nothing; making them would only build inventory. The benefit is then the cost avoided to make the same output:

| Benefit | IMM-12 example (per year, illustrative) |
|---|---|
| Fewer scrapped parts | (85 + 76 − 50 − 60) = 51 parts/day × $0.30 material × 250 days = $3,825 |
| Less planned time for the same output | at fixed demand the machine needs about 12.7 % less planned production time; savings come only if labor, energy or a shift can actually be removed or redeployed |
| Fewer breakdowns | lower emergency repair, overtime and expediting cost: take actual figures from the CMMS and payroll |
| Deferred capital spending | if TEEP shows that a second machine is no longer needed, the avoided investment and its depreciation |

The 12.7 % comes from the OEE ratio: the same good output needs 73.6 ÷ 84.3 = 87.3 % of the previous planned time.

| Cost item | Capex or opex | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Operator and technician time for CILS, tags, training and workshops | opex | the largest cost; budget hours explicitly |
| TPM coordinator | opex | full or part time |

| Tools, lubrication equipment, visual controls | opex | small |

| External training or consulting | opex | NIST MEP centers offer lean and process improvement services for small and medium-sized manufacturers [27][28] |

**Illustrative first-year payback for the IMM-12 pilot (constraint case):**

| Item | Calculation | Amount |
|---|---|---|
| Pilot workshops and training (initial cleaning, SMED, minor stop project, OPLs) | 400 team hours × $45 loaded rate | $18,000 |
| Restoration parts and small modifications (hoses, seals, chute guide, access covers) | estimate from step 1 tags | $8,000 |
| TPM coordinator share for the pilot | 0.2 FTE × 6 months, approx. | $9,000 |
| **First-year cost** | | **$35,000** |
| Additional contribution once the full improvement holds | $281.50 × 250 days | $70,375 per year |
| **Simple payback** | $35,000 ÷ ($70,375 ÷ 12) | **about 6 months after the improvement holds** |

All rates and amounts are illustrative; replace them with your own payroll and purchasing data. In the non-constraint case the same costs pay back far more slowly, which is one more reason to start TPM at the constraint. CILS time is not in the table because in this time model it runs inside the existing planned maintenance window.

**Finance rules for a credible case:** set the baseline before the program starts, agree in advance which benefits count (constraint output, scrap, overtime, avoided capex), track them monthly with the plant controller and do not count the same minute twice (for example as both "more output" and "less overtime").

The EPA estimates that TPM can raise equipment efficiency rates by 15 to 25 percent within three years [13]. That is a government estimate without a published method on the page, so use your own baseline for the business case and treat the EPA figure as orientation.

## Common TPM mistakes

| Mistake | What happens | Better approach |
|---|---|---|
| TPM launched as a maintenance program | production sees it as "maintenance's job"; operators do not join | plant manager sponsors; production leads autonomous maintenance |
| Big-bang rollout to all machines | coordinators and technicians overloaded; tags pile up; credibility lost | one pilot, stage gates, expansion only after the gate |
| OEE reported but not broken down | a monthly percentage nobody can act on | loss waterfall in minutes and a Pareto per machine |
| OEE as a target tied to bonuses | changeovers avoided, stops booked as "planned", ideal cycle times stretched | OEE as an improvement KPI for the same asset; bonuses (if any) on process KPIs and team results |
| Comparing OEE across plants or machine types | wrong conclusions, defensive teams | compare each asset with its own baseline |
| Skipping initial cleaning | standards written for a machine in poor condition | step 1 done thoroughly, with lockout/tagout |
| Tags written but not closed | operators stop tagging after a few weeks | closure targets and weekly review of tag age |
| No time for CILS in the schedule | CILS done on paper only | CILS minutes in the shift standard; adherence tracked |
| Operators asked to do repairs | safety risk, conflict with maintenance and union | written task boundary; operators tag, maintenance fixes |
| Planned maintenance plan copied from the OEM manual | PM tasks that find nothing, failures that PM does not prevent | tasks by failure mode and criticality; review PM yield |
| Software first | an OEE dashboard with wrong definitions and no routine behind it | definitions and manual routine first, then automate |
| No finance involvement | benefits doubted, budget cut in year two | controller agrees baseline and benefit rules up front |
| Announcing TPM next to layoffs | operators protect their knowledge | clear statement on employment security for TPM-freed capacity |

## Limits and criticism of TPM and OEE

TPM is well established, but it has limits that the vendor pages in the SERP rarely mention.

**1. Implementation takes long and often stalls.** Reliable Plant reports a survey by a manufacturing research center in which 27 % of respondents named TPM a current business initiative, but only 6 % were fully implementing it and 5 % called it their plant's approach to maintenance and reliability [23]. The source is second-hand and the survey details are not given, but the pattern (many start, few finish) matches what practitioners describe.

**2. OEE is easy to game.** Every input is a convention: what counts as planned time, which cycle time is ideal, where the threshold between minor stop and breakdown lies. Change any of them and OEE moves without any physical change. When OEE becomes a target for bonuses or plant rankings, these conventions drift. Goodhart's law applies (see the [Manufacturing KPIs](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/manufacturing-kpis/) guide).

**3. OEE is not a profit measure.** A non-constraint machine at 90 % OEE that builds inventory nobody needs is worse for the business than the same machine at 60 % OEE that runs only to customer demand. Use OEE on constraints and on machines with quality or reliability problems; do not push it everywhere.

**4. OEE ignores some costs.** Energy, labor, tooling and material losses outside the six categories are invisible in OEE. The 16-loss view and maintenance cost per unit fill part of the gap [15].

**5. Fit to high-mix, low-volume production.** In a job shop with frequent changeovers and many products, ideal cycle times are harder to maintain and setup dominates the loss picture. TPM still works, but SMED and planned maintenance usually matter more than chasing a high OEE number.

**6. Data quality.** Manual recording tends to miss small stops [8], and Muchiri and Pintelon name data collection accuracy as one of the main practical problems of OEE measurement [8]. Ljungberg recommends starting with a simple model and then combining automatic and manual recording to get both an accurate measure and an insight into causes [9].

**7. Workload and trust.** Autonomous maintenance adds tasks to the operator's day. Without time in the schedule, a fair task boundary and visible response to tags, it is experienced as more work for the same pay. That is why the worker voice is part of the roadmap, not an afterthought.

## TPM in the digital plant: data, CMMS, MES and predictive maintenance

Digital tools make TPM faster and more precise once the routines exist. They do not replace initial cleaning, tags or the conversation at the machine.

### Data collection for OEE

| Level | How | Strengths | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Paper log per shift | operator writes stops, counts, reasons | cheap, starts tomorrow, builds awareness | misses small stops [8]; transcription effort |
| Spreadsheet or form app | operator enters the same data digitally | easier analysis | same detection limits as paper |
| Machine signal plus operator reason | run/stop and count signals from the PLC or a sensor; operator selects reason on a terminal (define in writing which machine state counts as "running", or two systems will report two OEEs) | accurate times and counts, small stops visible | reason quality depends on the catalog and on discipline |
| MES with ISO 22400 KPIs | OEE calculated in the manufacturing execution system at ISA-95 Level 3 | consistent definitions across the plant, integration with orders and quality | implementation effort, master data maintenance |

**Interfaces worth knowing:** MTConnect is an open standard for manufacturing data interoperability, published as ANSI/MTC1.4-2018 and common in US machine shops [31]. OPC UA offers companion specifications, including an ISA-95 common object model [32]. The ISA-95 standard places manufacturing operations management, where OEE and maintenance management live, at Level 3 between control systems and ERP [33]. The planned [MES and ISA-95 guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/mes-isa-95/) explains the architecture; [Manufacturing IT with Azure](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/manufacturing-it-azure/) shows how machine data reach the cloud. Every new connection into the machine network is also an OT security question; see the planned [OT cybersecurity and ISA/IEC 62443 guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/ot-cybersecurity-iec-62443/).

### CMMS and the tag system

A computerized maintenance management system (CMMS) holds the asset register, work orders, PM schedules and failure history. For TPM, two configurations matter most: abnormality tags should become work requests in the CMMS (with the tag number), and breakdown work orders should carry a failure mode code that maps to the downtime reason catalog. Then the loss Pareto from production and the failure history from maintenance tell the same story.

### Predictive maintenance

Condition-based and predictive maintenance belong to the planned maintenance pillar. Sensors and analytics are worth it for critical assets with failure modes that give a measurable warning (bearings, pumps, motors, gearboxes). They are not a substitute for autonomous maintenance: a model that predicts a hydraulic pump failure from vibration data does not tell you that the hose next to it is rubbing on a bracket, while an operator with a flashlight does. The planned [predictive maintenance and IIoT guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/predictive-maintenance-iiot/) and the m365.fm episode [Predictive Maintenance on Azure for Manufacturers: What You Actually Need Before the Model](https://www.m365.fm/show/episodes/predictive-maintenance-on-azure-for-manufacturers-what-you-actually-need-before-the-model/) cover the prerequisites.

### Dashboards

A good TPM dashboard per machine has four elements: OEE trend from summed times, the loss waterfall in minutes, the top downtime reasons as a Pareto, and the open tags with their age. The [Manufacturing KPIs](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/manufacturing-kpis/) guide contains a Power BI data model and DAX measures that roll OEE up correctly. For capacity questions across a whole line, a [digital twin or simulation model](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/digital-twin-manufacturing/) can show how availability and performance losses at one machine propagate through buffers to the line's output.

## FAQ: total productive maintenance and OEE

### What is total productive maintenance in simple terms?

TPM is a way of running a plant in which everyone, not only the maintenance department, takes care of the equipment. Operators keep their machines clean, inspected and lubricated and report problems early; maintenance plans its work and fixes root causes; engineering buys equipment that is easy to maintain. The goal is no breakdowns, no small stops, no defects and no accidents caused by equipment [6][21].

### What does TPM stand for in manufacturing?

Total productive maintenance. In other contexts TPM can mean something else (for example a trusted platform module in computers), so add "maintenance" or "OEE" when you search for the manufacturing meaning.

### What are the 8 pillars of TPM?

Focused improvement, autonomous maintenance, planned maintenance, quality maintenance, early equipment management, education and training, safety, health and environment, and office (administrative) TPM, all resting on a 5S foundation. JIPM treats the first four as mandatory because they act directly on losses and OEE [1].

### What is the difference between TPM and OEE?

TPM is the improvement system; OEE is the metric that shows its effect on equipment. OEE measures how much of planned production time produced good parts at ideal speed, and its breakdown into six losses tells TPM teams where to work [8][9].

### How do you calculate OEE?

OEE = availability × performance × quality, or in one step: good count × ideal cycle time ÷ planned production time. Example: 3,887 good parts × 0.25 minutes ÷ 1,320 minutes = 73.6 % (see the [worked example](#how-to-calculate-oee-worked-example-with-the-six-big-losses)).

### What are the six big losses?

Breakdowns and setup/adjustment (availability losses), minor stops and reduced speed (performance losses), and defects/rework and startup losses (quality losses) [8].

### What is a good OEE score?

There is no universal answer. 85 % is widely called world class [21], but its attribution to Nakajima could not be confirmed, and research found typical values around 55 % [9]. Set targets from your own baseline, from demand, and from the minutes you can remove from your top losses.

### Is OEE a KPI?

Yes. ISO 22400-2, the international standard for key performance indicators in manufacturing operations management, defines OEE as one of its KPIs [11]. In a TPM program, OEE is the main result KPI per machine; process KPIs such as tag closure and PM compliance show whether the routines that move OEE are in place.

### Can OEE exceed 100 %?

No. An OEE score of 100 % is perfect production: only good parts, at ideal cycle time, with no stop time. A value above 100 %, or a performance factor above 100 %, means the ideal cycle time is set too slow or the counts are wrong; it is a data error by definition [22].

### How can you improve OEE?

Measure the losses in minutes, sort them in a Pareto and attack the biggest one first. Breakdowns call for restoring basic condition and planned maintenance, minor stops for autonomous maintenance and abnormality tags, and setup losses for [SMED](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/smed-changeover-reduction/). Improve OEE on one machine before rolling the method out, and check that the time won is used to produce, otherwise OEE does not move (see [focused improvement](#focused-improvement-attack-the-biggest-loss-first)).

### What is autonomous maintenance?

The TPM pillar in which operators take over basic equipment care in seven steps: initial cleaning and inspection, eliminating contamination sources and hard-to-access areas, tentative CILS standards, general inspection training, autonomous inspection, standardization and self-management [26][3].

### What is the difference between TPM and preventive maintenance?

Preventive maintenance is a maintenance technique: scheduled tasks done by maintenance to prevent known failures. TPM is a plant-wide system that includes preventive maintenance in its planned maintenance pillar and adds operator involvement, loss measurement and structured improvement. Historically, TPM grew out of American preventive maintenance [7][2].

### What is TBM and CBM in TPM?

TBM is time-based maintenance: tasks at fixed intervals, such as changing a filter every 500 operating hours. CBM is condition-based maintenance: tasks triggered by a measured condition, such as vibration, temperature or oil analysis. Both belong to the planned maintenance pillar, which builds a time-based system first and adds condition-based maintenance where the failure mode gives a warning and the asset is critical enough (see [planned maintenance](#planned-maintenance-from-firefighting-to-a-system)).

### What is the difference between TPM and TQM?

TQM focuses on the quality of products and processes across the company; TPM focuses on equipment and its losses. Both rely on employee involvement and structured problem solving. In McKone, Schroeder and Cua's study of 97 plants, TQM, JIT and employee involvement practices were among the managerial factors that explained how far TPM was implemented [16].

### How long does TPM implementation take?

Expect a pilot in the first six months and two to three years until the core pillars run across a plant. The Kaizen Institute describes phases of 6 to 12, 12 to 24 and about 12 months [15]. JIPM award preparation usually takes longer. [unverified: no study gives a typical duration.]

### What is the role of 5S in TPM?

5S is the foundation. Without a clean and orderly workplace, abnormalities stay hidden; "shine" becomes cleaning as inspection, which is autonomous maintenance step 1.

### Does lockout/tagout apply to autonomous maintenance?

Often, yes. OSHA's 29 CFR 1910.147 covers servicing and maintenance; minor servicing is only excluded when it is routine, repetitive and integral to production and done with alternative measures that give effective protection [14]. Initial cleaning inside guards needs full lockout/tagout.

### Is TPM only for manufacturing?

TPM began in manufacturing and fits best where equipment drives output: discrete manufacturing, process industries, packaging, utilities and logistics centers with automated equipment. Office TPM applies loss thinking to support processes.

### What does TEEP mean and when should I use it?

Total effective equipment performance = OEE × loading, where loading = planned production time ÷ calendar time [10]. Use it for capacity and investment decisions, because it shows unused calendar time.

### How is TPM success measured?

With OEE and the loss waterfall as results, MTBF, MTTR and maintenance cost per unit as reliability and cost results, and leading process KPIs such as CILS adherence, tag closure, PM compliance and planned maintenance percentage.

### Can small manufacturers do TPM?

Yes. Start with one machine, paper logs and the first three autonomous maintenance steps. NIST MEP centers in every state and Puerto Rico support small and medium-sized manufacturers with lean and process improvement services [27][28].

## Conclusion and first steps

Total productive maintenance turns equipment care from a specialist job into a plant routine. Its logic is simple: measure the six big losses, restore basic conditions, let operators keep them, let maintenance plan and prevent, and remove the biggest loss with a focused team. OEE is the scoreboard that makes this visible, and its value lies in the minutes per loss, not in the headline percentage.

The worked example shows what that means in numbers: a molding machine at 73.6 % OEE has 100 minutes of setup, 84 minutes of minor stops and 70 minutes of breakdowns per day that can be attacked one by one. Whether the result is worth money depends on whether the machine is a constraint, and the business case should say so honestly. The limits are real: OEE can be gamed, TPM takes years and often stalls, and operators will only take part if they get time, safety and a response to their tags.

**Your first steps in the next 30 days**

- [ ] Pick one pilot machine, ideally a constraint with visible losses
- [ ] Write the OEE definition sheet (time model, ideal cycle time, threshold, counting points) and agree it with production, maintenance and finance
- [ ] Start a paper stop log per shift with the two-level reason catalog
- [ ] Calculate OEE for two weeks from sums and draw the loss waterfall in minutes
- [ ] Review every planned autonomous maintenance task with EHS and sort it into groups A, B and C for lockout/tagout
- [ ] Brief the union or employee representatives on the task boundary and the employment-security position
- [ ] Schedule the initial cleaning day for the pilot with lockout procedures and tags ready
- [ ] Ask your plant controller to sign off the baseline and the benefit rules
- [ ] Check whether your state's NIST MEP center offers training or an assessment for your pilot [27]
