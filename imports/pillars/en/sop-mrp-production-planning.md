# Sales and Operations Planning (S&OP) and MRP: How Production Planning Works From Plan to Parts

*Updated: November 2026 · Reading time: about 60 minutes (quick start: short answer, key takeaways, and the worked MRP example in the MRP section)*

> **Short answer:** Sales and operations planning (S&OP) is a monthly management process in which sales, operations, supply chain and finance agree on one plan for demand, supply and inventory by product family, typically on a rolling horizon of 18 to 36 months [5][6]; academic sources describe its tactical core as reaching up to about 18 months [1]. The master production schedule (MPS) turns that plan into end-item quantities by week, and material requirements planning (MRP) explodes the MPS through the bills of material into dated orders for every part and raw material [7][13]. In most plants the MPS and MRP run inside the ERP system; S&OP runs in a meeting cycle supported by spreadsheets, ERP or dedicated planning software.

> **Key takeaways**
> - S&OP decides *how much* by product family and month; the MPS decides *which end items* by week; MRP decides *which parts, how many and when*. Detailed scheduling (sequence by machine and hour) sits below and is covered in the [production scheduling and APS guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/production-scheduling-aps/).
> - The S&OP concept goes back to aggregate production planning in the 1950s; Oliver Wight consultants shaped modern S&OP in the 1980s and coined integrated business planning (IBP) in the early 2000s [1][2].
> - Joseph Orlicky formalized MRP in 1964; Black & Decker was the first user, and by 1981 about 8,000 companies ran MRP [3][4]. Oliver Wight extended it to MRP II in 1983 [3].
> - A typical monthly S&OP cycle has five steps: data gathering, demand review, supply review, pre-S&OP (reconciliation) meeting and executive S&OP meeting [1].
> - MRP assumes infinite capacity and fixed lead times; that is why it needs a rough-cut capacity check above it and a finite scheduler below it [8][9].
> - A small change in the MPS can move orders at several BOM levels at once ("nervousness"). Freezing the near-term schedule and change-cost rules dampen it better than extra safety stock [10].
> - DDMRP replaces the forecast-driven explosion at selected decoupling points with sized buffers (red, yellow, green) and a net flow equation [11][12].
> - Measure S&OP with forecast accuracy (WMAPE) *and* bias, plan attainment, schedule stability and service level. A 94% accurate forecast that is always 2% high still builds inventory.

## What is sales and operations planning? Definition and scope

**Sales and operations planning (S&OP)** is a recurring decision process that integrates the demand forecast with supply planning into one plan that sales, marketing, operations, supply chain and finance all work to [1]. The academic literature describes it as a tactical process with a horizon of up to about 18 months, usually run monthly or triggered by events [1]. Vendor and practitioner sources often describe a rolling horizon of 18 to 36 months [5][6]. The difference is not a contradiction: the first months are planned in more detail, the outer months only in families and capacity blocks.

Three features distinguish S&OP from a sales forecast meeting or a production meeting:

- **One set of numbers.** Demand, supply, inventory or backlog and the financial value of the plan are reviewed together, by product family, in units and dollars.
- **Decisions, not reports.** The output is a set of decisions: approve a capacity increase, accept a lower service level for a slow family, delay a launch, prebuild ahead of a shutdown.
- **Integration instead of coordination.** Rogelio Oliva and Noel Watson showed in a case study that S&OP adds value when functions plan *together against shared targets*, not when they merely coordinate plans that each function made alone [21].

The professional body of knowledge for S&OP, master scheduling and MRP in the US is maintained by ASCM (Association for Supply Chain Management, formerly APICS). Its CPIM (Certified in Planning and Inventory Management) program covers S&OP, demand, supply, inventory and detailed schedules as separate modules [24].

### Planning, not scheduling

S&OP is not the production schedule. It works with product families, monthly buckets and aggregate capacity such as "welding hours" or "line 2 shifts." The question "which order runs first on machine 14 tomorrow" belongs to detailed scheduling, which the [production scheduling and APS guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/production-scheduling-aps/) covers in depth, including finite capacity scheduling and drum-buffer-rope. This article stays one level up and connects S&OP to the master production schedule and MRP.

### What S&OP is used for

The table shows typical decisions that only a cross-functional S&OP can make, because no single function owns both the cost and the benefit.

| Decision | Who gains | Who pays | Why it needs S&OP |
|---|---|---|---|
| Add a second shift in welding from March | Sales (service level) | Operations (labor cost), HR (hiring) | trade-off between revenue and cost |
| Prebuild 2,000 units before a summer shutdown | Customers, sales | Finance (working capital), warehouse | inventory investment must be approved |
| Cut a low-margin variant | Operations (fewer changeovers) | Sales (lost revenue) | portfolio decision with margin impact |
| Accept a large promotion order | Sales, marketing | Operations (overtime), procurement | capacity and material feasibility |
| Qualify a second supplier for a critical part | Supply chain (risk) | Procurement, quality (cost of qualification) | resilience versus unit cost, see [supply chain resilience](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/operational-excellence/supply-chain-resilience/) |

### Who takes part

The executive meeting is chaired by the general manager or business unit president. Typical standing members are the heads of sales, marketing, operations, supply chain, finance and, in engineered products, engineering or product management. Below the executive meeting, the demand planner, master scheduler, supply planner and a finance analyst do most of the work. Section 4 lists the roles step by step.

## The production planning hierarchy: S&OP, MPS, RCCP, MRP and scheduling

Manufacturing planning and control (MPC) is organized as a hierarchy. Each level works with a longer horizon and coarser data than the level below and passes its decisions down as targets. Stanley Gershwin's MIT course material summarizes MRP II this way: a hierarchy of long-, intermediate- and short-range planning, with demand management, forecasting, capacity planning and shop floor control around the MRP core [8].

| Level | Typical horizon | Bucket | Planning object | Key question | Output | Typical tool |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Strategic and business plan | 3–5 years | year | business, plant, network | Which markets, plants and capacity investments? | budget, capex plan | finance tools, Excel |
| S&OP / IBP | 18–36 months [5][6] | month | product family, aggregate resource | How much do we sell and make, at what inventory and cost? | approved family plan | S&OP software, ERP, Excel |
| Master production schedule (MPS) | a few weeks to several months, at least the cumulative lead time | week (day near term) | end item or planning item | Which end items, how many, in which week? | MPS, ATP | ERP |
| Rough-cut capacity planning (RCCP) | same as MPS | week or month | critical resources | Is the MPS feasible at the bottleneck? | load vs. capacity per key resource | ERP, Excel |
| Material requirements planning (MRP) | same as MPS | day or week | every component and material | Which parts, how many, when to order or start? | planned orders, purchase requisitions, exception messages | ERP |
| Capacity requirements planning (CRP) | weeks | day or week | every work center | Is there enough capacity for all planned and released orders? | detailed load profiles | ERP |
| Detailed scheduling and dispatching | hours to a few weeks | hour, shift | operation on a machine | In which sequence does each operation run? | schedule, dispatch list | APS, MES |

Two rules hold the hierarchy together:

1. **Disaggregation must add up.** The sum of the MPS for all end items in a family must match the S&OP family plan for the same month, within an agreed tolerance (for example plus or minus 5%). Otherwise the S&OP decision has no effect on the floor.
2. **Feasibility is checked at each level.** S&OP checks aggregate capacity (resource planning), the MPS is checked by rough-cut capacity planning, and MRP output is checked by capacity requirements planning or by a finite scheduler. MRP itself does not check capacity [8][9].

### Where ERP, APS and MES sit

ANSI/ISA-95 separates business planning and logistics (Level 4, typically ERP) from manufacturing operations management (Level 3, typically MES and detailed scheduling) [25]. S&OP, MPS and MRP are Level 4 functions. The [MES and ISA-95 guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/mes-isa-95/) (planned) explains the Level 3 side, and the [Azure for manufacturing guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/manufacturing-it-azure/) shows how the data between the levels can be integrated.

### Make-to-stock, assemble-to-order, make-to-order

The production environment decides where the MPS sits in the bill of materials:

| Environment | Customer waits for | MPS planned at | Example | S&OP focus |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Make-to-stock (MTS) | nothing (ships from stock) | end item | consumer goods, standard pumps | inventory target per family |
| Assemble-to-order (ATO) | final assembly | modules and options (planning bill) | configurable desks, trucks, PCs | module mix, option percentages |
| Make-to-order (MTO) | manufacturing | end item or key subassemblies after order | custom machinery parts | backlog target, lead time |
| Engineer-to-order (ETO) | design and manufacturing | project milestones, long-lead items | special machines, aircraft interiors | engineering and test capacity, see [aerospace production planning](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/aerospace-production-planning/) |

In MTS, S&OP balances demand against a target inventory. In MTO and ETO, it balances demand against a target backlog or quoted lead time. Mixing these logics in one family is a common reason for confusing S&OP numbers.

## History: from aggregate planning and Orlicky to IBP and DDMRP

### Aggregate planning and the first MRP systems (1950s–1960s)

S&OP began in the early 1950s as aggregate production planning: matching an aggregated production rate to forecast demand by trading off inventory, workforce changes and overtime [1]. At about the same time, aero engine makers Rolls-Royce and General Electric computerized material planning [3].

In 1964, Joseph Orlicky, an engineer at IBM, formalized material requirements planning [4]. The first company to implement it was Black & Decker in 1964, with Dick Alban as project leader [3]. Orlicky's book *Material Requirements Planning* (McGraw-Hill, 1975) became the standard text [13]. Its third edition, a 2011 update by Carol Ptak and Chad Smith, introduced demand driven MRP (DDMRP) [4].

Adoption grew quickly: about 700 companies ran MRP by 1975 and about 8,000 by 1981, according to figures cited in Wikipedia [3]. In 1967, George Plossl and Oliver Wight, an IBM colleague of Orlicky's, co-wrote a book on production and inventory control [4].

### MRP II, ERP and S&OP (1980s–1990s)

In 1983, Oliver Wight extended MRP into **manufacturing resource planning (MRP II)** by adding master scheduling, rough-cut capacity planning, capacity requirements planning, S&OP and the link to financial planning [3]. Oliver Wight's firm describes S&OP as developed by Oliver Wight in the 1980s [2]; Wikipedia attributes the term and its modern meaning to Richard (Dick) Ling, then an Oliver Wight consultant [1]. Gartner coined the term "enterprise resource planning" (ERP) in 1990 [4]. ERP integrated finance, sales, purchasing and HR around a planning core that remained MRP II.

### IBP, DDMRP and concurrent planning (2000s–today)

In the early 2000s Oliver Wight coined **integrated business planning (IBP)**, which extends S&OP to the whole enterprise, including portfolio and financial planning, with a horizon of at least 24 months [2]. In 2011, Ptak and Smith published the third edition of Orlicky's book with **demand driven MRP (DDMRP)** [4], and ERP vendors such as Microsoft have since built DDMRP into their planning engines [11]. Planning software vendors now promote "concurrent" or continuous planning that recalculates plans in minutes rather than in a monthly batch [16]; that claim is a vendor claim and is discussed in the AI section.

## The S&OP process: the monthly cycle step by step

The most cited academic description of the S&OP process has five steps: data gathering, demand planning, supply planning, pre-meeting and executive meeting [1][20]. Some authors add an event or product review at the start and a revision step at the end [1]; Anaplan, a planning software vendor, describes six reviews: product, demand, supply, finance, pre-S&OP and executive S&OP [15]. Kinaxis describes a traditional monthly cycle with five stages [16]. The differences are mostly labels: the cycles cover the same ground in roughly the same order.

The following calendar assumes the month closes on the last working day and the executive meeting takes place in the third week.

| Working day | Step | Owner | Inputs | Outputs | Meeting length |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1–3 | 1. Data gathering and portfolio update | demand planner, product management | last month's actuals (shipments, orders, production, inventory), new product and phase-out dates | updated history, KPI pack, product changes | none (desk work) |
| 4–6 | 2. Demand review | VP sales or marketing; demand planner prepares | statistical forecast, sales input, marketing events, key customer plans | unconstrained consensus demand plan by family, in units and dollars, with assumptions and risks | 60–90 minutes |
| 7–9 | 3. Supply review | VP operations or supply chain; master scheduler and supply planner prepare | consensus demand, inventory or backlog targets, capacity by key resource, supplier constraints | constrained supply plan, capacity issues and options with costs | 60–90 minutes |
| 10–12 | 4. Pre-S&OP (reconciliation) meeting | S&OP process owner; finance analyst | demand and supply plans, gaps, financial plan | reconciled plan in units and dollars, decisions prepared with options, open issues | 90–120 minutes |
| 13–15 | 5. Executive S&OP meeting | general manager | pre-S&OP recommendations, KPIs | approved plan, decisions, escalations closed | 60–120 minutes |
| 16–20 | Rollout | master scheduler, planners | approved family plan | MPS updated, MRP run, purchase and capacity actions | none |

### Step 1: Data gathering and portfolio update

The cycle starts as soon as the month closes. The demand planner pulls shipments, incoming orders, production, inventory and backlog by family and SKU, cleans the history of one-off events (a fire at a customer, a one-time tender) and runs the statistical forecast. Product management updates launch and phase-out dates. A forecast that does not know a variant is being discontinued in month 4 will keep ordering its parts.

**Checklist, step 1**

| Item | Done when |
|---|---|
| Actuals loaded | shipments, orders, production and inventory reconcile with finance month-end within agreed tolerance |
| History cleaned | outliers flagged with reason, not deleted |
| Statistical forecast run | model and parameters documented per family |
| Portfolio changes | launches, phase-outs, engineering changes with dates and owners |
| KPI pack | forecast accuracy and bias, plan attainment, service, inventory vs. target |

### Step 2: Demand review

The demand review produces the **unconstrained consensus demand plan**: what customers will want if supply were unlimited. Sales and marketing adjust the statistical forecast with information the history does not contain: price changes, promotions, a lost customer, a new distributor. Every adjustment is recorded with its owner and assumption. That record later shows whether manual overrides improved or worsened accuracy.

Typical errors at this step are forecasting in revenue only (operations cannot build dollars), forecasting at SKU level for 24 months (too much noise), and confusing the sales target with the forecast. A target is what the company wants; a forecast is what it expects. The gap between the two is a business issue for the executive meeting, not a number to hide in the forecast.

**Demand review agenda (60–90 minutes)**

| Time | Topic | Lead |
|---|---|---|
| 10 min | last month: forecast accuracy and bias by family, top misses with root cause | demand planner |
| 20 min | statistical baseline vs. last consensus, changes above threshold | demand planner |
| 30 min | sales and marketing inputs by family: events, customers, pricing | sales, marketing |
| 15 min | risks and opportunities with probability and quantity | VP sales |
| 5 min | consensus by family, open items, owners | demand planner |

### Step 3: Supply review

The supply review tests the consensus demand against capacity, materials and inventory targets. Planners translate family volumes into load on **key resources** (bottleneck work centers, critical suppliers, skilled labor groups) using a bill of resources: hours per unit per resource. Where load exceeds capacity, they develop options with costs: overtime, an extra shift, outsourcing, prebuilding, a later delivery date, or accepting a lower service level. The supply review does not decide between options with significant financial impact; it prepares them for the pre-S&OP meeting. Section 5 shows how such options are compared.

**Supply review agenda (60–90 minutes)**

| Time | Topic | Lead |
|---|---|---|
| 10 min | last month: plan attainment, MPS adherence, supplier performance | master scheduler |
| 25 min | load vs. capacity by key resource and month (rough-cut) | supply planner |
| 20 min | inventory and backlog projection vs. targets by family | master scheduler |
| 20 min | constraints and options with cost and risk | VP operations |
| 5 min | constrained supply plan, issues for pre-S&OP | master scheduler |

### Step 4: Pre-S&OP (reconciliation) meeting

The pre-S&OP meeting brings demand, supply and finance together. Finance values the constrained plan at standard cost and selling price and compares it with the budget or latest financial forecast. The team resolves what it can within agreed decision rights and prepares the rest as decision papers: two or three options, each with volume, service, cost and working capital impact and a recommendation. A good decision paper fits on one page.

### Step 5: Executive S&OP meeting

The executive meeting reviews KPIs, approves the plan and makes the prepared decisions. It is not the place to rework numbers. A typical agenda:

| Time | Topic |
|---|---|
| 10 min | KPI review: service, forecast accuracy and bias, inventory and backlog vs. target, plan attainment |
| 15 min | business overview by family: demand plan vs. budget, supply plan vs. demand |
| 45 min | decisions: one decision paper at a time, options, recommendation, decision recorded |
| 10 min | risks for the next 3–6 months, new product status |
| 10 min | review of decision log, owners and dates |

### Rollout: from the approved plan to the MPS

Within a few days of the executive meeting, the master scheduler disaggregates the approved family plan into the MPS by end item and week (Section 8), checks it with rough-cut capacity planning (Section 9) and runs MRP (Section 10). Purchasing receives new or changed planned orders, and capacity decisions (a second shift, overtime) are released through HR and the plant. Without this rollout, S&OP becomes a monthly slide deck with no effect on the floor.

### Roles and decision rights

| Role | Responsibilities |
|---|---|
| Executive sponsor (GM or president) | chairs the executive meeting, owns final decisions, enforces attendance and decision discipline |
| S&OP process owner | runs the calendar, prepares the pre-S&OP, maintains decision and assumption logs |
| Demand planner | statistical forecast, consensus process, forecast KPIs |
| Sales and marketing leads | market intelligence, ownership of the consensus demand number |
| Master scheduler | MPS, ATP rules, rough-cut capacity, disaggregation |
| Supply and materials planners | MRP exceptions, supplier capacity, purchase orders |
| Plant or operations manager | capacity plan, labor, overtime and shift decisions |
| Finance partner | valuation in dollars, reconciliation with budget, working capital |
| Product management or engineering | launches, phase-outs, engineering changes |

Decision rights should be written down. Example: the supply review may approve overtime up to 10% of a work center's capacity; anything above, and any change to inventory targets, goes to the executive meeting.

## Supply strategies: level, chase or hybrid (worked example)

Aggregate planning offers three classic supply strategies, which S&OP guides name but rarely calculate [6]:

- **Level:** produce at a constant rate and let inventory absorb the demand swings.
- **Chase:** change the production rate every month to match demand, with overtime, temporary labor or shifts, and keep inventory flat.
- **Hybrid (mixed):** change the rate a few times and accept some inventory build.

**Worked example (illustrative numbers).** A desk manufacturer plans the family "steel-frame desks" for six months. Starting inventory is 800 units, target ending inventory is 800 units, and a minimum of 500 units must be on hand at every month end (safety stock). The previous production rate was 3,000 units per month. Welding is the bottleneck: 5 welding cells × 2 shifts × 8 hours × 20 days = 1,600 hours per month; each desk needs 0.4 welding hours, so the maximum rate is 1,600 / 0.4 = **4,000 desks per month**. Costs: holding $4 per unit per month on ending inventory; each unit of change in the monthly rate (hiring, training, overtime set-up, or layoff) costs $5.

| Month | Demand | Level: production | Level: ending inventory | Chase: production | Chase: ending inventory | Hybrid: production | Hybrid: ending inventory |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Jan | 3,000 | 3,800 | 1,600 | 3,000 | 800 | 3,500 | 1,300 |
| Feb | 3,200 | 3,800 | 2,200 | 3,200 | 800 | 3,500 | 1,600 |
| Mar | 4,200 | 3,800 | 1,800 | **4,200** | 800 | 4,000 | 1,400 |
| Apr | 4,800 | 3,800 | 800 | **4,800** | 800 | 4,000 | 600 |
| May | 4,000 | 3,800 | 600 | 4,000 | 800 | 4,000 | 600 |
| Jun | 2,800 | 3,800 | 1,600 | 2,800 | 800 | 3,000 | 800 |
| **Total** | **22,000** | **22,800** | **8,600 unit-months** | **22,000** | **4,800 unit-months** | **22,000** | **6,300 unit-months** |

Why the level rate is 3,800: cumulative demand through May is 19,200 units; with 500 units required at the end of May and 800 on hand at the start, production through May must be at least 19,200 + 500 − 800 = 18,900, or 3,780 per month. Rounded to 3,800, the level plan ends June with 1,600 units, 800 above target.

| Cost element | Level | Chase | Hybrid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rate changes (units × $5) | 3,000 → 3,800: 800 → $4,000 | +200, +1,000, +600, −800, −1,200 = 3,800 → $19,000 | +500, +500, −1,000 = 2,000 → $10,000 |
| Holding (unit-months × $4) | 8,600 → $34,400 | 4,800 → $19,200 | 6,300 → $25,200 |
| **Total** | **$38,400** | **$38,200** | **$35,200** |
| Feasible at the welding bottleneck (≤ 4,000/month)? | yes | **no** (March 4,200, April 4,800) | yes |
| Ending inventory vs. target 800 | +800 | on target | on target |

The chase plan looks almost as cheap as the level plan on paper, but it is infeasible: March and April exceed welding capacity unless the company adds a third shift, which would bring its own cost. The hybrid plan is cheapest among the feasible options and runs welding at exactly 100% for three months, which leaves no protective capacity. Rate changes are not only a cost line. A chase plan means hiring, temporary labor, layoffs or sustained overtime; union contracts, overtime notice rules and fatigue limit how fast a plant can change its rate, and operators carry the cost of every swing. The supply review should ask the plant and HR what is realistic before modeling it. Finance will add a second effect: under standard costing, months with low production absorb less fixed overhead, so a chase plan creates absorption variances that a level plan avoids.

That is the decision the supply review should bring to the executive meeting: approve the hybrid plan, and decide whether to qualify a welding subcontractor for 200–400 desks a month as insurance. Running a bottleneck at full utilization drives queues and lead times up sharply, as the [APS guide's section on factory physics](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/production-scheduling-aps/) shows with Little's Law and operating curves, and the [theory of constraints guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/theory-of-constraints/) (planned) explains how to protect the drum.

## S&OP vs. IBP vs. S&OE

The three terms describe different scopes and horizons.

| Aspect | S&OP | Integrated business planning (IBP) | Sales and operations execution (S&OE) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Question | Do demand and supply match by family? | Does the whole business plan (portfolio, demand, supply, financials, strategy) hold together? | What do we change this week to execute the plan? |
| Horizon | Oliver Wight: typically 6–12 months; SAP and others: 18–36 months rolling [2][5] | at least 24 months [2] | 0–3 months (weeks) [unverified: horizon as commonly used by practitioners] |
| Cadence | monthly | monthly | weekly |
| Units | units and dollars by family | mainly dollars, plus units | units by SKU and customer |
| Chair | GM or VP operations | CEO or business unit president | supply chain or planning manager |
| Origin | Oliver Wight, 1980s [1][2] | Oliver Wight, early 2000s [2] | practitioner term, no single origin verified |

Oliver Wight describes S&OP as centered on supply chain trade-offs and IBP as bringing the entire enterprise into view across portfolio, demand, supply and financials [2]. Many companies use the terms interchangeably. The label matters less than two facts: whether finance and the portfolio are part of the process, and whether the executive team makes decisions in it. S&OE is the weekly bridge between the approved plan and the MPS: it handles short-term demand changes, supply disruptions and allocation within the frozen and slushy zones of the MPS (Section 8).

**S&OE weekly agenda (30–45 minutes, illustrative)**

| Time | Topic | Lead |
|---|---|---|
| 5 min | last week: MPS adherence, shipments vs. plan, top three misses | master scheduler |
| 10 min | demand changes inside the planning time fence: new large orders, cancellations, allocation of short supply | demand planner, customer service |
| 10 min | supply issues: supplier delays, machine outages, quality holds, material shortages from MRP exceptions | supply planner, plant |
| 10 min | decisions within the MPS rules; anything outside the rules is escalated to the next S&OP step | supply chain manager |
| 5 min | actions, owners, dates | master scheduler |

## S&OP maturity models

Maturity models help a company see where it stands and what the next step is. Three are widely used.

**Gartner.** Gartner publishes a five-level S&OP maturity roadmap [17]. Its public overview page does not list the level names; secondary sources describe the levels roughly as follows, from no shared goals and spreadsheet-based, uncoordinated planning (level 1) through operational sales planning, demand and supply balancing across the end-to-end supply chain, and a demand-oriented, profitable product mix, to a connected company in which S&OP is part of overall business planning and decisions are scenario-based (level 5) [18]. [unverified: Gartner's own level names; the descriptions above follow Jedox's summary, a planning software vendor.]

**Grimson and Pyke (2007).** J. Andrew Grimson and David Pyke proposed a framework with five dimensions and five stages: no S&OP process, reactive, standard, advanced and proactive [1][19].

**Wagner, Ullrich and Transchel (2014).** Stephan Wagner and colleagues describe four dimensions and six stages from undeveloped to proactive, together with the five-step process used in Section 4 [1][20].

A simple self-assessment, derived from the dimensions these models share (meetings and collaboration, organization, measurement, information technology, plan integration):

| Dimension | Stage 1: none or reactive | Stage 2: standard | Stage 3: advanced | Stage 4: proactive |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Meetings | ad hoc firefighting | monthly cycle, executive attends sometimes | full five-step cycle, executive chairs every month | cycle includes scenarios and strategy, executives decide on options |
| Organization | no owner | part-time coordinator | dedicated process owner, written decision rights | S&OP is how the business is run |
| Measurement | none | service and inventory | forecast accuracy and bias, plan attainment | KPIs linked to financial results and incentives |
| IT | separate spreadsheets | shared spreadsheet or ERP reports | integrated planning tool, family/SKU reconciliation | scenario planning, automated data flow |
| Plan integration | sales and production plans differ | one volume plan | volume plan reconciled with financial plan | single plan drives budget, MPS and capex |

The research evidence on S&OP effects is supportive but not uniform. A literature review by Antônio Márcio Tavares Thomé and co-authors in the *International Journal of Production Economics* synthesized the S&OP literature up to 2012 [22], and a companion study linked S&OP to firm performance [23]. Later work stresses that the right S&OP design depends on context, such as company size, product complexity and supply chain uncertainty [34]. Claims of fixed percentage improvements from S&OP on vendor pages should be read as vendor claims.

## Master production schedule (MPS) and available to promise (ATP)

The **master production schedule** is a statement of what the company expects to produce, based on the tactical plan and budget constraints, as Oracle's JD Edwards documentation puts it [7]. It sits between S&OP and MRP: it is specific enough (end item, week) to drive material and capacity planning, and stable enough to protect the plant from daily noise. MRP then calculates the time-phased net requirements for every component to support the MPS [7].

### Disaggregation: from family to end item

The approved S&OP plan for "steel-frame desks" in April is 4,000 units. The master scheduler splits it by end item using the current mix and known orders: 55% D100 (2,200), 30% D120 (1,200) and 15% D140 (600), then by week. Planning bills of material are used where options are planned as percentages, for example 60% black frames and 40% white.

### Time fences

The MPS is divided into zones with different change rules. Typical zone names and rules (exact definitions and zone lengths differ by company and ERP):

| Zone | Typical extent | What may change | Who approves |
|---|---|---|---|
| Frozen | from today to the demand time fence (for example final assembly lead time) | nothing, except emergencies | plant manager or S&OE meeting |
| Slushy | from the demand time fence to the planning time fence (for example cumulative lead time of long-lead purchased parts) | mix within the family; quantities within agreed tolerance | master scheduler |
| Liquid | beyond the planning time fence | anything consistent with S&OP | planning system and master scheduler |

The frozen zone is what Gershwin's MIT slides mean by freezing the near-term schedule: it reduces nervousness at a cost in flexibility [8]. Section 12 quantifies why.

### MPS record with projected available balance and ATP

**Worked example (illustrative numbers).** End item D100, on hand 60 units, MPS lot size 200, safety stock 0, demand time fence at the end of week 2. Inside the demand time fence, the projected demand is customer orders only; outside, it is the greater of forecast and customer orders.

| Week | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Forecast | 100 | 100 | 100 | 100 | 100 | 100 | 100 | 100 |
| Customer orders | 90 | 70 | 40 | 20 | 10 | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| Projected demand | 90 | 70 | 100 | 100 | 100 | 100 | 100 | 100 |
| MPS (completions) | 200 | | | 200 | | 200 | | 200 |
| Projected available balance (PAB), start 60 | 170 | 100 | 0 | 100 | 0 | 100 | 0 | 100 |
| Discrete ATP | 60 | | | 170 | | 200 | | 200 |
| Cumulative ATP | 60 | 60 | 60 | 230 | 230 | 430 | 430 | 630 |

How the numbers come about:

- **PAB, week 1:** 60 + 200 − 90 = 170. Week 2: 170 − 70 = 100. Week 3: 100 − 100 = 0. Week 4 would go to −100, so the MPS plans a lot of 200: 0 + 200 − 100 = 100. And so on.
- **Discrete ATP** is calculated only in weeks with an MPS receipt: on hand (first period only) plus the MPS quantity minus all customer orders until the next MPS receipt. Week 1: 60 + 200 − (90 + 70 + 40) = 60. Week 4: 200 − (20 + 10) = 170. Weeks 6 and 8: 200 − 0 = 200.
- **Cumulative ATP** adds up the discrete ATP over time: 60, then 60 + 170 = 230, then 430, then 630.
- Some ERP systems use a look-ahead variant: if customer orders in a later period exceed that period's MPS receipt, the shortfall is deducted from the ATP of earlier periods so that the same units are not promised twice. In this example no period is oversold, so both methods give the same result.

What sales can promise: a customer who wants 100 desks in week 2 can get at most 60 from the current plan; the full 100 are available from week 4 (cumulative ATP 230). The PAB of 0 in week 3 is not a problem for promising, because ATP uses customer orders, not the forecast. It is a signal for the master scheduler that the plan has no slack if week 3 orders exceed the forecast. ERP systems such as JD Edwards report ATP and cumulative ATP as standard MPS outputs [7].

### MPS rules that keep the schedule credible

| Rule | Why |
|---|---|
| The MPS sum per family stays within ±5% of the S&OP plan (example tolerance) | otherwise S&OP decisions do not reach the floor |
| No overloading: RCCP load ≤ demonstrated capacity at key resources | an overstated MPS makes ATP and MRP dates fictional |
| Changes inside the frozen zone require named approval | protects material and capacity already committed |
| Firm planned orders for the near term | the MRP run cannot move them automatically |
| ATP is checked before every order promise | prevents promising the same capacity twice |
| Weekly MPS review in the S&OE meeting | catches deviations before they become past-due orders |

## Rough-cut capacity planning (RCCP)

Rough-cut capacity planning checks the MPS against a few **critical resources** before MRP explodes it. It is "rough" because it uses a bill of resources (hours per end item at each key resource), not full routings, and ignores most lead time offsets.

**Worked example (illustrative numbers).** Weekly MPS for the three desk models and their bill of resources at three key resources:

| Model | MPS week 14 | Welding (h/unit) | Powder coating (h/unit) | Final assembly (h/unit) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| D100 | 550 | 0.40 | 0.10 | 0.25 |
| D120 | 300 | 0.45 | 0.12 | 0.30 |
| D140 | 150 | 0.60 | 0.15 | 0.40 |

| Resource | Load calculation | Load (h) | Weekly capacity (h) | Utilization |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Welding | 550 × 0.40 + 300 × 0.45 + 150 × 0.60 = 220 + 135 + 90 | 445 | 400 | **111%** |
| Powder coating | 550 × 0.10 + 300 × 0.12 + 150 × 0.15 = 55 + 36 + 22.5 | 113.5 | 160 | 71% |
| Final assembly | 550 × 0.25 + 300 × 0.30 + 150 × 0.40 = 137.5 + 90 + 60 | 287.5 | 320 | 90% |

Welding is overloaded by 45 hours. Options: move 75 D140 desks (75 × 0.60 = 45 h) to week 15 if week 15 has slack and the customer dates allow it, plan 45 hours of overtime, or shift some D100 frames to the subcontractor prepared in the supply review. The weekly welding capacity of 400 hours matches the 1,600 hours per month used in S&OP (Section 5), which is the point: RCCP uses the same resource definitions as the S&OP supply review, so the levels can be reconciled.

Three RCCP techniques are common in textbooks and ERP systems: capacity planning using overall factors (historical percentages of total hours per work center), bills of capacity or bills of resources (hours per unit per resource, as above), and resource profiles that add time offsets. The more detailed the method, the closer it gets to capacity requirements planning (Section 11).

## Material requirements planning (MRP): how the calculation works

**Material requirements planning (MRP)** uses the bill of materials and inventory records to calculate time-phased net requirements for every component and subassembly and suggests a replenishment plan that supports the MPS [7]. Its logic rests on one idea from Orlicky: demand for components is *dependent* on the demand for the end item, so it should be calculated, not forecast [13]. If the plant builds 150 desks in week 3 and each desk has four legs, it needs 600 legs before week 3, regardless of what a statistical forecast for legs would say.

### The inputs

| Input | Content | Typical data quality problem |
|---|---|---|
| Master production schedule | end items, quantities, dates | MPS not reconciled with S&OP, or overloaded |
| Bill of materials (BOM) | parent-component relationships, quantity per, scrap factors, effectivity dates | engineering BOM used instead of manufacturing BOM; missing phantom assemblies; outdated after engineering changes |
| Inventory records | on hand, allocated, safety stock | counts wrong; scrap and consumption not booked |
| Open orders (scheduled receipts) | released production and purchase orders with due dates | due dates not maintained; orders closed late |
| Item master planning data | lead time, lot-sizing rule, order multiples, minimums, yield | lead times set years ago and never reviewed |

Gershwin's MIT slides list the same building blocks: gross requirements, scheduled receipts, projected on hand, net requirements, planned order receipts and planned order releases, offset by lead time [8].

### The five steps for each item

1. **Gross requirements:** the MPS for end items, or the planned order releases of all parents multiplied by the quantity per, for components.
2. **Netting:** projected on hand = previous on hand + scheduled receipts − gross requirements. Where it would fall below safety stock, the shortfall is the **net requirement**.
3. **Lot sizing:** the net requirement becomes a **planned order receipt** according to the lot-sizing rule (lot-for-lot, fixed order quantity, multiples, period order quantity).
4. **Offsetting:** the planned order receipt is shifted back by the item's lead time to give the **planned order release**.
5. **Explosion:** the planned order releases become gross requirements of the next BOM level.

Items are processed level by level in **low-level code** order: each item gets a code equal to the lowest level at which it appears in any BOM, so that all gross requirements from all parents are collected before the item is netted [8]. The worked example shows why that matters.

### Worked example: MRP explosion and netting across two BOM levels

**Product structure (illustrative).** Desk D100 consists of one desktop T-20, one leg frame F-10 and 1 meter of steel tube S-5 for a cross brace. Each leg frame F-10 is welded from 4 meters of steel tube S-5. S-5 therefore appears at level 1 (directly in D100) and at level 2 (in F-10); its low-level code is 2.

```
D100 Desk (level 0)
├── T-20 Desktop, qty 1 (level 1, made in house, lead time 1 week)
├── F-10 Leg frame, qty 1 (level 1, welded in house, lead time 2 weeks)
│   └── S-5 Steel tube, 4 m (level 2, purchased, lead time 2 weeks)
└── S-5 Steel tube, 1 m (level 1, cross brace)
```

To keep the tables readable, the MPS row shows the week in which final assembly of D100 starts; components must be available at the start of that week.

**MPS for D100 (final assembly starts):**

| Week | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| D100 assembly starts | 120 | 0 | 150 | 100 | 0 | 150 | 100 | 120 | 740 |

**Item T-20 Desktop.** Lot-for-lot, lead time 1 week, on hand 30, scheduled receipt 100 in week 1, safety stock 0.

| Week | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Gross requirements | 120 | 0 | 150 | 100 | 0 | 150 | 100 | 120 |
| Scheduled receipts | 100 | | | | | | | |
| Projected on hand (start 30) | 10 | 10 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| Net requirements | | | 140 | 100 | | 150 | 100 | 120 |
| Planned order receipts | | | 140 | 100 | | 150 | 100 | 120 |
| Planned order releases | | 140 | 100 | | 150 | 100 | 120 | |

Week 1: 30 + 100 − 120 = 10. Week 3: 10 − 150 = −140, so 140 are needed and released one week earlier, in week 2.

**Item F-10 Leg frame.** Fixed order quantity 200 (a welding batch), lead time 2 weeks, on hand 120, no scheduled receipts, safety stock 0.

| Week | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Gross requirements | 120 | 0 | 150 | 100 | 0 | 150 | 100 | 120 |
| Projected on hand (start 120) | 0 | 0 | 50 | 150 | 150 | 0 | 100 | 180 |
| Net requirements | | | 150 | 50 | | | 100 | 20 |
| Planned order receipts | | | 200 | 200 | | | 200 | 200 |
| Planned order releases | 200 | 200 | | | 200 | 200 | | |

Week 3: 0 − 150 = −150, so one lot of 200 arrives, leaving 50. Week 4: 50 − 100 = −50, another lot of 200, leaving 150. Week 6 uses the remaining 150 exactly. Releases are offset by two weeks.

**Item S-5 Steel tube (low-level code 2).** Purchased, lead time 2 weeks, order in multiples of 500 m, on hand 1,400 m, scheduled receipt 500 m in week 1, safety stock 100 m. Gross requirements combine both parents:

| Week | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| From F-10 releases (× 4 m) | 800 | 800 | | | 800 | 800 | | |
| From D100 starts (× 1 m) | 120 | | 150 | 100 | | 150 | 100 | 120 |
| **Gross requirements** | **920** | **800** | **150** | **100** | **800** | **950** | **100** | **120** |
| Scheduled receipts | 500 | | | | | | | |
| Projected on hand (start 1,400) | 980 | 180 | 530 | 430 | 130 | 180 | 580 | 460 |
| Net requirements | | | 70 | | 470 | 920 | 20 | |
| Planned order receipts | | | 500 | | 500 | 1,000 | 500 | |
| Planned order releases | 500 | | 500 | 1,000 | 500 | | | |

Week 3: 180 − 150 = 30, below the safety stock of 100, so the net requirement is 70 and one multiple of 500 is ordered (projected on hand 530). Week 6: 130 − 950 = −820; to end at least at 100, the net requirement is 920, rounded up to 1,000.

**What the example shows**

- **Dependent demand is lumpy.** Desk demand is fairly smooth (100 to 150 a week), but tube demand jumps between 100 and 950 m a week because the welding lots of 200 frames each pull 800 m at once. A statistical forecast of tube consumption would miss those peaks; the BOM explosion does not.
- **Low-level coding prevents shortages.** Had S-5 been netted at level 1 (with only the cross-brace demand of 120 to 150 m a week), MRP would have seen no requirement until week 7 and the frames would have run out of tube in week 2.
- **Lot sizes multiply.** Two lot-sizing rules (200 frames, 500 m multiples) turned 740 desks into 2,500 m of tube orders in five weeks. The [lot sizing guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/production-scheduling-aps/lot-sizing/) (planned) explains how to choose those rules; the [manufacturing inventory management guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/production-scheduling-aps/manufacturing-inventory-management/) (planned) covers safety stock.
- **The ERP output is a list of actions:** release 200 frames and buy 500 m of tube this week; release 140 desktops next week. The planner works through these as **exception messages** (release, reschedule in, reschedule out, cancel) rather than reading the full tables.

### Pegging and exception messages

**Pegging** links each requirement to its source: the week 6 tube requirement of 950 m pegs to the F-10 release in week 6 (800 m) and the D100 start in week 6 (150 m). With pegging, a planner who cannot get 1,000 m of tube in week 6 sees which desks and which customer orders are at risk.

Typical MRP exception messages and what to do:

| Message | Meaning | Typical action |
|---|---|---|
| Release order | planned order is inside its release window | convert to production or purchase order after checking capacity and material |
| Reschedule in (expedite) | an open order is due later than needed | ask supplier or plant to pull in; if impossible, escalate to MPS change |
| Reschedule out (de-expedite) | an open order is due earlier than needed | push out to reduce inventory and free capacity |
| Cancel | an open order is no longer needed | cancel or reduce; check cancellation terms |
| Past due | release date or due date is already in the past | the plan is infeasible as stated: fix the MPS, not just the order |
| Below safety stock | projected on hand below safety level | check if safety stock is right before expediting |

A plant with hundreds of past-due planned orders after every MRP run does not have an MRP problem. It has an MPS that is not feasible, or lead times and lot sizes that do not reflect reality.

### MRP vs. reorder point and kanban

MRP is not the only way to replenish parts. Reorder point systems and kanban replenish when stock falls to a trigger level and work well for items with stable, high-frequency demand. MRP is better for lumpy dependent demand, long lead times, low volume and engineered products. Many plants use both: kanban for fasteners and fast-moving parts on the line, MRP for long-lead and expensive parts. The [kanban and pull system guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/kanban-pull-system/) (planned) and the [lean manufacturing guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/lean-manufacturing/) explain the pull side.

## MRP II, CRP and the closed loop

**Manufacturing resource planning (MRP II)** closes the loop around MRP: S&OP and the MPS above it, capacity requirements planning (CRP) and shop floor control below it, and the link to financial planning [3][8]. Oliver Wight described it in 1983 [3].

**Capacity requirements planning (CRP)** takes all planned and released orders from MRP, applies the full routings (setup and run time per operation and work center) and calculates load per work center and period. It shows overloads but does not resolve them: CRP, like MRP, rests on an infinite capacity assumption [8]. Wallace Hopp and Mark Spearman argue in *Factory Physics* that the basic MRP model assumes lead times that do not depend on load, which is only possible with unlimited capacity [9]. The APS guide shows a worked example of the overload MRP does not see and explains the **lead time syndrome**: planners lengthen planned lead times to cover late orders, which releases work earlier, raises WIP and makes lead times even longer [26]. Rather than repeat those sections, this guide links to them: see [the weaknesses of classic MRP logic](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/production-scheduling-aps/).

| Closed-loop element | Checks | Feedback if it fails |
|---|---|---|
| Resource planning in S&OP | aggregate capacity per month | change family plan, capacity decision |
| RCCP | key resources per week | change MPS |
| CRP | every work center per day or week | overtime, alternate routing, change MPS |
| Input/output control and finite scheduling | actual queues and sequence | reschedule, release control, escalate to MPS |
| Shop floor feedback (MES) | actual completions, scrap, downtime | update open orders and inventory for the next MRP run |

## MRP nervousness: why plans jump and how to calm them

**Nervousness** means that small changes in the MPS or in inventory cause large or frequent changes in the planned orders at lower levels. Gershwin's slides describe it as small deviations causing large schedule changes [8]. The causes are the interaction between lot sizing, lead time offsets and the rolling planning horizon, plus noise in demand, supply and inventory records.

### Worked example: 30 desks more in week 4

Sales accepts an extra order: the D100 assembly starts in week 4 increase from 100 to 130, a change of 4% of the eight-week volume (30 of 740). Rerunning the example above gives:

| Item | Before | After | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| T-20 Desktop, planned releases | wk 2: 140, wk 3: 100, wk 5: 150, wk 6: 100, wk 7: 120 | wk 2: 140, wk 3: **130**, wk 5: 150, wk 6: 100, wk 7: 120 | quantity +30 |
| F-10 Leg frame, planned releases | wk 1, 2, **5**, 6 (200 each) | wk 1, 2, **4**, 6 (200 each) | one welding lot pulled in by a week |
| S-5 Steel tube, planned releases | wk 1: 500, **wk 3: 500**, wk 4: 1,000, wk 5: 500 | wk 1: 500, **wk 2: 500**, wk 4: 1,000, wk 5: 500 | one purchase order pulled in by a week, now due for release next week |

The extra 30 units did not change any quantity of tube or frames. They changed **dates** at two lower levels, because the fixed lot of 200 frames now runs out a week earlier. In a real BOM with hundreds of components, a handful of such changes every day produce thousands of reschedule messages, and planners stop trusting the system. That is nervousness.

### Remedies

Joseph Blackburn, Dean Kropp and Robert Millen compared five strategies to dampen nervousness in a simulation study published in *Management Science* in 1986: freezing the schedule within the planning horizon, lot-for-lot below the end item, safety stock, forecasting beyond the planning horizon, and a change-cost procedure [10]. For nervousness caused by the rolling horizon and lot sizing, safety stock and lot-for-lot were not cost-effective, while the change-cost and freezing strategies generally performed best [10]. The study did not cover nervousness caused by demand, supply or lead time uncertainty [10].

| Remedy | How it works | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| Frozen zone and time fences | no automatic changes inside the frozen zone | less responsiveness to real changes |
| Firm planned orders | the planner fixes selected orders; MRP does not move them | planner must maintain them |
| Change-cost or "dampening" rules | MRP only proposes a reschedule if the date change exceeds a threshold (for example 3 days) or the cost of change is worth it | small mismatches remain |
| Lot-for-lot at lower levels | removes lot-size interaction | more setups and orders; Blackburn et al. found it not cost-effective for horizon-driven nervousness [10] |
| Safety stock | absorbs small changes | inventory cost; also not cost-effective in that study [10] |
| Accurate data | fewer artificial changes from wrong inventory or BOMs | requires cycle counting and engineering change discipline |
| Decoupling with buffers (DDMRP) | breaks the dependency chain at selected points | buffer inventory, new method to learn |

## Demand driven MRP (DDMRP)

**Demand driven material requirements planning (DDMRP)** keeps the BOM logic of MRP but stops the explosion of forecast-driven requirements at selected **decoupling points**, where it places sized, actively managed buffers. Supply orders are generated from actual demand and the buffer status rather than from the forecast alone. The method comes from consultants Carol Ptak and Chad Smith: their 2011 update of Orlicky's book, *Orlicky's Material Requirements Planning, Third Edition*, adds guidance on planning from actual sales orders rather than forecasts under the name DDMRP [4]. TechTarget notes that some practitioners view the approach as controversial [4]. Ptak and Smith also founded the Demand Driven Institute in 2011; it evaluates and certifies software for DDMRP compliance free of charge to vendors and awards professional endorsement certificates to individuals [35].

### The five components

Microsoft's documentation for Dynamics 365 Supply Chain Management lists the five components of DDMRP [11]:

1. **Strategic inventory positioning:** identify decoupling points in the supply chain where an inventory buffer is held, monitored and replenished.
2. **Buffer profiles and levels:** size each buffer (minimum, maximum, reorder point) and the reorder quantity.
3. **Dynamic buffer adjustments:** adjust buffer levels for changing operating parameters or planned events.
4. **Demand-driven planning:** generate supply orders (manufacturing, purchase, transfer) as required.
5. **Highly collaborative and visible execution:** run supply orders with visual priority, typically by buffer color.

### Buffer sizing: worked example

Each buffer has three zones, calculated from average daily usage (ADU), decoupled lead time (DLT), a lead time factor and a variability factor, as documented by Microsoft [12]:

| Zone | Formula [12] |
|---|---|
| Yellow | ADU × DLT |
| Red base | ADU × DLT × lead time factor |
| Red safety | red base × variability factor |
| Red | red base + red safety |
| Green | the largest of: minimum order quantity; ADU × order cycle; ADU × DLT × lead time factor |

Microsoft recommends lead time factors of 0.20–0.40 for long, 0.41–0.60 for medium and 0.61–1.00 for short lead times, and variability factors of 0.20–0.40 for low, 0.41–0.60 for medium and 0.61–1.00 for high variability [12]. **Decoupled lead time** is the time needed to produce or receive an item if the upstream decoupling points are in stock; it is usually much shorter than the cumulative lead time [12].

**Example (illustrative numbers).** A purchased bracket B-7: ADU 40 pieces, decoupled lead time 10 days, lead time factor 0.5 (medium), variability factor 0.5 (medium), minimum order quantity 300, order cycle 7 days.

| Zone | Calculation | Size | Cumulative (top of zone) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Red base | 40 × 10 × 0.5 | 200 | |
| Red safety | 200 × 0.5 | 100 | |
| Red | 200 + 100 | 300 | 300 |
| Yellow | 40 × 10 | 400 | 700 |
| Green | max(300; 40 × 7 = 280; 200) | 300 | 1,000 |

### The net flow equation

DDMRP decides on replenishment with the **net flow position**: on hand + on order − qualified sales order demand [14]. Qualified demand typically includes orders due today, past-due orders and qualified spikes inside a defined horizon. If the net flow position falls below the top of yellow, a supply order is created to bring it back to the top of green [14].

Continuing the example: on hand 350, on order 400, qualified demand 90. Net flow position = 350 + 400 − 90 = **660**. That is below the top of yellow (700), so DDMRP proposes an order of 1,000 − 660 = **340** pieces. The order exceeds the minimum order quantity of 300, so it stands. Execution priority is then set by on-hand status: on hand of 350 is just above the top of red (300), which a planner would treat as urgent.

### When DDMRP fits and when it does not

| Situation | DDMRP fit | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Long cumulative lead times, customers expect short lead times | good | decoupling compresses the lead time the customer sees |
| Many shared components, high variability, frequent MRP nervousness | good | buffers absorb variability instead of passing it down the BOM |
| Stable, forecastable demand, short BOMs | limited | classic MRP or kanban may be simpler |
| One-off engineer-to-order projects | limited | few repeating items to buffer; project planning dominates |
| Weak inventory accuracy and master data | poor | buffers sized on wrong ADU or lead times protect the wrong items |

DDMRP is not a replacement for S&OP. Buffer policies, capacity and inventory investment still need a monthly cross-functional decision, and the ERP still needs the MPS for end items that are not buffered. In Dynamics 365, DDMRP is part of the master planning module, uses its own coverage code, comes without additional license fees and requires the Planning Optimization add-in; Microsoft stresses that it does not replace existing planning functionality [11]. SAP lists DDMRP among the topics of its MRP material [27], and IFS documents DDMRP in IFS Cloud [14]. Independent, peer-reviewed evidence on DDMRP results is still thinner than vendor and consultancy case material; treat improvement percentages from DDMRP vendors as vendor claims.

### How ERP runs MRP

Most ERP systems offer two run modes: a **regenerative** run that recalculates all items from scratch, often nightly or weekly, and a **net change** run that only recalculates items whose demand, supply or master data changed. Either way, the plan is only as current as the data the run sees: if the MES posts completions and scrap once per shift but MRP runs at 2 a.m., the morning exception list reflects yesterday's floor. Align the MRP run time with the MES and inventory posting cycle, and keep the MPS stable during the run so that planners and the system do not work against each other. The [MES and ISA-95 guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/mes-isa-95/) (planned) covers the data exchange between Level 3 and Level 4.

### Where ERP planning stops

- **Finite capacity.** ERP MRP and CRP plan against infinite capacity [8]. Where a bottleneck decides delivery dates, a finite scheduler or APS is needed; see the [APS guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/production-scheduling-aps/).
- **Scenarios.** Comparing three supply options with full material and capacity effects is slow in most ERP MRP runs. Planning platforms advertise fast scenario recalculation; Kinaxis, for example, describes concurrent planning that shrinks cycles from weeks to hours (vendor claim) [16].
- **Disruption response.** In an IDC survey commissioned by Kinaxis (1,800 respondents in nine countries, fielded December 2023), 83% of supply chains could not respond to disruptions within 24 hours (vendor-commissioned research) [30].
- **Execution.** Dispatching, confirmations and real-time WIP belong to MES (ISA-95 Level 3) [25].

## S&OP and MRP KPIs with worked examples

A good S&OP KPI pack is short: about ten measures, each with a definition, an owner and a target. The [manufacturing KPIs guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/manufacturing-kpis/) covers plant-level measures such as OEE and on-time delivery; this section covers planning measures.

### Forecast accuracy, bias and MAPE: one data set, five measures

**Data (illustrative).** Monthly forecast (F, made one month ahead) and actual demand (A) for the desk family:

| Month | Forecast F | Actual A | Error e = F − A | Absolute error \|e\| | Absolute percentage error \|e\| / A |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Jan | 3,000 | 2,850 | +150 | 150 | 5.26% |
| Feb | 3,200 | 3,350 | −150 | 150 | 4.48% |
| Mar | 4,200 | 3,900 | +300 | 300 | 7.69% |
| Apr | 4,800 | 5,100 | −300 | 300 | 5.88% |
| May | 4,000 | 3,700 | +300 | 300 | 8.11% |
| Jun | 2,800 | 2,650 | +150 | 150 | 5.66% |
| **Sum** | **22,000** | **21,550** | **+450** | **1,350** | **37.08%** |

| Measure | Formula | Calculation | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| MAPE (mean absolute percentage error) | average of \|e\| / A | 37.08% / 6 | **6.18%** |
| WMAPE (weighted MAPE) | Σ\|e\| / ΣA | 1,350 / 21,550 | **6.26%** |
| Forecast accuracy | 1 − WMAPE | 1 − 0.0626 | **93.7%** |
| Bias | Σe / ΣA | +450 / 21,550 | **+2.1% (over-forecast)** |
| MAD (mean absolute deviation) | Σ\|e\| / n | 1,350 / 6 | **225 units** |
| Tracking signal | Σe / MAD | 450 / 225 | **+2.0** |

How to read it:

- **Accuracy and bias answer different questions.** 93.7% accuracy sounds good. But four of six months were over-forecast, and the cumulative error is +450 units. If supply follows the forecast, inventory grows by about 450 units in six months. Bias is the measure that shows systematic optimism or sandbagging.
- **MAPE vs. WMAPE.** MAPE weights every month equally; WMAPE weights by volume. At SKU level, MAPE explodes for small items: a SKU with forecast 10 and actual 2 has an error of 400%. Use WMAPE (or MAD/mean) for reporting at SKU level and keep MAPE for stable, high-volume series.
- **Tracking signal.** The cumulative error divided by MAD. A value that drifts steadily away from zero indicates bias; a common rule of thumb treats the model as working correctly while the tracking signal stays between −4 and +4 [36].
- **Aggregation hides error.** Two SKUs with forecasts of 1,000 each and actuals of 1,200 and 800 give a family error of 0 but a SKU-level WMAPE of 400 / 2,000 = 20%. Report accuracy at the level where decisions are made: family for S&OP, SKU and location for the MPS and replenishment.
- **Lag matters.** Measure the forecast that was frozen at the lead time that counts (for example, the forecast made three months before the month for a part with a three-month lead time), not the last-minute update.

### Forecast value added

Forecast value added (FVA) compares the error of each step in the forecasting process with the step before. If the statistical forecast had a WMAPE of 7.5% and the consensus forecast after sales adjustments has 6.3%, the consensus process added 1.2 percentage points of accuracy. If it had 8.5%, the meeting made the forecast worse. FVA turns the demand review from an opinion contest into a measured process.

### Financial view: what S&OP does for working capital

Finance usually asks two questions: how much inventory does the plan carry, and what does it cost? With the hybrid plan from Section 5, average month-end inventory is 6,300 / 6 = 1,050 desks. At a standard cost of $180 per desk (illustrative), that is $189,000 tied up on average. The level plan carries 8,600 / 6 = 1,433 desks, or $258,000. | Phase | Months | Main activities | Exit criteria |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0. Assess | 0–1 | maturity self-assessment (Section 7), data inventory, interviews, sponsor commitment | sponsor named, scope agreed, baseline KPIs measured |
| 1. Design | 1–2 | product families, key resources, bill of resources, calendar, decision rights, KPI definitions, templates | design document signed off by sponsor |
| 2. Data foundation | 1–4 (parallel) | inventory accuracy (cycle counting), BOM audit incl. engineering change effectivity, lead time review, MPS rules and time fences in ERP | inventory accuracy and BOM accuracy measured and improving; lead times reviewed for top 80% of value |
| 3. First cycles | 3–5 | run the five-step cycle with spreadsheets; three cycles in a row with executive attendance | three cycles completed on schedule, decision log in use |
| 4. Connect to MPS and MRP | 4–7 | disaggregation rules, MPS reconciled with S&OP, RCCP at key resources, MRP exception discipline | MPS within tolerance of S&OP; past-due planned orders falling |
| 5. Measure and improve | 6–9 | forecast accuracy and bias, FVA, plan attainment; root causes of misses; nervousness rules | KPIs reported monthly; at least one process change from FVA results |
| 6. Extend | 9–12 | finance integration (IBP direction), scenarios, S&OE weekly meeting, tool evaluation if spreadsheets limit the process | plan in units and dollars; S&OE running; tool decision made on evidence |

**Practical notes**

- **Start with spreadsheets.** A tool cannot fix a process that does not exist yet. Most companies learn what they need from software only after three to six cycles.
- **Pilot one business unit or plant.** S&OP across five plants and twenty families on day one rarely survives the second month.
- **Data work runs in parallel.** S&OP decisions are only as good as the MPS and MRP below them; inventory and BOM accuracy determine whether MRP output can be trusted.
- **Quality and configuration control.** In regulated or certified environments (aerospace, automotive, medical), BOM changes are also a quality-system matter: an engineering change must reach the MRP BOM with the right effectivity date, or MRP will order parts for a superseded revision. Involve the quality lead in the BOM audit; the [aerospace production planning guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/aerospace-production-planning/) covers configuration management.
- **Change management.** Sales teams fear being held to a forecast; plants fear being blamed for capacity they never had. Make bias visible without blaming people, and celebrate decisions made early.
- **Training.** Planners and master schedulers benefit from structured training in MPC fundamentals; the ASCM CPIM program is the common US credential [24].

## Limits of S&OP and MRP

**S&OP is a monthly process in a world that changes weekly.** A plan approved on the 15th may be outdated by the 25th. S&OE and event-driven S&OP updates address this, but they need discipline to avoid replanning everything every week.

**Aggregation hides problems.** A family plan can be feasible while a specific variant is short of a specific component. That is why the MPS and MRP below S&OP matter, and why family definitions should group items that share resources.

**MRP is deterministic; reality is not.** MRP treats forecasts, lead times and yields as fixed numbers [8]. Safety stock and safety lead time cover some variation, but MRP cannot represent uncertainty explicitly. DDMRP, stochastic models and simulation (see [manufacturing simulation](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/digital-twin-manufacturing/manufacturing-simulation/), planned) address that from different angles.

**MRP plans against infinite capacity.** MRP and CRP show overloads but do not resolve them [8][9]. Finite scheduling and APS fill that gap at the operational level.

**MRP fixes lead times that actually depend on load.** Planned lead times are inputs to MRP but outcomes of the shop's workload. The lead time syndrome shows what happens when planners treat them as levers [26].

**Data dependency.** MRP amplifies data errors: a wrong quantity per in a BOM at level 2 multiplies through every parent. No algorithm compensates for wrong inventory records.

**Culture.** The academic literature lists organizational barriers, such as functional silos and missing executive commitment, among the main reasons S&OP initiatives stall [1]. Software does not remove them.

**Not every business needs the full stack.** A job shop with short lead times and mostly customer-specific orders may need a good order intake and finite scheduling more than a 24-month S&OP. A distributor may need DRP more than MRP. Match the method to the business.

## AI and digital planning: what works today

Planning software and ERP vendors now market machine learning forecasting, "demand sensing", planning agents and copilots. For a plant, the useful questions are which planning decision gets better and how the improvement is measured.

| Application | What it does | Where it helps | What to check |
|---|---|---|---|
| Machine learning forecasting | uses many drivers (price, promotions, weather, orders) beyond history | many SKUs with known demand drivers | measure FVA against a simple statistical baseline; ML often beats it for some families and not for others |
| Short-term demand sensing | adjusts the near-term forecast with recent orders and point-of-sale data | consumer goods, distribution | only useful if the MPS and S&OE can act on weekly changes |
| Scenario planning and fast recalculation | recomputes demand, capacity and material effects of a decision | supply review, pre-S&OP | calculation time, data latency, who can run scenarios [16] |
| Parameter tuning | suggests lead times, safety stocks, lot sizes, DDMRP buffer factors from actual data | MRP master data maintenance | planner reviews before changes; audit trail |
| Exception triage | ranks MRP exception messages by impact on customer orders | materials planning | acceptance rate of recommendations; time saved |
| Generative AI assistants | summarize S&OP packs, draft decision papers, answer "why did this plan change?" from planning data | preparation of meetings | answers must cite the data used; no autonomous plan changes without approval |
| Digital twin and simulation | tests capacity, buffer and layout options before deciding | supply review for capacity investments | model validation against actual performance; see the [digital twin guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/digital-twin-manufacturing/) |

Four rules keep AI useful in planning:

1. **Fix the process first.** An AI forecast fed into a process without a demand review, without bias measurement and without decision rights produces faster confusion.
2. **Measure value added.** Treat every model like any other forecast step and track its FVA (Section 16).
3. **Keep humans accountable for decisions.** S&OP decisions involve money, people and customers. Tools can prepare options; the executive team decides.
4. **Data foundation before models.** Most AI planning projects stall on history quality, master data and integration, not on algorithms. In Microsoft environments, platform choices such as Fabric and Power BI are covered in the [Azure for manufacturing guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/manufacturing-it-azure/) [31][32].

## FAQ: sales and operations planning and MRP

### What is sales and operations planning in simple terms?

S&OP is a monthly meeting cycle in which sales, operations, supply chain and finance agree on one plan for what the company will sell, make and stock over the next 18 to 36 months, by product family [1][5]. The executive team uses it to make trade-offs, such as adding a shift or building inventory before a shutdown.

### What are the steps of the S&OP process?

The common five steps are data gathering, demand review, supply review, pre-S&OP (reconciliation) and executive S&OP meeting [1][20]. Some companies add a product or portfolio review at the start; Anaplan, for example, lists product, demand, supply, finance, pre-S&OP and executive reviews [15].

### How often should S&OP be done?

Monthly, in a fixed calendar [1][5]; Anaplan, for example, describes monthly cycles with weekly touchpoints for key participants [15]. A weekly S&OE meeting handles short-term changes between cycles, and major events (a large order, a plant outage) can trigger an extra review.

### Who should attend the executive S&OP meeting?

The general manager or business unit president as chair, plus the heads of sales, marketing, operations, supply chain and finance, and product management or engineering where portfolio decisions are on the agenda. The S&OP process owner prepares and documents it.

### What is the difference between S&OP and IBP?

IBP extends S&OP from demand-supply balancing to the whole business plan, including portfolio, strategy and financials, with a horizon of at least 24 months [2]. Oliver Wight developed S&OP in the 1980s and coined IBP in the early 2000s [2].

### What is the difference between S&OP and MRP?

S&OP decides volumes by product family and month across functions. MRP calculates, for every component, how many to buy or make and when, from the master production schedule, BOMs and inventory [7]. S&OP sets the frame; MRP executes it at part level.

### What is the difference between MPS and MRP?

The MPS plans end items (or planning items) by week; MRP explodes the MPS through the BOMs to plan components and materials [7]. Both use the same netting logic, but the MPS is set by a master scheduler and MRP is calculated by the system.

### How does MRP work?

For each item, level by level: take gross requirements, subtract on-hand inventory and scheduled receipts, apply safety stock to get net requirements, size lots, offset by lead time to get planned order releases, and pass those down as gross requirements of the components [8]. Section 10 shows a full worked example.

### What are the inputs of MRP?

The master production schedule, the bills of materials, inventory records (on hand, safety stock), open orders (scheduled receipts) and item planning data (lead times, lot-sizing rules) [7][8].

### What is the difference between MRP and MRP II?

MRP calculates material requirements. MRP II, defined by Oliver Wight in 1983, adds S&OP, master scheduling, rough-cut and detailed capacity planning, shop floor control and the link to financial planning [3].

### What is the difference between MRP and ERP?

MRP is a planning method and an ERP module. ERP is an integrated system for finance, sales, purchasing, HR and manufacturing, with MRP as its planning core for materials. Gartner coined the term ERP in 1990 [4].

### What is DDMRP?

Demand driven MRP places inventory buffers at selected decoupling points, sizes them in red, yellow and green zones from average daily usage and lead time, and replenishes them with the net flow equation (on hand + on order − qualified demand) [11][12][14]. It is designed to reduce the forecast dependency and nervousness of classic MRP; independent evidence on results is still limited.

### What is available to promise?

ATP is the part of planned supply that is not yet committed to customer orders and can be promised to new ones. In the example in Section 8, a customer asking for 100 units in week 2 can get 60 at once and the rest from week 4.

### What KPIs measure S&OP success?

Forecast accuracy (1 − WMAPE) and bias at family level, supply plan attainment, MPS adherence, customer service (OTIF or fill rate), inventory or backlog against target, and the closure rate of decisions. Section 16 gives formulas and a worked example.

### How do you calculate forecast accuracy?

A common formula is 1 − WMAPE, where WMAPE = Σ|forecast − actual| / Σ actual. With absolute errors of 1,350 units on actual demand of 21,550, WMAPE is 6.26% and forecast accuracy 93.7%. Always report bias alongside it.

### Why do S&OP processes fail?

The usual reasons are missing executive participation, a forecast that is really a sales target, no finance involvement, too much detail (SKUs instead of families), no link to the MPS, and buying software before the process works. Section 18 lists fixes.

### Can a small manufacturer do S&OP without special software?

Yes. A company with a handful of product families can run S&OP with ERP extracts, Excel and a shared decision log. Software becomes worthwhile when reconciliation between families and SKUs, scenarios or data preparation take more time than the decisions.

### What is MRP nervousness?

Nervousness is the tendency of MRP plans to change frequently when inputs change slightly. In the example in Section 12, 30 extra desks moved orders at two lower BOM levels. Time fences, firm planned orders and change-cost rules reduce it [10].

### What are the benefits of S&OP?

S&OP replaces separate sales, production and finance numbers with one agreed plan per product family. Decisions that move cost from one function to another, such as a second shift, a prebuild or cutting a variant, are made by the people who own both the cost and the benefit (Section 1). The MPS and MRP receive a plan that has already been checked against capacity, so fewer orders have to be expedited later. Finance sees the working capital effect before it happens: in the illustrative example in Section 16, the hybrid plan carries about 380 fewer desks on average than the level plan, worth about $13,800 a year in carrying cost. Published benefit percentages from vendors and surveys vary widely, so measure your own baseline before the first cycle.

### What are the outputs of MRP?

For every made and purchased item, MRP produces time-phased planned order receipts and planned order releases, offset by lead time [8]. It also produces exception messages for open orders (release, reschedule in, reschedule out, cancel, past due, below safety stock) and pegging, which links each requirement to its parent item and customer order. Section 10 shows all three with a worked example.

### What is the difference between MRP and JIT?

MRP is a planning calculation: it explodes the master production schedule through the bills of material and creates dated orders before the need arises. Just-in-time (JIT) is a lean production approach that replenishes to actual consumption, usually with kanban signals, and aims for small lots and low inventory. The two are not mutually exclusive. Many plants use MRP for long-lead, expensive and lumpy-demand parts and kanban for fast-moving parts on the line (Section 10). The [kanban and pull system guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/kanban-pull-system/) (planned) covers the pull side.

## Conclusion and first steps

Sales and operations planning and MRP solve the same problem at two levels. S&OP lets the leadership team decide, once a month, how much to sell, make and stock by family, and what that costs. The master production schedule turns that decision into end items by week, rough-cut capacity planning checks it against the bottleneck, and MRP calculates what every part needs, when. Each level needs the one above it to be realistic and the one below it to be executed. Detailed scheduling and the shop floor complete the chain.

The methods are old (Orlicky formalized MRP in 1964, Oliver Wight shaped S&OP in the 1980s) and still the backbone of ERP planning [2][3][4]. Their weaknesses are known: infinite capacity, fixed lead times, nervousness and dependence on data. Time fences, RCCP, DDMRP buffers and finite scheduling address them. AI tools can speed up forecasting and scenario work, but only where the process, the data and the decision rights are already in place.
