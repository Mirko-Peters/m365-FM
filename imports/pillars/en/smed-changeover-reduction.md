# SMED: Single-Minute Exchange of Die and Changeover Time Reduction, Step by Step

*Updated: November 2026 · Reading time: about 45 minutes (quick start: short answer, "Key takeaways" box, the eight steps and the templates section)*

> **Short answer:** SMED (single-minute exchange of die) is a method for changing a machine or line over from one product to the next in as little time as possible, with a target of under ten minutes, a "single-digit" number of minutes. Its core idea comes from Shigeo Shingo: separate the setup work that can only be done while the machine is stopped (internal setup) from the work that can be done while it runs (external setup), convert as much internal work as possible to external, then streamline everything that remains [1][6].

> **Key takeaways**
> - Changeover time runs from the last good unit of the old run to the first good unit of the new run, at normal speed. Run-up and adjustment count as changeover time [2][21].
> - SMED works in a fixed order: observe, separate internal from external, convert internal to external, streamline internal, streamline external, standardize, repeat [5].
> - Most early gains come from organization (staging, checklists, carts, parallel work), not from expensive hardware [19][20].
> - A shorter changeover only pays off fully when you use the time: smaller lots, more frequent changeovers and a shorter EPEI, or more capacity at a bottleneck. Decide which before the project starts.
> - Lot size and inventory scale with changeover time. In the classic lot-size formula, cutting setup cost by 75 percent halves the economic lot size.
> - Every internal setup step happens on stopped equipment. In the US, setting up is "servicing" under OSHA's lockout/tagout standard unless a narrow exception applies [8][9].
> - Changeover gains fade without standard work, audits and a changeover KPI on the shop floor board. Research has criticized SMED practice for under-using equipment design and for weak sustainment [12][13].
> - Good KPIs: average changeover time and its spread, percentage of changeovers within standard, run-up scrap, changeovers per week, EPEI and days of inventory.

## What is SMED? Definition and core idea

The Lean Enterprise Institute (LEI) defines [SMED](https://www.m365.fm/entity/smed/) as a process for changing over production equipment from one part number to another in as little time as possible [1]. "Single-minute" does not mean one minute. It means a single-digit number of minutes, so under ten [1]. The term "die" comes from the stamping presses where the method was first applied, but SMED applies to any changeover: molds, fixtures, cutting tools, print plates, format parts on a packaging line, recipes on a filling machine.

A [changeover](https://www.m365.fm/entity/setup-time/) (also called a setup) is the act of switching a machine or a linked group of machines, such as a cell or an assembly line, from one product to another [2]. LEI measures changeover time from the moment the last unit of the completed run leaves the process until the first acceptable unit of the next run comes off [2]. That boundary matters, and we return to it in the measurement section.

### Internal setup and external setup

Shingo's key insight was to split every changeover into two kinds of work [1]:

| Type | Definition | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Internal setup** | Work that can only be done while the machine is stopped | Unclamping and removing the old die, mounting the new one, changing format parts inside the guard, first-piece adjustment |
| **External setup** | Work that can be done while the machine is still running the previous job or already running the next one | Fetching the new die, tools and material; preheating a mold; checking the new die for damage; returning the old die to storage; paperwork |

In an unimproved changeover, almost everything happens while the machine stands: the setter stops the machine and then goes looking for the die, the crane, the right wrench and the drawing. SMED moves the "looking" and "fetching" out of the stop time, then attacks the work that truly needs a stopped machine.

### SMED, quick changeover and setup reduction: the terms

The terms overlap, and US plants use them interchangeably:

- **SMED (single-minute exchange of die):** Shingo's name for the method and its goal of single-digit minutes [1][6].
- **Quick changeover (QCO):** the common US shop floor term for the same practice, used for instance by NIST MEP centers [18].
- **Setup reduction / setup time reduction:** a general label that covers SMED plus engineering changes to equipment.
- **OTED (one-touch exchange of die):** a stricter target, a changeover in under 100 seconds [5].
- **Changeover time reduction:** the outcome. SMED is the most widely used method for it, but not the only lever; product design, equipment design and scheduling contribute too [13][15].

### The core idea in one sentence

Do not stop the machine for anything that could be done while it runs, and make whatever must happen during the stop so simple that it needs no searching, no adjusting and no trial parts.

In practice, the SMED approach separates setup tasks that need the machine stopped from those that can be performed while the machine is still running, moves as much work as possible to the second group, and then shortens what is left. That makes SMED a continuous improvement method for the changeover process, and one of the standard tools of lean manufacturing.

### Benefits of SMED

Competitor pages list benefits with percentages that rarely carry a source. The benefits below are the mechanisms; the size of each depends on your starting point.

1. **Capacity:** every minute removed from internal setup returns a minute of machine time. At a bottleneck, that is output the whole plant gains.
2. **Smaller lots and less inventory:** when a changeover is cheap, you can run each product more often in smaller quantities. LEI notes that a machine's EPEx (every product every interval) depends directly on its changeover times [3].
3. **Shorter lead times and better delivery:** less work-in-process means shorter queues; small lots let you react to demand changes within days instead of weeks.
4. **Quality:** standardized changeovers with fixed settings reduce trial-and-error, run-up scrap and first-piece failures. Fewer parts made per lot also means fewer parts at risk when a defect is found.
5. **Safety and ergonomics:** a well-designed changeover removes awkward lifts, reaching into dies and rushing, provided safety is designed in rather than bypassed (see the safety section).

## Why changeover time matters: lot size, inventory, flexibility and OEE

Long changeovers push plants into a predictable trap. If a changeover costs two hours, nobody wants to do it often, so the plant runs large batches. Large batches create inventory, long lead times and the need to forecast customer demand further ahead. Forecasts are wrong more often the further they reach, so the plant builds even more safety stock. The changeover becomes the hidden reason for half the inventory in the building.

### The batch-size trap in numbers

**Worked example (illustrative):** A stamping press runs 16 hours a day (960 minutes). Each changeover takes 90 minutes. The press makes 8 part numbers.

- If the press changes over twice a day, it loses 180 minutes, or 18.8 percent of its time (180 / 960). Running all 8 part numbers then takes 4 days, so each part is made about every 4 days, and the plant must hold roughly 4 days of stock for each part, plus safety stock.
- To make every part every day, the press would need 8 changeovers a day: 8 × 90 = 720 minutes, 75 percent of its time. That is not possible if the parts need more than 240 minutes of run time.
- Cut the changeover to 15 minutes, and 8 changeovers cost 120 minutes, 12.5 percent of the day. Every part can be made every day, and cycle stock drops from about 4 days to about 1 day.

That is the reason Toyota invested in changeover reduction long before it was fashionable: without fast changeovers, small-lot just-in-time production does not work.

### Changeover time in OEE

Overall equipment effectiveness (OEE) multiplies availability, performance and quality [4]. LEI counts adjustments among the six major losses that OEE targets [4]; in most OEE schemes, planned changeovers are booked as an availability loss, and run-up scrap after a changeover as a quality loss. That has two consequences:

- A changeover reduction shows up in OEE availability only if the time saved is used to produce. If the plant simply runs fewer hours, OEE availability does not move.
- If the plant uses the saved time for more frequent changeovers (to cut lot sizes), OEE availability may stay flat or even fall while inventory and lead time improve. That is a good result. Judge SMED by the goal you chose, not by OEE alone.

The detailed OEE definitions are covered in the [Manufacturing KPIs](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/manufacturing-kpis/) guide, and the maintenance side of OEE in the planned guide on [TPM and OEE](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/tpm-oee/).

### Three ways to use the time you win

| Option | When it fits | What you measure |
|---|---|---|
| **More capacity** (same number of changeovers) | Machine is a bottleneck, demand exceeds capacity | Output per week, overtime hours, OEE availability |
| **Smaller lots** (more changeovers, same total changeover time) | Machine has spare capacity, high inventory, long lead times, many variants | EPEI, lot size, days of inventory, lead time |
| **Mix** (some capacity, some smaller lots) | Most real plants | Both sets, with an explicit split agreed with planning |

The single most common reason SMED projects disappoint is that nobody made this decision. The changeover gets faster, planning keeps the old lot sizes, and the time saved disappears into longer idle periods. We return to that in the mistakes section.

## History: from Taylor and Ford to Shingo and Toyota

Setup reduction is older than its name. Frederick Winslow Taylor analyzed non-value-adding parts of setups in *Shop Management* (1911), Frank Gilbreth described setup-time reduction in *Motion Study* (1911), and Henry Ford's plants documented setup reduction in *Ford Methods and Ford Shops* (1915) [5]. None of them turned it into a structured, widely used method [5]. For most of the 20th century, the standard answer to long setups was mathematical: the economic order quantity, which accepts setup cost as given and calculates the "right" large batch [5].

### Toyota and quick die change

At Toyota, tool changeovers reportedly took two to eight hours in the early postwar years; the Wikipedia article flags this figure as needing a citation [5]. Taiichi Ohno became manager of Toyota's machine shops in 1945 and, in 1955, observed Danly stamping presses in the United States; Toyota later bought several for its Motomachi plant [5]. Toyota's internal "quick die change" program used the ECRS questions (eliminate, combine, rearrange, simplify), which came from the US Training Within Industry program of World War II [5][33]. According to the same source, Toyota's changeover times fell from hours to 15 minutes by the 1960s and to 3 minutes by the 1970s [5].

### Shigeo Shingo

Shigeo Shingo (1909–1990) was an industrial engineer and consultant with the Japan Management Association from 1945 [6]. He gathered improvement experience at Toyo Kogyo (today Mazda) in 1950 and at Mitsubishi Heavy Industries in Hiroshima in 1957, and from 1969 he worked at Toyota on reducing die-change time on presses, where die changes of one to two hours, sometimes half a day, dropped to a few minutes [6]. LEI places his key insights, the separation of internal and external setup and the conversion of internal to external work, in the 1950s and 1960s [1].

Shingo published the method in English in 1985 as *A Revolution in Manufacturing: The SMED System* (Productivity Press) [6][7]. The book reports that across his cases between 1975 and 1985, setup times fell on average to about 2.5 percent of the original, a factor of 40 [5]. Treat that figure as Shingo's own summary of his consulting cases, not an independent benchmark.

In 1988, Utah State University's business school created the Shingo Prize in his honor; today the Shingo Institute at the Jon M. Huntsman School of Business runs it [6][30].

### SMED in the US

US manufacturers met SMED through the translation of Shingo's books in the 1980s, the spread of just-in-time and the success of Japanese automakers. Today it is a standard module in lean training, including the quick changeover offers of NIST Manufacturing Extension Partnership (MEP) centers [18][31]. The broader lean context is covered in the [Lean Manufacturing](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/lean-manufacturing/) guide.

## How to measure changeover time correctly

Most changeover data in plants is wrong, and not because people lie. It is wrong because nobody agreed on where a changeover starts and ends. The ERP system often books "setup" as the time between two order confirmations; operators often log the time "the machine was apart"; engineers often time only the die swap. All three are shorter than the real loss.

### The definition: last good part to first good part

Use the LEI boundary: from the last good unit of the old run to the first good unit of the new run [2]. Two refinements make it watertight:

1. **At normal speed.** The new run is only "up" when it produces good parts at standard rate. Leanproduction.com defines the end as the first good part at full speed [19]. If the line runs at half speed for 20 minutes after restart, those 20 minutes are part of the changeover loss.
2. **Including wind-down.** If the old run slows down before the stop (for example, the line is emptied and runs partial), count that as well. Some plants call this the "3 Ups": clean-up, set-up and start-up [24].

### The three phases of a changeover

| Phase | What happens | Typical hidden losses |
|---|---|---|
| **Run-down / clean-up** | Last parts of the old order, emptying the line, removing material, cleaning | Line runs at reduced speed; operators leave to fetch the next job |
| **Setup (exchange)** | Removing and mounting tools, dies, format parts; changing settings | Searching, waiting for crane or forklift, missing parts, bolts |
| **Run-up / start-up** | First parts, measurement, adjustment, approval, ramp to full speed | Trial-and-error adjustments, waiting for quality approval, scrap |

Shingo's own breakdown of a conventional setup, as reported on Wikipedia, assigns about 30 percent to preparation, checking materials and tools; about 5 percent to mounting and removing; about 15 percent to centering, dimensioning and setting conditions; and about 50 percent to trial runs and adjustments [5]. An open engineering textbook on lean manufacturing gives a similar picture for metal stamping, with about 20 to 50 percent of setup time spent adjusting [36]. The practical lesson: the swap itself is the small part. The big parts are preparation and run-up.

### Changeover formulas

**Changeover time (per event):**
Changeover time = time of first good part at standard speed − time of last good part of the previous run

**Average changeover time:**
Average changeover time = sum of all changeover times / number of changeovers

**Changeover share of available time:**
Changeover share = total changeover time / planned production time × 100%

**Internal ratio:**
Internal ratio = internal setup time / total setup work content × 100%

**Cost of one changeover (simple model):**
Cost per changeover = lost contribution margin (only if the machine is a bottleneck or sold out) + labor cost of the changeover crew + run-up scrap + consumables

MachineMetrics, a machine-monitoring vendor, uses a comparable split: lost labor cost = hours down × number of staff × labor cost per hour, and lost product value = unit price × standard rate × hours down [21]. Be careful with the second term: it only counts as a real loss if the lost output could have been sold. On a machine with spare capacity, the cost of a changeover is mainly labor and scrap, and the benefit of SMED lies in smaller lots, not in recovered output.

**Worked example (illustrative):** A CNC machining center works two 8-hour shifts, 5 days a week, with 60 minutes of planned breaks per day: planned production time = (16 − 1) × 60 × 5 = 4,500 minutes per week. Last week it had 9 changeovers that took 52, 68, 47, 95, 61, 58, 73, 49 and 87 minutes.

- Total = 590 minutes; average = 590 / 9 = 65.6 minutes.
- Changeover share = 590 / 4,500 = 13.1 percent.
- Spread: shortest 47, longest 95 minutes, a range of 48 minutes. A range that wide usually means there is no standard; the long ones are the ones with missing tools or programs.
- Cost: crew of 1 setter at a loaded rate of $48 per hour (illustrative) = 590 / 60 × $48 = $472 per week in labor. If the machine is a bottleneck with a contribution margin of $180 per hour, the lost margin is 590 / 60 × $180 = $1,770 per week, or about $92,000 a year at 52 weeks. That is the number that justifies a project.

### How to collect the data

1. **Video first.** Film one complete changeover from the last good part to the first good part, with a clock visible. One camera on the machine, ideally a second one following the setter. Ask the team for permission first and explain the purpose: the video is about the process, not about the people. NIST MEP's Michigan center used video, a spaghetti diagram and a setup analysis chart in the Brighton NC Machine project [17].
2. **Spaghetti diagram.** Trace the setter's walking path on a layout. Changeovers often include hundreds or thousands of feet of walking; in the Brighton example, it was 9,121 feet per changeover before improvement [17].
3. **Element list.** Break the video into elements of a few seconds to a few minutes: "walk to tool crib, 3:20", "loosen 8 clamp bolts, 4:10". A typical changeover breaks down into roughly 30 to 50 elements, according to leanproduction.com [19].
4. **Two weeks of baseline.** Collect start and end times for all changeovers for at least two weeks before you change anything, so you know the average and the spread [19]. Vorne warns that teams change their behavior when they know they are being observed (the Hawthorne effect), so the first filmed changeover can be faster than normal [20].

The template for the element list is in the [templates section](#smed-templates-and-checklists).

## The SMED method in 8 steps

Shingo described four conceptual stages, often called the SMED stages: in the preliminary stage internal and external setup are mixed; stage 1 separates them; stage 2 converts internal to external; stage 3 streamlines all aspects of the setup [5]. Practitioner sources unfold these into seven basic steps: observe, separate, convert, streamline internal, streamline external, document, repeat [5]. The eight steps below add the step that most guides skip: preparing the project, including safety and the decision how the saved time will be used.

| Step | Shingo stage | Output |
|---|---|---|
| 0. Prepare: choose the machine, the team, the goal and the use of saved time | (preliminary) | Charter, baseline data, safety review |
| 1. Record the current changeover | preliminary stage | Video, element list with times, spaghetti diagram |
| 2. Separate internal and external elements | stage 1 | Each element marked I or E; checklist and staging plan |
| 3. Convert internal to external | stage 2 | Action list: preparation, function standardization, intermediate jigs |
| 4. Streamline internal elements | stage 3 | Quick clamps, eliminated adjustments, parallel work |
| 5. Streamline external elements | stage 3 | Carts, shadow boards, storage near the machine |
| 6. Standardize and train | (follow-up) | Standard changeover sheet, training records |
| 7. Sustain and repeat | (follow-up) | KPI on the board, audits, next cycle |

### Step 0: Prepare the project

**Choose the pilot machine.** Good pilots have a changeover of roughly 30 to 90 minutes that happens several times a week, a cooperative crew and visible business pain (bottleneck, high inventory, late orders). Vorne suggests a pilot changeover of 30 to 60 minutes [20]; leanproduction.com recommends SMED when changeovers account for at least 20 percent of lost productive time, and TPM first otherwise [19]. Both are vendor rules of thumb, not standards, but they point in the right direction: do not start with a 12-hour changeover or a 5-minute one.

**Form the team.** Operator(s) and setter(s) who do the changeover every day, a maintenance technician, a manufacturing engineer, a quality technician for first-piece approval, and a safety representative. A supervisor sponsors the team. Four to seven people is a workable size.

**Agree on the goal and on the use of saved time.** Write down the baseline (average and range), the target (for example, 50 percent reduction in the first cycle, as Vorne suggests [20]), and what planning will do with the time: capacity, smaller lots, or a split.

**Safety review.** List the energy sources (electrical, hydraulic, pneumatic, gravity, thermal) and the current energy control procedure. Decide before the workshop which steps fall under lockout/tagout. Never accept "faster" ideas that skip energy isolation (see the safety section).

### Step 1: Record the current changeover

Film it, build the element list, draw the spaghetti diagram. Write the elements in the order they happened, with start time, duration and who did them. Mark obvious waste right away: searching, walking, waiting for the crane, trial runs.

Questions to ask for every element (the classic "five whys" style questions also work):

- Why is this done at all? (Can we eliminate it?)
- Why now, while the machine stands? (Can it be external?)
- Why this way? (Can it be simpler?)
- Why by this person? (Can two people do it in parallel, or can the operator do it?)

### Step 2: Separate internal and external elements

Mark every element as I (needs a stopped machine) or E (could be done while the machine runs). Do not change the method yet; just stop doing external work during the stop. This step is mainly organizational:

- **Pre-changeover checklist:** everything the next job needs (die, tools, fixtures, inserts, material, program, drawing, gauges, packaging) is listed and checked before the machine stops.
- **Function checks:** the new die is checked for damage and completeness before it goes in, not after the first bad part.
- **Transport:** the new die is staged at the machine before the stop; the old die goes back to storage after the restart.

Leanproduction.com notes that moving elements to external can nearly halve the changeover time [19]. That matches practitioner experience, but it depends entirely on how much searching and fetching happens today.

### Step 3: Convert internal to external

Now look again at every remaining I element and ask what would have to change for it to become external. Shingo's techniques here are preparing operating conditions in advance, standardizing functions and using intermediate jigs [5]:

- **Preheat** molds and dies outside the machine so the first shots do not have to bring them to temperature.
- **Preset** tools in a presetter or on a second fixture while the machine runs.
- **Standardize** the dimensions that matter for the machine (clamping height, locating positions, connection points) so that a new die needs no adjustment.
- **Intermediate jigs**: mount the next workpiece or die on a standard pallet or subplate outside the machine, then swap the whole pallet.

### Step 4: Streamline internal elements

What remains internal is made fast and simple:

- **Functional clamps** instead of bolts: quarter-turn fasteners, cam clamps, U-slots so a bolt only needs a half turn, hydraulic or magnetic clamping.
- **Eliminate adjustments:** fixed stops, locating pins, scales and marks, settings stored as recipes. Adjustment is the largest single category of setup time in Shingo's breakdown [5].
- **Parallel operations:** two people on opposite sides of the machine, each with a defined sequence, can cut elapsed time by more than half by removing walking around the machine. They need a clear signal system and, under lockout/tagout, each person applies their own lock.
- **One tool, one size:** standardize bolt sizes so a single tool works everywhere.

### Step 5: Streamline external elements

External work does not stop the machine, but it ties up people and it can delay the start of the changeover. Streamline it with changeover carts organized by sequence, shadow boards, storage of dies and tools at the point of use following [5S](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/5s-visual-management/), and clear visual signals ("next job ready").

### Step 6: Standardize and train

Write the new method as a standard changeover sheet: the sequence of elements, who does what, key points (safety, quality), times and the tools needed. LEI describes [standardized work](https://www.m365.fm/entity/standard-work/) as establishing precise procedures for each operator's work [26]. Train everybody who does the changeover, not only the pilot crew; the TWI Job Instruction method, which breaks a job into important steps and key points, is a practical format [33]. The cluster on [standard work](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/shop-floor-management/standard-work/) covers the format in detail.

### Step 7: Sustain and repeat

Put changeover time on the shop floor board, review deviations daily and audit the standard regularly. SMED is a continuous improvement loop, not a one-time event. Run the next cycle: Wikipedia's summary of SMED practice says several iterations may be needed to get under ten minutes [5]. A second cycle typically attacks the hardware-dependent elements that the first cycle deferred.

## Shingo's eight techniques with examples

Shingo summarized his approach in eight techniques [5]. The table gives each a plain explanation and examples from different industries.

| # | Technique | What it means | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Separate internal from external setup | Do not stop the machine for work that can be done while it runs | Checklist, staging of die and material before the stop |
| 2 | Convert internal setup to external | Change the method so that work moves outside the stop | Preheating molds, presetting tools, preassembling format parts |
| 3 | Standardize function, not shape | Standardize only the features the machine interacts with, not the whole tool | Same clamping height and edge on every die via spacer blocks; same coupling on every mold |
| 4 | Use functional clamps or eliminate fasteners | Fasten with one motion, or not at all | Quarter-turn screws, cam levers, pear-shaped holes, hydraulic clamps, magnetic platens |
| 5 | Use intermediate jigs | Prepare on a standard carrier outside the machine and swap the carrier | Zero-point clamping pallets on machining centers, subplates for press dies |
| 6 | Adopt parallel operations | More than one person works at the same time on a defined sequence | Two setters on both sides of a press, operator prepares material while setter swaps the die |
| 7 | Eliminate adjustments | Replace trial-and-error with fixed positions and stored settings | Locating pins, stops, scales, recipe download, digital readouts |
| 8 | Mechanization | Use power assistance where it truly helps, after the other seven | Die carts with rollers, die lifters, motorized adjustment axes |

Two remarks on the list. First, mechanization comes last on purpose. Hardware bought before the organizational steps often automates waste. Second, "standardize function, not shape" is the most underused technique. A plant does not need to rebuild 200 dies; often a set of spacer blocks or adapter plates that bring all dies to the same clamping height and locating edge does the job.

## Changeover techniques by equipment type

The principles are the same everywhere, but the big levers differ by process. The tables below are a practitioner checklist, not a source-backed benchmark.

### Stamping presses

| Lever | Example |
|---|---|
| Standard die height and locating | Parallels or spacer blocks bring all dies to the same shut height; locating pins or a back stop center the die |
| Die transport | Die carts or rolling bolsters staged at the press; roller or ball tracks on the bolster |
| Clamping | Hydraulic or quick-acting mechanical clamps; U-slots and a single bolt size if bolts remain |
| Settings | Shut height, counterbalance pressure and feed settings stored per die; marked scales |
| Safety | Safety blocks whenever the die is adjusted or repaired in the press, as OSHA requires [10]; die-setting procedure under 29 CFR 1910.217(d)(9) |

### Injection molding

| Lever | Example |
|---|---|
| Preheating | Mold preheated on a separate temperature control unit before the swap |
| Connections | Multi-couplings for water and hot runner connections, color-coded |
| Material | Next material dried and ready; purge compound and procedure standardized |
| Clamping | Quick mold change systems (magnetic or hydraulic platens), standardized mold plate thickness |
| Settings | Process parameters downloaded from a recipe instead of tuned from memory |

### CNC machining

| Lever | Example |
|---|---|
| Tool presetting | Tools measured offline in a presetter, offsets transferred to the control |
| Workholding | Zero-point clamping, standardized pallets, dedicated fixtures stored at the machine |
| Programs | Proven programs released and loaded in advance; no editing at the machine |
| First part | In-machine probing replaces manual measurement for setting work offsets |
| Tool families | Standard tool library so that most jobs need few tool changes |

### Packaging and filling lines

| Lever | Example |
|---|---|
| Format parts | Color-coded format parts per product size, stored on a cart in changeover order |
| Adjustments | Fixed stops and numbered scales instead of free adjustment; servo axes with stored positions |
| Cleaning | Cleaning sequence standardized; dry cleaning first; parallel cleaning of removed parts |
| Labels and materials | Line clearance checklist; staging of labels, cartons and caps before the stop |
| Run-up | Defined first-off checks; speed ramp procedure |

### Cleaning-dominated and regulated changeovers (food, pharma, chemicals)

In food, beverage, pharmaceutical and chemical plants, the "exchange" is often small and the cleaning and verification are large. SMED thinking still applies: prepare cleaning agents, tools and documents beforehand; clean removable parts offline while the machine is reassembled with a second set; sequence products to minimize full cleans (light to dark, allergen-free before allergen-containing). The difference is that some steps are mandated and cannot be shortened by skipping them:

- US drug manufacturers must inspect packaging and labeling facilities immediately before use to ensure that all drug products and unsuitable packaging and labeling materials from previous operations have been removed, and document the results in the batch production records (21 CFR 211.130(e)) [11].
- US food facilities under the preventive controls rule must have food allergen controls that include procedures to protect food from allergen cross-contact during storage, handling and use (21 CFR 117.135(c)(2)) [37].

Here SMED shortens the work around the mandated steps and makes the mandated steps faster and more reliable, for instance with a line clearance checklist and a fixed sequence. It never removes them.

### Assembly lines and cells

Assembly changeovers are mostly about material and information: different kits, different work instructions, different fixtures at a few stations. Levers are kitting of the next variant before the changeover, fixtures that cover several variants, digital work instructions that switch with the order, and "rolling" changeovers where each station switches when the first unit of the new variant arrives, so that the line never stops as a whole.

## SMED example: a complete project on an injection molding machine

The following example is illustrative. The numbers are realistic for a midsize molding shop, but they do not describe a real plant. They show how the steps fit together and how to calculate the effect.

**Situation.** A 300-ton injection molding machine runs 6 molds for a family of housings. It works 3 shifts of 8 hours with 30 minutes of breaks per shift, 5 days a week: planned production time = 3 × 7.5 × 60 × 5 = 6,750 minutes per week. It changes molds 5 times a week. Baseline over two weeks: average changeover 96 minutes, range 78 to 131 minutes. The machine is not a bottleneck; the problem is 11 days of finished-goods inventory and frequent rush orders.

**Goal agreed with planning.** Cut average changeover time by at least 50 percent in the first cycle and use the time for more changeovers, so that every mold runs at least every 3 days instead of every 6.

### Step 1: element list (before)

| # | Element | Time (min) | I/E today | Could be |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Last shots, empty hopper, stop machine | 6 | I | I (partly E: empty hopper before the end of the run) |
| 2 | Walk to mold storage, find next mold, fetch crane | 14 | I | E |
| 3 | Disconnect water and hot runner lines (12 single couplings) | 8 | I | I, streamline |
| 4 | Unclamp mold (8 bolts), lift out | 9 | I | I, streamline |
| 5 | Lift in new mold, align by eye, clamp (8 bolts) | 13 | I | I, streamline |
| 6 | Connect water and hot runner lines, find right hose for each port | 10 | I | I, streamline |
| 7 | Search for material, dry it | 7 | I | E |
| 8 | Heat mold to temperature | 12 | I | E (preheat) |
| 9 | Set parameters from setup sheet, adjust | 9 | I | I, eliminate adjustment |
| 10 | Trial shots, adjust, wait for quality approval | 8 | I | I, streamline |
| **Total internal (elapsed)** | | **96** | | |

The two external candidates (elements 2 and 7) and the preheating (element 8) add up to 33 minutes of machine downtime that does not need a stopped machine.

### Step 2 and 3: separate and convert

- Elements 2 and 7 become external with a pre-changeover checklist triggered 45 minutes before the planned end of the run: mold, crane slot, material (dried), hoses, documents, first-off gauge. Saving: 21 minutes.
- Element 8 becomes external with a preheating station where the next mold is brought to temperature while the old one runs. Saving: about 10 minutes (2 minutes remain for stabilization in the machine).
- Element 1: the hopper is emptied in the last minutes of the run instead of after the stop. Saving: 3 minutes.

Result after the first two steps: 96 − 21 − 10 − 3 = **62 minutes**, achieved mainly with a checklist, a preheater and discipline.

### Step 4: streamline internal

| # | Measure | Before (min) | After (min) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3 + 6 | Multi-coupling plates for water and hot runner, color-coded | 18 | 4 |
| 4 + 5 | Standardized locating ring and clamp slots, quick-acting clamps; second person for parallel work | 22 | 9 |
| 9 | Process parameters stored per mold and loaded from the controller | 9 | 2 |
| 10 | First-off check with a dedicated gauge at the machine, quality technician pre-notified | 8 | 5 |
| 1 | Run-down with hopper already empty | 3 | 3 |
| 8 | Stabilization after preheating | 2 | 2 |
| **Total** | | **62** | **25** |

Elapsed time is now 25 minutes. Note that parallel work by two people reduces elapsed time but not labor time: the labor content of elements 4 and 5 is still about 18 person-minutes.

### Step 6: standardize

The new sequence is written as a standard changeover sheet with two columns (setter A, setter B), trained to all three shifts, and audited weekly for a month. After four weeks, the average is 27 minutes with a range of 23 to 34 minutes.

### The effect

| Indicator | Before | After |
|---|---|---|
| Average changeover time | 96 min | 27 min (−72%) |
| Changeover range | 78–131 min | 23–34 min |
| Changeovers per week | 5 | 10 |
| Total changeover time per week | 480 min | 270 min |
| Changeover share of planned time | 7.1% | 4.0% |
| Interval at which each mold runs (EPEI) | about 6 days | about 3 days |
| Finished-goods inventory (cycle stock) | about 11 days | about 6 days (target) |

The machine now changes over twice as often and still spends 210 fewer minutes per week in changeovers. Cycle stock falls roughly in proportion to the interval. The investment: a preheating station, six multi-coupling sets, clamp standardization and two days of the team's time. The business case for this kind of project is calculated in the [business case section](#the-business-case-costs-roi-and-inventory-accounting).

## From shorter changeovers to smaller lots: EPEI and lot sizing

SMED creates potential; scheduling turns it into results. Two tools connect changeover time with lot size and inventory: EPEI and the classic lot-size formula.

### What EPEI means

EPEI stands for "every part every interval" (LEI's lexicon now uses the term EPEx, "every product every interval"). It describes how often a machine or process cycles through all its part numbers: if a machine is changed over so that it produces every part number every three days, its EPEx is three days [3]. LEI notes that EPEx depends on changeover times and the number of part numbers; long changeovers and many part numbers push it up unless changeover time is reduced [3]. A shorter EPEI means smaller lots and less inventory. In [value stream mapping](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/value-stream-mapping/), EPEI is a standard entry in the data box of a process with changeovers [35].

### How to calculate EPEI

A common practitioner formula (derived from the time balance of a process; not an LEI formula):

EPEI = total changeover time for one cycle through all parts / (available time per day − run time needed per day)

The idea: each day, the machine needs a fixed amount of run time to meet demand. Whatever time is left can be spent on changeovers. The more changeover time a full cycle through all parts needs, the more days the cycle takes.

**Worked example (illustrative):** A machine has 900 minutes of available time per day. It makes 4 parts with the following daily demand and cycle times:

| Part | Daily demand (pieces) | Cycle time (min/piece) | Run time per day (min) | Changeover time (min) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 400 | 0.8 | 320 | 40 |
| B | 300 | 0.8 | 240 | 40 |
| C | 150 | 1.0 | 150 | 60 |
| D | 50 | 1.2 | 60 | 60 |
| **Total** | | | **770** | **200** |

- Time left for changeovers per day: 900 − 770 = 130 minutes.
- One full cycle through all four parts needs 200 minutes of changeover time.
- EPEI = 200 / 130 = 1.54 days, rounded up in practice to **2 days**: every part is made every 2 days.
- Lot sizes at EPEI 2 days = 2 days of demand: A 800, B 600, C 300, D 100 pieces.

**Now apply SMED.** Changeovers drop to 15 minutes for A and B and 20 minutes for C and D: total = 70 minutes.

- EPEI = 70 / 130 = 0.54 days, rounded up to **1 day**: every part every day.
- Lot sizes halve: A 400, B 300, C 150, D 50 pieces.
- Average cycle stock (about half a lot, if demand is steady) falls from about 1 day of demand to about half a day.
- Spare time: at EPEI 1 day, changeovers need 70 of the 130 free minutes; 60 minutes remain as a buffer for breakdowns and demand peaks.

Two practical notes. First, never plan with zero spare time; real machines have breakdowns, and an EPEI that uses every free minute collapses on the first bad day. Second, EPEI can be different for high runners and low runners: A and B every day, C and D every second day is often a better compromise than a uniform interval. This is where SMED meets [heijunka](https://www.m365.fm/entity/heijunka/), the leveling of production by volume and mix [25].

### The lot-size formula and why setup cost matters

The economic order quantity (EOQ), developed by Ford W. Harris in 1913 [39] and for decades the standard approach to lot sizing [5], balances setup cost against holding cost:

EOQ = √(2 × D × S / H)

where D = annual demand, S = cost per setup (or order), H = annual holding cost per unit. For production with a finite production rate, the economic production quantity (EPQ) adds a correction factor √(1 / (1 − d/p)), where d is the demand rate and p the production rate. The details of both formulas, including their assumptions, are covered in the cluster on [lot sizing](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/production-scheduling-aps/lot-sizing/).

The key point for SMED is the square root. If setup cost S falls to a quarter, the economic lot size halves (√0.25 = 0.5). If S falls to one-ninth, the lot size falls to a third.

**Worked example (illustrative):** D = 24,000 pieces per year, H = $2.40 per piece per year (for example, 20 percent carrying cost on a $12 part), S = $160 per setup (two hours of a two-person crew at $40 per hour loaded).

- EOQ = √(2 × 24,000 × 160 / 2.40) = √3,200,000 ≈ **1,789 pieces**, about 13 lots per year (24,000 / 1,789 ≈ 13.4).
- After SMED, the changeover takes 30 minutes with the same crew: S = $40.
- EOQ = √(2 × 24,000 × 40 / 2.40) = √800,000 ≈ **894 pieces**, about 27 lots per year (24,000 / 894 ≈ 26.8).
- Average cycle stock (EOQ / 2) falls from about 894 to about 447 pieces; at $12 per piece, that frees about $5,400 of inventory for this one part.

The formula has well-known limits: it assumes constant demand, known costs and no capacity constraint, and the "cost per setup" is hard to pin down when the setter is paid anyway. Lean practitioners therefore use it as an argument for direction ("lower setup cost justifies smaller lots"), not as a scheduling rule. A pull system with [kanban](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/kanban-pull-system/) and an EPEI-based schedule usually replaces it [34].

### Capacity check: can you afford more changeovers?

Before planning cuts lot sizes, check the capacity balance:

Required time per week = Σ (demand × cycle time) + number of changeovers × average changeover time

It must stay comfortably below the available time. At a bottleneck, a shorter changeover is first a capacity gain; whether you spend it on smaller lots is a deliberate decision, which the [theory of constraints](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/theory-of-constraints/) guide discusses in detail. At a non-bottleneck, spare capacity exists by definition, and it is usually wiser to spend saved changeover time on more frequent changeovers.

## Safety: changeovers, lockout/tagout and ergonomics

None of the twelve top-ranking pages analyzed for this guide addresses lockout/tagout, although almost every internal setup element happens inside a machine's danger zone. In the US, this is not optional.

### Setting up is servicing under OSHA's lockout/tagout standard

OSHA's standard for the control of hazardous energy, 29 CFR 1910.147, covers the servicing and maintenance of machines and equipment in which unexpected energization, start-up or release of stored energy could harm employees [8]. Its definition of "servicing and/or maintenance" explicitly includes setting up and adjusting, and "setting up" is defined as any work performed to prepare a machine or equipment to perform its normal production operation [8]. A changeover is therefore, by default, a servicing activity under the standard.

The standard requires a documented energy control procedure, training, and a fixed application sequence: preparation for shutdown, machine shutdown, isolation of energy sources, application of lockout or tagout devices, release of stored energy, and verification of isolation [8]. Employers must inspect each energy control procedure at least annually [8].

### The minor servicing exception: narrow, not a loophole

A note to the scope paragraph exempts minor tool changes and adjustments and other minor servicing activities that take place during normal production operations, if they are routine, repetitive and integral to the use of the equipment for production, and only if the work is performed using alternative measures that provide effective protection [8]. OSHA's lockout/tagout eTool explains that such activities then fall under the machine guarding requirements of Subpart O, and lists examples of alternative measures: specially designed tools, remote devices, interlocked barrier guards, local disconnects and control switches under the exclusive control of the employee doing the work [9]. If any part of the exception is not met, lockout/tagout applies [9].

What this means for SMED:

- A full die or mold change is typically not a "minor" tool change during normal production; plan it with lockout/tagout. Whether a specific task qualifies for the exception is a legal and safety judgment for your safety professional, not for the SMED team.
- SMED must make lockout faster and easier, never skip it. Examples: lockout points grouped and labeled at the machine, a lockout station in the changeover cart, group lockout boxes for parallel work where each person applies their own lock, verification steps written into the standard changeover sheet.
- A changeover time standard that can only be met by skipping energy isolation is a wrong standard. Write lockout time into the target.

### Mechanical power presses

For mechanical power presses, OSHA requires a die-setting procedure that ensures compliance with the point-of-operation safeguarding rules, spring-loaded turnover bars where the press is designed for them, die stops on inclined presses, safety blocks whenever dies are adjusted or repaired in the press, and tools such as brushes or pressure guns so that operators and die setters do not reach into the point of operation to lubricate [10]. These belong in every press changeover standard.

### Ergonomics

Changeovers involve heavy, awkward handling: lifting dies, format parts and fixtures, working bent over inside guards, reaching for bolts. OSHA describes ergonomics as fitting the job to the worker to reduce musculoskeletal disorders [32]. SMED and ergonomics usually point in the same direction: die carts, roller tracks, lifting aids, fixtures at waist height and fewer bolts are faster and easier on the body. The risk lies in speed targets: when a team is pushed to beat the clock, lifting technique and two-person lifts get skipped. Time targets must be set so that they can be met at a sustainable pace.

### Safety checklist for SMED projects

| Check | Done |
|---|---|
| Energy sources of the machine listed (electrical, hydraulic, pneumatic, gravity, thermal, chemical) | ☐ |
| Current energy control procedure reviewed; changeover steps under lockout/tagout identified | ☐ |
| Safety professional has reviewed any claim that a step falls under the minor servicing exception | ☐ |
| Lockout and verification steps written into the standard changeover sheet, with time | ☐ |
| Parallel work: each person applies their own lock; communication signals defined | ☐ |
| Presses: die-setting procedure, safety blocks, turnover bars, die stops checked [10] | ☐ |
| Heavy lifts identified; lifting aids or two-person lifts defined | ☐ |
| Hot surfaces (preheated molds, heated dies) identified; gloves and handling defined | ☐ |
| New quick-clamping hardware checked for clamping force and fail-safe behavior | ☐ |
| First changeover with the new standard observed by a supervisor | ☐ |

## Quality: first-piece approval, error proofing and regulated line clearance

Run-up and adjustment are the largest part of many changeovers [5]. Quality problems after a changeover usually have the same causes: wrong tool or program, settings from memory, missing first-piece checks, or a first-piece approval that waits for an inspector who is busy elsewhere.

### First-piece approval without waiting

- **Define the check** in the standard changeover sheet: which characteristics, which gauge, which sample size.
- **Bring the gauge to the machine** and train the operator to measure, with a quality technician approving only where the control plan requires it.
- **Notify quality in advance** with the pre-changeover checklist, so that the inspector is there when the first part comes off.
- **Record the first-piece result** with the order, so that it is traceable.

### Error proofing the changeover

[Poka-yoke](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/lean-manufacturing/poka-yoke/) devices prevent or detect mistakes [28]. In changeovers, typical examples are:

| Mistake | Poka-yoke |
|---|---|
| Wrong die or mold for the order | Coded die (RFID, barcode or mechanical key) that the machine control checks against the order |
| Wrong program or recipe | Recipe called up automatically from the order number |
| Hoses connected to the wrong ports | Multi-coupling plates or couplings that only fit one way, color-coded |
| Format part mounted in the wrong position | Asymmetric locating pins |
| Old labels or materials left on the line | Line clearance checklist with a sign-off; dedicated containers for removed material |

### Regulated industries

In regulated plants, the changeover is part of the quality system. In US pharmaceutical packaging, the line clearance inspection before use and its documentation in the batch record are required by 21 CFR 211.130(e) [11]. In food, allergen changeovers are part of the food allergen controls required under 21 CFR 117.135(c)(2) [37]. In automotive (IATF 16949) and aerospace (AS9100) supply chains, control plans and customer-specific requirements may call for setup verification or first-piece verification after setups; check your contracts and control plans rather than assuming a generic rule. The [aerospace production planning](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/aerospace-production-planning/) guide covers the aerospace context. SMED projects in these environments should include the quality lead from day one and validate any change to cleaning or verification steps before it is used.

### Statistical view

A changeover is also a source of variation. If parts from the start of each run differ systematically, a control chart split by "first 30 minutes after changeover" versus "rest of run" makes it visible. Cakmakci used process capability (Cpk) in an automotive SMED study to evaluate the link between setup reduction and equipment design [16]. The [Six Sigma and root cause analysis](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/six-sigma-root-cause-analysis/) guide covers capability analysis and control charts.

## People and roles: who does what in a changeover

SMED is often described as a technical method. In practice, it changes the work of operators and setters more than any other lean tool, so it succeeds or fails with them.

### Roles

| Role | In the SMED project | In the daily changeover |
|---|---|---|
| Operator | Team member; knows the details, films and is filmed | Does external preparation, parts of the internal work, first-piece measurement |
| Setter / die setter | Core team member; owns the method | Does the internal exchange, leads parallel work |
| Maintenance technician | Builds and maintains quick-change hardware, couplings, clamps | Supports complex changeovers; keeps dies and molds in good condition |
| Manufacturing engineer | Facilitates, analyzes video, designs technical solutions | Owns the standard; improves it |
| Quality technician | Defines first-piece checks and error proofing | Approves first pieces where required |
| Supervisor | Sponsors; frees time for the team | Plans changeovers with the schedule; checks adherence |
| Production planner | Agrees on how saved time is used; changes lot sizes | Sequences orders to minimize changeover effort |
| Safety representative | Reviews energy control, ergonomics | Audits lockout during changeovers |

### What changes for operators and setters

SMED usually shifts work: operators take over external preparation and parts of the internal setup, and setters spend less time searching and more time on the technical steps. That is a change in job content. In unionized plants, job descriptions, classifications and pay grades are often defined in the contract, so agree on the new split with the union before the new standard goes live. In non-union plants, the same question comes up informally: who gets trained, who is responsible if a first piece fails, and whether the new tasks are recognized.

### Respect for people

Three rules from practice make the difference between a SMED project that the floor supports and one it resists:

1. **Film the process, not the person.** Explain the purpose before you film. Let the setter comment on the video first; he or she usually knows exactly where the waste is. In unionized plants, inform the union representatives early and agree on how videos are stored and used.
2. **Do not turn time saved into headcount cuts.** If SMED leads to layoffs, the next improvement project will not get any ideas from the floor. Use the time for capacity, smaller lots or training.
3. **Set targets that can be met safely.** A changeover standard describes a good method at a normal pace. Pit-stop analogies help explain parallel work and preparation, but a pit crew trains for one stop under race conditions; a setter does the same changeover at the end of a long shift, many times a year.

Vorne recommends starting with "human" improvements, meaning preparation and organization, before technical changes [20]; leanproduction.com makes the same recommendation [19]. That order also builds trust: the team sees quick results from its own ideas before money is spent.

## SMED templates and checklists

## Rollout roadmap: from a pilot to a plant-wide changeover system

The timeline below is a practitioner estimate for a midsize discrete plant; time frames depend on equipment, hardware lead times and how much time the team gets [unverified].

| Phase | Time frame | Activities | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Prepare | Weeks 1–3 | Choose pilot machine, collect baseline data for at least two weeks [19], charter, safety review, agree on use of saved time | Charter signed, baseline known |
| 2. Pilot workshop | Week 4 (3–5 days) | Film and analyze the changeover, separate and convert, quick wins on the floor, first trial of new sequence | First new standard, action list |
| 3. Implement | Weeks 5–10 | Hardware (couplings, clamps, carts), preheating or presetting, second trial, training of all shifts | New standard trained |
| 4. Stabilize | Weeks 11–16 | Daily KPI on the board, weekly audits, adjust lot sizes and schedule | Stable time, lots reduced |
| 5. Spread | Months 5–12 | Next 3–5 machines, internal SMED coaches trained, changeover standard as part of new equipment specifications | Changeover system across the area |

### Choosing the next machines

Rank candidates by impact and effort:

| Criterion | Weight | Question |
|---|---|---|
| Bottleneck | High | Is the machine the constraint of the value stream? |
| Changeover frequency × duration | High | How many hours per week are lost to changeovers? |
| Inventory / lead time pain | Medium | Does this machine drive large lots and high inventory downstream? |
| Variation of changeover time | Medium | Is the range wide (a sign of missing standards)? |
| Team readiness | Medium | Is the crew willing, is a supervisor sponsoring? |
| Hardware effort | Low (negative) | Is major investment needed before results? |

### Make changeovers a design requirement

The cheapest SMED project is the one you do not need. Write changeover requirements into specifications for new equipment, tools and molds: maximum changeover time, standardized clamping heights and couplings, stored settings, no adjustments. Braglia and colleagues propose a structured method, quick changeover design (QCD), to help machine builders design for rapid changeovers from the start [15]. Purchasing and engineering need to own this, not only the lean team.

| KPI | Formula / definition | Why it matters | Typical review |
|---|---|---|---|

| First-piece first-time-right | First pieces approved at first check / all first pieces × 100% | Quality of the changeover | Weekly |

Keep the board simple: one chart per machine with the daily changeover times against target, and one line for the reason when a changeover exceeded the target. The general rules for KPI boards are covered in the [shop floor management](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/shop-floor-management/) guide.

**A word on targets:** "under ten minutes" is Shingo's aspiration, not a universal benchmark. A good changeover time is one that is stable, safe, and short enough to support the EPEI your customers need. For a large press line, 20 minutes may be excellent; for a small CNC lathe, 20 minutes may be slow.

## The business case: costs, ROI and inventory accounting

Finance teams ask three questions about SMED: what does it cost, what does it return, and when does it show in the numbers. The answers depend heavily on whether the machine is a bottleneck.

### Costs

| Cost item | Typical nature | Accounting |
|---|---|---|
| Team time for workshop and follow-up | Hours of operators, setters, engineers | Operating expense |
| Low-cost hardware (carts, shadow boards, spacer blocks, color coding) | Small | Usually operating expense, depending on your capitalization threshold |
| Quick-change hardware (couplings, clamping systems, preheaters, presetters) | Medium to high | Often capital expenditure if above your threshold |
| Training | Hours | Operating expense |
| Production loss during trials | Usually small if trials run during planned changeovers | Operating expense |

### Returns

1. **Capacity at a bottleneck:** saved changeover hours × contribution margin per hour, if the additional output can be sold. In the CNC example above, the 590 minutes of changeover per week cost about $92,000 a year in lost margin; halving them would recover about $46,000 a year, but only if the extra output finds buyers.
2. **Inventory reduction:** a one-time cash release (balance sheet) plus ongoing savings in carrying cost (P&L).
3. **Fewer expedites and rush changeovers:** overtime, premium freight, and disruption of planned work.
4. **Scrap and rework:** shorter and more stable run-ups.
5. **Labor:** sometimes lower, often similar; parallel work shortens elapsed time but not always labor content.

### Worked ROI example (illustrative, continuing the molding example)

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Investment: preheating station | $12,000 |
| Investment: 6 multi-coupling sets at $1,500 | $9,000 |
| Investment: clamp and locating standardization | $6,000 |
| Team time: 6 people × 16 hours × $45 loaded | $4,320 |
| **Total one-time cost** | **$31,320** |
| Finished-goods inventory reduced by 5 days at $6,000 per day of cost of goods | $30,000 one-time cash release |
| Carrying cost saved at 20% per year on $30,000 | $6,000 per year |
| Fewer expedites and overtime (estimate from last year's records) | $8,000 per year |
| Run-up scrap: 15 fewer scrap shots per changeover × 10 changeovers × 50 weeks × $0.90 | $6,750 per year |
| **Recurring benefit** | **$20,750 per year** |
| **Simple payback on recurring benefits** | 31,320 / 20,750 ≈ 1.5 years |

The cash released from inventory ($30,000) almost covers the investment in the first year, but it is a balance-sheet effect, not profit. Keep these apart in the business case, and do not count the same saving twice.

### The absorption-costing effect

Under absorption costing [38], which US GAAP requires in substance for external inventory reporting (ASC 330-10-30-1 and 30-3 include an allocation of fixed production overhead based on normal capacity, and 330-10-30-8 states that excluding all overheads from inventory cost is not an accepted accounting procedure [40]; ask your controller how your company applies it), part of the fixed manufacturing overhead is capitalized in inventory. When inventory falls, the overhead that was capitalized in the reduced inventory is released to cost of goods sold in that period. Reported profit can therefore dip temporarily while inventory comes down, even though cash improves.

**Worked example (illustrative):** If 30 percent of the $30,000 inventory value is fixed overhead, reducing inventory releases about $9,000 of overhead into the period's cost of goods sold. Plant controllers should explain this to management before the SMED program starts, so that a lean success does not look like a cost overrun. The [Manufacturing KPIs](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/manufacturing-kpis/) guide discusses how to present such effects on a plant scorecard.

## Documented SMED examples

Many competitor pages cite "typical" reductions of 50 to 90 percent without a source. The examples below are documented, with their limits.

### Toyota press changeovers

Wikipedia, drawing on Shingo's books, reports that Toyota's changeovers fell from hours to 15 minutes by the 1960s and to 3 minutes by the 1970s [5]; Shingo's own account describes die changes of one to two hours, sometimes half a day, reduced to a few minutes after he began working at Toyota in 1969 [6]. An open engineering textbook reports a 1,000-ton press changeover at Toyota reduced from four hours to three minutes [36]. The figures come from Shingo and Toyota accounts and were not independently audited, but they are consistent across sources.

### Brighton NC Machine Corporation (Michigan, NIST MEP)

Brighton NC Machine, a Michigan manufacturer with more than 100 employees, worked with the Michigan Manufacturing Technology Center, part of the NIST MEP National Network [17]. Before the project, changeovers could only be done on first shift, took 8 hours and 20 minutes and required 9,121 feet of employee travel [17]. A kaizen event with video, a spaghetti diagram and a setup analysis chart led to a 70 percent reduction in changeover time and 98 percent less travel time, and the company reported $460,000 in new sales and $125,000 in cost savings [17]. Results are as reported by the company and MEP; MEP success stories are client-reported.

### Shingo's consulting cases

Shingo reported that across his setup reduction cases between 1975 and 1985, setup times fell on average to about 2.5 percent of the original [5]. That is an average of selected cases from the method's inventor, and therefore an upper benchmark rather than an expectation.

### Research overview

A state-of-the-art review by da Silva and Godinho Filho in the *International Journal of Advanced Manufacturing Technology* (2019) classified 130 articles on SMED by area, method, tools, implementation and results [14]. The literature shows SMED applied in automotive, metalworking, packaging, printing, food and pharmaceutical settings. One case from the printing industry cited in an open textbook reported a 46 percent reduction in internal setup time [36]. Published cases tend to report successes; failed or faded projects are rarely written up, so treat averages from case literature with caution.

## Common SMED mistakes

| Mistake | Why it hurts | Better |
|---|---|---|
| No decision on how saved time is used | Gains disappear into idle time; lots stay the same | Agree with planning before the project: capacity, smaller lots or split |
| Buying hardware first | Automates waste; budget gone before organization is fixed | Exhaust separation and conversion first; mechanization last [5] |
| Measuring only the "swap" | Misses run-up and adjustment, often the largest part | Last good part to first good part at standard speed [2] |
| Filming without explanation | Crew feels watched; resistance | Explain purpose, involve setter in analysis, agree on video use |
| Pilot on the hardest machine | Long, costly, slow results | Pilot with a 30–90 minute changeover that happens often |
| One "hero" changeover | Record time with engineers present, not repeatable | Standardize, train all shifts, measure over weeks |
| Skipping lockout/tagout to save time | Serious injury risk; OSHA violation | Write lockout into the standard; make it faster, never skip it [8] |
| No standard and no audit | Times drift back within months | Standard changeover sheet, audits, KPI on the board |
| Ignoring the schedule | Random sequences cause full changeovers that could be partial | Sequence by product family; plan changeovers at fixed times |
| Starting a changeover just before shift change | Handover in the middle of the internal setup; steps get skipped or repeated, locks change hands | Plan changeovers so that one crew finishes them, or define a formal handover point with lockout transfer as your energy control procedure requires |
| Setup cost in planning system never updated | ERP keeps calculating large lots | Update setup times and lot-size rules in the ERP after SMED |
| Treating SMED as a one-time project | No second cycle; the hardware stage never happens | Plan cycles; make SMED part of the improvement routine |

The last but one point is easy to miss. Many ERP systems calculate lot sizes from setup times stored in routings. If those stay at the old values, the planning system keeps recommending the old large lots. The [S&OP and MRP](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/sop-mrp-production-planning/) guide explains where these parameters live.

## Limits and criticism of SMED

SMED is one of the best-documented lean tools, but it has limits, and the research literature has pointed them out.

### 1. Too much organization, too little design

McIntosh and colleagues argued in 1996 that the widespread interpretation of SMED has led practitioners to favor low-cost, team-based organizational changes and to overlook design-based solutions that could improve changeovers more fundamentally; they paid particular attention to the problem of sustaining improvements [13]. The same research group later published a critical evaluation of Shingo's methodology in the *International Journal of Production Research* (2000) [12]. Cakmakci's automotive study similarly links setup problems to inadequate equipment and die design [16], and Braglia and colleagues developed quick changeover design to bring changeover capability into machine design [15]. The practical conclusion: run SMED workshops, but also write changeover requirements into specifications for new equipment and product designs.

### 2. Sustaining the gains

Changeover times tend to drift back when the standard is not trained to new staff, the changeover cart loses its tools, or quick-change hardware is not maintained. McIntosh and colleagues highlighted sustainment as a central weakness [13]. The countermeasures are unglamorous: standard work, audits, a KPI on the board, and maintenance of the hardware under TPM.

### 3. Not every process gains equally

- **Process industries** (chemicals, food, pharma) often have changeovers dominated by cleaning, validation and mandated checks. SMED shortens the work around them, but campaign planning and product sequencing (light to dark, allergen-free first) may matter more.
- **High-mix, low-volume job shops** have many unique setups that happen once. Element-by-element analysis of a single setup pays off less; standardized workholding, tool libraries, offline programming and grouping by part family pay off more.
- **Machines with spare capacity and low inventory** gain little from SMED. Check the share of changeovers in lost time first [19].

### 4. Gains that do not reach the bottom line

If saved time is not used (see the three options above), SMED improves a number on a chart and nothing else. The method cannot prevent that; management has to, and this is the most common reason SMED disappoints.

### 5. Pressure on people

Speed targets and stopwatches can create pressure, rushed work and shortcuts around safety. The method itself does not require that, but its implementation can. Good practice sets targets at a sustainable pace, protects lockout time and does not use saved time to cut jobs.

## SMED goes digital: data, video, MES and scheduling

Digital tools do not replace the SMED method, but they make the analysis faster and the results easier to sustain.

### Measuring changeovers automatically

Manual changeover logs are often incomplete. Machine-monitoring systems can detect changeovers from machine states and part counts, so that every changeover gets a start, an end and a duration without paperwork; vendors such as MachineMetrics argue that automated data capture is more reliable than manual logging [21] (vendor claim). The operator still needs to enter the reason when a changeover runs long. The [manufacturing execution system (MES) and ISA-95](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/mes-isa-95/) guide explains how such data fits into the plant's system architecture, and [Manufacturing IT with Azure](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/manufacturing-it-azure/) covers cloud options for collecting it.

Quick-change hardware is increasingly connected: coded dies and molds that the controller checks against the order, recipe downloads, clamping systems with sensors. Each of these is an OT asset on the plant network, with the same patching and access questions as any controller; the planned guide on [OT cybersecurity and ISA/IEC 62443](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/ot-cybersecurity-iec-62443/) covers how to secure them.

### Video analysis

Smartphone or action-camera video is enough for most SMED workshops. Software that lets the team cut the video into elements and tag them as internal or external saves hours of manual timing. Agree on data privacy: who sees the videos, how long they are stored, and that they are not used for individual performance evaluation.

### Digital work instructions and recipes

Digital work instructions can switch automatically with the order, show the standard changeover sheet step by step with pictures, and record the first-piece result; this is covered in the cluster on [paperless manufacturing](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/shop-floor-management/paperless-manufacturing/). Recipe management, where machine settings are stored per product and downloaded from the order, removes adjustment as a source of time and error. In regulated industries, both must be validated.

### Scheduling with sequence-dependent setups

When changeover times depend on the sequence (light to dark colors, small to large formats, allergen-free first), a scheduling tool can sequence orders to minimize total changeover time. Advanced planning and scheduling (APS) systems can model sequence-dependent setup matrices; the [production scheduling and APS](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/production-scheduling-aps/) guide covers how they work and what they need from the shop floor. The setup matrix in the APS must be updated after every SMED cycle, otherwise the scheduler optimizes against old times.

### Simulation and value stream models

Before a plant reduces lot sizes across a value stream, it can test the effect of shorter changeovers on EPEI, inventory and lead time in a value stream model or a discrete event simulation. The [digital twin in manufacturing](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/digital-twin-manufacturing/) guide and the cluster on [manufacturing simulation](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/digital-twin-manufacturing/manufacturing-simulation/) explain the approach. Value stream software such as iFAKT Polaris is one example of a tool that holds changeover times and lot sizes per process step and calculates their effect on the value stream; see the [iFAKT Polaris](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/ifakt-polaris/) profile. **Note: The author works at iFAKT GmbH.**

### Limits

Digital tools amplify a working standard; they do not create one. A machine-monitoring dashboard showing 90-minute changeovers changes nothing until a team films the changeover and fixes it. Start with paper and video, and digitize the data collection once the standard and the KPI routine work.

## FAQ: SMED and changeover questions

### What does SMED stand for?

SMED stands for single-minute exchange of die (sometimes written "dies"). "Single-minute" means a single-digit number of minutes, so a changeover under ten minutes [1]. The method applies to any changeover, not only to press dies.

### Who invented SMED?

Shigeo Shingo (1909–1990), a Japanese industrial engineer, developed the method from the 1950s on, with work at Toyo Kogyo (Mazda), Mitsubishi Heavy Industries and, from 1969, Toyota [6]. He published it in English in 1985 as *A Revolution in Manufacturing: The SMED System* [6].

### What are the steps of SMED?

Observe and record the changeover, separate internal from external elements, convert internal to external, streamline internal elements, streamline external elements, standardize, and repeat [5]. This guide adds a preparation step (pilot, team, safety review, use of saved time) in front.

### What is the difference between internal and external setup?

Internal setup can only be done while the machine is stopped, such as mounting a die. External setup can be done while the machine runs, such as fetching and checking the next die [1]. SMED moves as much work as possible from internal to external.

### How do you calculate changeover time?

Measure from the last good part of the previous run to the first good part of the new run at standard speed [2][21]. Average changeover time is the sum of all changeover times divided by the number of changeovers; the changeover share is total changeover time divided by planned production time. See the formulas and the CNC example in the measurement section.

### What is the difference between changeover time and setup time?

In everyday use, they are often the same, although some sources separate them explicitly [23]. Strictly, many practitioners use "setup time" for the exchange itself and "changeover time" for the whole loss from last good part to first good part, including run-down and run-up. Use the wider definition for improvement work.

### What is a good changeover time?

Shingo's aspiration is under ten minutes [1]. A practical answer: a good changeover time is stable (narrow range), safe, and short enough to support the lot sizes and EPEI your customers need. For large press lines, 20 minutes can be excellent; for a small machine, it can be slow.

### What is OTED?

One-touch exchange of die: a changeover in under 100 seconds [5]. It is the next target after SMED, usually reached only with dedicated quick-change hardware.

### How does SMED relate to OEE?

Changeovers are usually booked as availability losses and run-up scrap as quality losses in OEE [4]. Shorter changeovers raise OEE availability only if the time saved is used to produce. If it is used for more frequent changeovers, OEE may stay flat while inventory and lead time improve.

### Does SMED require lockout/tagout?

In the US, setting up is part of "servicing and/or maintenance" under OSHA's lockout/tagout standard, 29 CFR 1910.147, so internal changeover steps on equipment with hazardous energy fall under it by default [8]. A narrow exception covers minor tool changes during normal production that are routine, repetitive and integral, and only with effective alternative protection [8][9]. Have your safety professional decide which steps qualify.

### What is EPEI and how does SMED change it?

EPEI (every part every interval; LEI: EPEx) is the time in which a process produces all its part numbers once [3]. It depends on changeover times and the number of parts. A common formula is total changeover time for all parts divided by the daily time left after run time. In the example in this guide, cutting changeovers from 200 to 70 minutes per cycle reduces EPEI from 2 days to 1 day and halves the lots.

### Why do changeover improvements fail over time?

Because the standard is not trained to all shifts, the changeover cart loses its tools, quick-change hardware is not maintained, or nobody watches the KPI. Research identified sustainment as a central weakness of SMED practice [13]. Practitioner sources point to the same causes, such as inconsistent execution and equipment that is not ready [22]. Standard work, audits and a daily changeover KPI on the board are the countermeasures.

### Is SMED part of TPM?

No. SMED and TPM are separate lean methods, but they depend on each other. TPM keeps dies, molds and quick-change hardware in condition, and leanproduction.com recommends starting with TPM when breakdowns, not changeovers, dominate lost time [19][29]. In most OEE schemes, both changeovers and breakdowns are booked as availability losses.

### Does SMED only apply to dies and presses?

No. The name comes from stamping presses, but LEI defines SMED as a process for changing over any production equipment from one part number to another in as little time as possible [1]. The same steps work for a line changeover in packaging, a mold change, a fixture change on a machining center or a product change on an assembly line.

### What is the difference between a SMED event and a SMED program?

A SMED event is a focused workshop, usually a kaizen event, on one machine or line. A SMED program is the plant-wide system behind it: a standard changeover template, trained teams, changeover KPIs on the shop floor board and a rollout plan from machine to machine. Events produce the first results; the program keeps them and spreads them (see the rollout roadmap).

### What industries use SMED?

Any industry with changeovers: automotive and metal stamping, plastics, machining, packaging, printing, electronics, food and beverage, and pharmaceuticals. A review of 130 SMED articles covered a wide range of sectors [14]. In process industries, cleaning and mandated checks often dominate, so SMED is combined with sequencing and campaign planning.

### How long does a SMED project take?

A first SMED cycle on one machine often takes about three to four months from baseline to a stable new standard: a few weeks of preparation and baseline, a workshop of three to five days, several weeks for hardware and training, and a month of stabilization [unverified; practitioner estimate]. Hardware lead times can stretch this.

### How much does SMED cost?

The first cycle is often cheap: team time, checklists, carts, color coding and spacer blocks. Quick-change hardware such as multi-couplings, hydraulic clamping or preheating stations costs more and should come after organizational steps. The worked ROI example in this guide estimates a payback of about 1.5 years for a molding machine; your numbers will differ.

### Can SMED work in a high-mix, low-volume job shop?

Yes, but with a different focus. When most setups are unique, standardize what repeats: workholding, tool libraries, offline programming, presetting and part families. Analyze the setup process, not one specific setup.

## Conclusion and first steps

SMED is one of the most practical lean methods because its logic is simple and its results are measurable: do not stop the machine for work that could be done while it runs, and make what must happen during the stop simple, safe and free of adjustment. Shingo's insight from the 1950s and 1960s still holds, and the first big gains still come from checklists, staging and parallel work rather than from expensive hardware.

The method only pays off when the saved time is used. A shorter changeover is potential capacity or potential flexibility; the plant has to decide which, then change lot sizes, production runs, schedules and ERP parameters to match customer demand. The numbers in this guide show why it is worth it: in the EPEI example, cutting changeover time per cycle from 200 to 70 minutes halves lot sizes, and in the lot-size formula, a quarter of the setup cost means half the economic lot.

The limits deserve attention too. Organizational SMED reaches a ceiling where equipment and product design take over, gains fade without standards and audits, and speed must never come at the expense of lockout/tagout or ergonomics.

**Your first steps in the next 30 days**

- [ ] Pick one machine with frequent changeovers of 30 to 90 minutes and visible business pain
- [ ] Record start and end of every changeover for two weeks (last good part to first good part)
- [ ] Agree with planning how saved time will be used: capacity, smaller lots or a split
- [ ] Review the energy control procedure of that machine with your safety professional
- [ ] Film one changeover with the crew's agreement and build the element list
- [ ] Mark every element internal or external and introduce a pre-changeover checklist
- [ ] Calculate the current EPEI and the lot sizes it implies
- [ ] Put the changeover time on the shop floor board with a target and a reason column
- [ ] Check whether your state's NIST MEP center offers a quick changeover workshop [18][31]
