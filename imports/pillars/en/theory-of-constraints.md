# Theory of Constraints: The Five Focusing Steps, Bottlenecks and Drum-Buffer-Rope

*Updated: November 20, 2026 · Reading time: about 50 minutes (quick start: short answer, "Key takeaways" box, the worked plant example and the FAQ)*

> **Short answer:** The theory of constraints (TOC) is a management approach developed by Eliyahu M. Goldratt and introduced in the 1984 business novel *The Goal*. It says that the output of any system is limited by very few constraints, often a single bottleneck, so improvement effort should go there first. TOC puts this into practice with five focusing steps (identify, exploit, subordinate, elevate, prevent inertia), the drum-buffer-rope scheduling method and throughput accounting [1][4][6].

> **Key takeaways**
> - *The Goal* by Eliyahu M. Goldratt and Jeff Cox was first published in 1984 by North River Press; by 2011 it had sold more than 5 million copies in 35 languages [1][2].
> - The five focusing steps are: identify the constraint, exploit it, subordinate everything else to it, elevate it, and prevent inertia from becoming the constraint [4].
> - An hour lost at the constraint is an hour lost for the whole plant. An hour saved at a non-constraint changes nothing unless it relieves the constraint.
> - Drum-buffer-rope (DBR) schedules the constraint (drum), protects it with a time buffer (buffer) and releases material only at the pace the constraint can absorb (rope) [6][8].
> - Throughput accounting judges decisions by throughput (sales minus truly variable cost), inventory/investment and operating expense, and ranks products by throughput per constraint minute, which can reverse the ranking that standard cost accounting gives [6][19][20].
> - Bottleneck detection should be data-driven: a capacity load check, queue observation and, where machine state data exist, the active period method, which also shows shifting bottlenecks [13][14][15].
> - A 2003 review of more than 80 published TOC applications found large operational and financial gains and no reported failures, which also says something about which cases get published [10].
> - TOC, lean and Six Sigma work best together: TOC says where to focus, lean removes waste and creates flow, Six Sigma reduces variation at the spot that matters.

## What is the theory of constraints? Definition and core idea

The theory of constraints is a way of managing an organization as a chain of dependent steps. Its starting point is simple: every system that has a goal has at least one factor that limits how much of that goal it can reach. The TOC Institute uses the chain analogy: a chain is only as strong as its weakest link, and strengthening any other link does not make the chain stronger [4]. Goldratt's later books and the TOC community extended the idea from the shop floor to distribution, projects, finance and strategy [6].

For a manufacturing plant, the goal is usually stated as making money now and in the future. The limiting factor can be a machine, a skill, a supplier, a policy or the market itself. TOC calls it the constraint. The core claim is that most systems have only one or very few constraints at any time. The TOC Institute states that most systems have one capacity-constrained resource and rarely more than two or three [4].

### The core idea in one paragraph

If the output of a plant is limited by one resource, then the output of the plant equals the output of that resource. Every minute that resource loses is a minute of output the plant loses for good. Every minute saved somewhere else is, at best, spare capacity, and at worst it creates more inventory that waits in front of the constraint. This is why TOC says that local efficiencies at non-constraints do not add up to plant performance. Trying to optimize every step separately is wasted effort; TOC improves the whole system through its constraint.

### Theory of constraints in manufacturing: why it matters in practice

Three everyday plant situations show the idea:

| Situation | Typical reaction without TOC | Reaction with TOC |
|---|---|---|
| Orders are late, WIP is everywhere | expedite, add overtime everywhere, start orders earlier | find the one resource that limits output, release work at its pace |
| A non-bottleneck machine is idle | find work for it to keep "efficiency" high | let it wait; idle time at a non-constraint is normal and cheap |
| A new machine is proposed | approve because the department is busy | ask whether it adds capacity at the constraint and what it does to throughput |

### Benefits of the theory of constraints

The benefits most often reported are shorter lead times, better due-date performance, lower inventory and higher throughput. A review of published applications by Mabin and Balderstone, as summarized by Panizzolo, reported mean improvements of 70 percent in order-to-delivery lead time, 65 percent in manufacturing cycle time, 49 percent in inventory, 63 percent in throughput or revenue and 44 percent in due-date performance [11]. Panizzolo attributes these means to Mabin and Balderstone's book-length review *The World of the Theory of Constraints* (2000); the 2003 journal article by the same authors analyzes the same body of more than 80 cases [10][11]. Treat those figures as the upper end of what is possible: they come from published success stories, and the original review found no reports of failure at all [10]. Section 19 discusses what that means.

Beyond the numbers, TOC gives a plant a shared answer to the question "where should we work first?" That is often the biggest change: fewer process improvement projects, aimed at the place where they change the result.

### The Goal: a novel as a textbook

*The Goal* tells the story of Alex Rogo, a plant manager whose plant has three months to turn around. His former physics professor, Jonah, guides him through questions rather than answers [7]. The most quoted scene is a scout hike: the troop can only move as fast as its slowest boy, Herbie, and the gaps in the line grow as long as the fast walkers set the pace. Rogo's fix is to put Herbie at the front, lighten his backpack and tie the group together. That is the five focusing steps and drum-buffer-rope in one afternoon: identify Herbie, exploit him (lighter pack), subordinate the troop to his pace, and keep the line short.

The novel format made the ideas accessible to supervisors and operators, not just engineers. North River Press reported in 2011 that the book had sold more than 5 million copies in 35 languages [2].

### From OPT to a management philosophy

TOC grew in stages. The first stage was scheduling (OPT, then DBR). The second was measurement (throughput accounting). The third was a general problem-solving method (the thinking processes). The fourth applied the logic to projects (critical chain) and distribution (replenishment) [6][12]. Today the Theory of Constraints International Certification Organization (TOCICO), a US 501(c)(3) nonprofit, certifies practitioners in three levels: fundamentals exams, practitioner exams and an implementer assessment [5].

## Constraint vs. bottleneck: types of constraints

The words "constraint" and "bottleneck" are often used as synonyms. They are not quite the same.

- A **bottleneck** is a resource whose capacity is less than the demand placed on it. It is a capacity term.
- A **constraint** is whatever limits the system's performance toward its goal. It can be a bottleneck, but it can also be the market, a material, a policy or a way of thinking.
- A **capacity constrained resource (CCR)** is a resource that is not a bottleneck on average but becomes one if it is not managed carefully, for example because of peaks in the product mix.

Every bottleneck limits throughput only if it sits on the path of the orders that matter. A plant can have a "bottleneck" machine that is overloaded with low-priority work while the real constraint is the market. TOC therefore asks first: what limits the goal?

### Types of constraints

| Type | Example | How you notice it | Typical first move |
|---|---|---|---|
| Physical: equipment | a 5-axis machining center, a heat-treat furnace, a paint line | permanent queue in front, idle resources behind | exploit: no lost minutes |
| Physical: people and skills | only two certified welders per shift | work waits for specific people | cross-training, schedule around skills |
| Material | a casting with 16-week lead time | assembly waits for one part | replenishment buffer, second source |
| Market | demand below plant capacity | spare capacity everywhere, sales below plan | the constraint is outside; improve offer and lead time |
| Policy | "run full batches only", "keep every machine above 85% efficiency" | rules that force wrong behavior | thinking processes, change the rule |
| Paradigm | "inventory is an asset", "a busy plant is a good plant" | resistance to idle non-constraints | education, simulation games |

In practice, policy constraints deserve special attention: a physical constraint is often held back by a rule (minimum batches, release rules, utilization targets) rather than by its real capacity. The worked example in Section 5 shows two such rules.

### Static, dynamic and shifting bottlenecks

A **static** bottleneck stays on the same resource for months. A **dynamic** or **shifting** bottleneck moves between resources with product mix, breakdowns or staffing. The difference matters for method choice: a static bottleneck can be run with a fixed DBR setup, while a shifting bottleneck needs data and often an APS to calculate the load per resource for the current order book [13][14]. Section 6 shows how to tell them apart.

### VATI: plant types shape the constraint

TOC classifies plants by the shape of their material flow, known as VATI analysis [6][9]:

| Type | Flow shape | Example | Typical problem |
|---|---|---|---|
| V | few raw materials diverge into many products | steel, paper, chemicals, extrusion | misallocation: material committed to the wrong product too early |
| A | many parts converge into few products | aerospace assembly, machine building | parts arrive unsynchronized, assembly waits for the last part |
| T | few components, assembled late into many variants | electronics, appliances | parts stolen between variants, finished goods mismatch |
| I | straight line | simple machining lines | classic bottleneck behavior |

Most real plants are combinations. The plant type tells you where buffers belong: before assembly in an A plant, before divergence in a V plant.

## The five focusing steps

The five focusing steps are the core procedure of TOC. The TOC Institute words them as follows: identify the system's constraint, exploit the constraint, subordinate everything else to the constraint, elevate the constraint, and prevent inertia from becoming the constraint [4]. Goldratt called the cycle the process of ongoing improvement (POOGI), which is also the subtitle of later editions of *The Goal* [6]. Because step 5 leads back to step 1, the five steps form a continuous improvement cycle: once one system constraint is broken, a new constraint appears elsewhere, and the work starts again.

Before step 1, two questions need an answer: what is the goal of the system, and how do we measure progress toward it? In a business, TOC uses throughput, inventory (or investment) and operating expense as the measures (Section 11).

| Step | Question | What it means in a plant | What it does not mean |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Identify | What limits throughput right now? | find the resource, material, market or policy that sets the pace | picking the machine with the most breakdowns or the highest price |
| 2. Exploit | How do we get the most out of it with what we have? | no lost minutes, no defective work, the right product mix | buying capacity |
| 3. Subordinate | How does everything else support step 2? | release, batch sizes, maintenance, quality and staffing follow the constraint | keeping every resource busy |
| 4. Elevate | If still not enough, how do we add capacity? | overtime, outsourcing, a second machine, a new supplier | the first reflex |
| 5. Prevent inertia | Has the constraint moved? Are old rules now the constraint? | go back to step 1, review rules made for the old constraint | declaring victory |

### Step 1: Identify the constraint

The constraint is the resource whose capacity is lowest relative to the demand placed on it. In practice, start with a load calculation (demand times process time against available time), then confirm on the floor: where does work queue, where do people wait for a resource, which resource are supervisors always asking about? The TOC Institute adds that a wrongly identified constraint is easy to correct without lasting damage, which is a good reason not to wait for perfect data [4]. Section 6 covers six detection methods.

### Step 2: Exploit the constraint

Exploiting means making the constraint as productive as possible without spending significant money. The TOC Institute states that a constraint is often used less than 50 percent of the time on a 24/7 basis and that the first three steps typically reveal at least 30 percent hidden capacity within a few months; both are claims without a published data set, so verify them in your own plant [4]. Typical exploitation measures:

- cover breaks, shift changes and meetings so the constraint keeps running;
- move setup preparation outside the constraint's running time (external setup, see the [SMED and changeover reduction](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/smed-changeover-reduction/) guide);
- inspect before the constraint so it never processes a part that is already scrap (in AS9100 or IATF 16949 environments, moving an inspection point means updating the control plan and PFMEA);
- check quality directly after the constraint, because a part scrapped later costs constraint time;
- keep a buffer of work in front of it so it never starves;
- give it priority for maintenance, programming, tooling and material handling;
- choose the product mix by throughput per constraint minute (Section 11);
- offload work that other resources can do, even less efficiently.

### Step 3: Subordinate everything else

Subordination is the hardest step because it goes against local efficiency metrics. Non-constraints must have spare capacity, and they should use it to protect the constraint, not to build inventory. Examples:

- **Release:** material enters the plant at the pace of the constraint (the rope in DBR).
- **Batch sizes:** non-constraints can run small batches and more setups because they have spare capacity; the constraint may need larger batches to save setup time.
- **Maintenance:** a breakdown at the constraint gets the next technician; preventive maintenance at the constraint is scheduled in planned stops.
- **Quality:** first-piece approval at the constraint has priority over other first-piece checks.
- **Measurement:** stop rewarding non-constraint departments for utilization or "earned hours."

The TOC Institute notes that frequently shifting constraints disrupt policies, procedures and people [4]. Subordination should therefore include protecting the constraint's position, for example by keeping other resources well above the constraint's capacity.

### Step 4: Elevate the constraint

Only after steps 2 and 3 have been taken should you spend money on capacity. Elevation options include overtime or an extra shift at the constraint only, outsourcing part of its work, adding people, buying equipment or redesigning the product to need less constraint time. Many plants skip straight to this step and buy a machine while the existing constraint still loses a quarter of its time to breaks, setups and starvation.

### Step 5: Prevent inertia from becoming the constraint

When exploitation and elevation work, the constraint often moves, and a new constraint appears: at another machine, at a supplier, or in the market. Rules that were right for the old constraint now cause damage. Typical inertia: the old bottleneck's large batch size stays in the routing, a release rule tied to the old drum stays in the ERP, or the sales team keeps rationing orders although the plant now has spare capacity. Go back to step 1 and review the rules.

## Theory of constraints example: the five focusing steps in a manifold plant

The following example is constructed for this guide. The numbers are plausible for a small US machine shop but they are not data from a real company.

### The plant

A 60-person plant in the Midwest makes hydraulic manifold blocks in two product families:

- **Product A** (standard manifold): price $900, raw material and purchased parts $400.
- **Product B** (custom manifold): price $1,200, raw material and purchased parts $450.

The plant runs two 8-hour shifts, five days a week: 80 hours or 4,800 minutes of available time per resource per week. Weekly demand is 100 units of A and 60 units of B. Operating expense (all labor, salaries, rent, energy, depreciation; everything except truly variable material) is $62,000 per week.

### Step 1: Identify the constraint with a load table

Process times in minutes per unit, from the routings and verified by timing on the floor:

| Resource | A (min/unit) | B (min/unit) | Load A (100 units) | Load B (60 units) | Total load (min) | Load vs. 4,800 min |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Saw | 8 | 10 | 800 | 600 | 1,400 | 29% |
| 5-axis CNC cell | 25 | 40 | 2,500 | 2,400 | 4,900 | **102%** |
| Deburr and wash | 12 | 12 | 1,200 | 720 | 1,920 | 40% |
| Assembly | 15 | 20 | 1,500 | 1,200 | 2,700 | 56% |
| Pressure test | 10 | 15 | 1,000 | 900 | 1,900 | 40% |

**Calculation for the CNC cell:** 100 × 25 + 60 × 40 = 2,500 + 2,400 = 4,900 minutes against 4,800 available, a load of 4,900 ÷ 4,800 = 102 percent. Even on paper, the CNC cell cannot meet demand.

The load table only uses theoretical time. The real picture is worse. A two-week time study at the CNC cell (shift log plus machine state data) showed where the 4,800 minutes went:

| Use of CNC time per week | Minutes | Share |
|---|---|---|
| Productive machining of good parts | 3,600 | 75.0% |
| Breaks and shift handover not covered (2 shifts × 30 min × 5 days) | 300 | 6.3% |
| Changeovers (15 per week × 30 min) | 450 | 9.4% |
| Starved: waiting for material, programs or fixtures | 250 | 5.2% |
| Unplanned downtime | 100 | 2.1% |
| Machining parts later scrapped at pressure test | 100 | 2.1% |
| **Total** | **4,800** | **100%** |

So the effective capacity of the constraint is 3,600 minutes per week, against a demand of 4,900. The plant can meet about 73 percent of demand (3,600 ÷ 4,900).

The floor confirmed the numbers: an average of 2.5 days of work waited in front of the CNC cell, while assembly and pressure test regularly waited for parts. Supervisors had pointed to assembly as the bottleneck because it was "always behind," but assembly was behind because it was starved by the CNC cell.

### Step 2a: Exploit through the product mix

With 3,600 effective minutes, the plant cannot make everything. Which products should it make first? Here throughput accounting and standard cost accounting give different answers (Section 11 explains the logic in detail).

| | Product A | Product B |
|---|---|---|
| Price | $900 | $1,200 |
| Truly variable cost (material) | $400 | $450 |
| Throughput per unit (T) | $500 | $750 |
| CNC minutes per unit | 25 | 40 |
| **Throughput per CNC minute** | **$20.00** | **$18.75** |
| Standard cost margin per unit (see Section 11) | $161.20 | $280.52 |

Product B looks better per unit, under both methods. But per constraint minute, A earns more.

- **Mix 1, B first (what the cost report suggests):** 60 B use 2,400 min; the remaining 1,200 min make 48 A. Throughput = 60 × $750 + 48 × $500 = $45,000 + $24,000 = **$69,000**. Net profit = $69,000 − $62,000 = **$7,000 per week**.
- **Mix 2, A first (throughput per constraint minute):** 100 A use 2,500 min; the remaining 1,100 min make 27 B (27 × 40 = 1,080 min). Throughput = 100 × $500 + 27 × $750 = $50,000 + $20,250 = **$70,250**. Net profit = $70,250 − $62,000 = **$8,250 per week**.

The constraint-based mix earns $1,250 more per week, about $65,000 per year at 52 weeks, without any investment. It also changes the conversation with sales: B customers need longer quoted lead times or a price that reflects B's use of the constraint.

### Step 2b: Exploit through lost time

The team then attacked the losses in the time study:

| Measure | Cost | Minutes recovered per week |
|---|---|---|
| Stagger breaks; a trained relief operator keeps the cell running | $1,500 per week (part of a relief operator) | 300 |
| External setup preparation: preset tools, fixtures and programs staged before the machine stops; changeover 30 to 18 min | about $6,000 one-time for a tool presetter and carts | 180 (15 × 12 min) |
| Material and programs staged one shift ahead (a time buffer, see Section 7) | none | 200 |
| Critical bore dimensions checked right after machining instead of at pressure test | gauges, about $2,500 one-time | 70 |
| Preventive maintenance moved to the planned weekend window | none | 50 |
| **Total** | | **800** |

Effective capacity rises from 3,600 to 4,400 minutes per week, a 22 percent increase (800 ÷ 3,600). With Mix 2, the plant now makes 100 A (2,500 min) and 47 B (47 × 40 = 1,880 min, total 4,380 min).

Throughput = 100 × $500 + 47 × $750 = $50,000 + $35,250 = **$85,250 per week**. Operating expense rises by the relief operator: $62,000 + $1,500 = $63,500. Net profit = $85,250 − $63,500 = **$21,750 per week**, up from $8,250. The one-time spending of about $8,500 is recovered in less than a week.

This is the typical TOC pattern: because operating expense is mostly fixed in the short run, every extra constraint minute flows almost fully into profit.

### Step 3: Subordinate the rest of the plant

To keep the 800 minutes, the rest of the plant had to change:

- The saw stopped cutting "to keep busy." It now cuts only what the CNC schedule needs, released one shift ahead (the rope).
- The CNC cell runs a fixed weekly sequence that groups products by fixture family; the saw and deburr follow that sequence.
- Maintenance answers CNC calls first; the response target is 10 minutes.
- The quality lab does CNC first-piece checks before any other request.
- The saw and deburr supervisors are no longer measured on utilization. Their measure is "CNC never waits for us."

Utilization at the saw falls from about 45 percent (it was building inventory) to 29 percent. That is intended. In TOC terms, the saw's idle time is the price of a protected constraint.

### Step 4: Elevate the constraint

After exploitation, 13 units of B per week are still unmet (60 − 47). Three elevation options:

| Option | Extra constraint minutes | Extra throughput per week | Extra operating expense per week | Net effect per week | Comment |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Saturday shift at the CNC cell only (8 h, staffed by voluntary sign-up or the overtime rules of the labor agreement) | 480 | 12 B × $750 = $9,000 | $1,200 overtime premium and support | **+$7,800** | quick, reversible |
| Outsource rough machining of B to a job shop, $180 per unit | 13 B do not need the full 40 min | 13 × ($750 − $180) = $7,410 | coordination, about $300 | **+$7,110** | T per unit falls to $570 because the job shop cost is truly variable |
| Buy a second 5-axis machine, $650,000 | 4,800 | 13 B × $750 = $9,750 (demand limits the gain) | $4,000 (operator, depreciation, service) | **+$5,750** | payback about $650,000 ÷ $5,750 = 113 weeks, unless demand grows |

The Saturday shift wins. The second machine only makes sense if sales can fill it, because once it is installed, the constraint moves to the market. The finance check is simple: does the option raise T minus OE, and by how much relative to the investment? Section 11 shows the formulas.

### Step 5: Prevent inertia

With the Saturday shift, the CNC cell has 4,880 effective minutes, enough for the full demand of 4,900 minus a few minutes, and demand is now the limit. New load picture:

| Resource | Load at full demand | Load vs. available |
|---|---|---|
| CNC cell (with Saturday) | 4,900 | about 100% of effective time |
| Assembly | 2,700 | 56% |
| Pressure test | 1,900 | 40% |

The constraint is about to move to the market. Two inertia traps came up in the review:

1. The ERP still held the old minimum batch of 40 for B, set years ago to save CNC setups. With faster changeovers it was blocking short lead times. The batch was cut to 10.
2. Sales was still quoting six weeks for B "because the plant is always full." Quoted lead time was cut to three weeks, which won two new B customers within a quarter.

Back to step 1: if the new B customers push demand up, the CNC cell becomes the constraint again and the second-machine option gets a new calculation. That loop is the process of ongoing improvement.

### What the example teaches

| Lesson | Evidence in the example |
|---|---|
| The visible bottleneck may not be the constraint | supervisors blamed assembly, which was starved |
| Exploitation beats investment | 800 minutes for $8,500 one-time plus $1,500 a week, versus $650,000 |
| Cost accounting can rank products wrong | B looked better per unit, A earns more per constraint minute |
| Non-constraints should have idle time | the saw went from 45% to 29% utilization |
| Old rules outlive old constraints | the batch of 40 and the six-week quote |

## Bottleneck analysis: how to identify the bottleneck (six methods)

Bottleneck analysis is the work of finding the resource that limits throughput, measuring how much output it costs and checking whether it shifts. It is step 1 of the five focusing steps, and it should be repeated whenever the product mix, demand or the production schedule changes.

The bottleneck is not always the most expensive machine, the one with the most breakdowns or the department with the loudest supervisor. Use more than one method and check that they agree.

### Method 1: Capacity load calculation

For each resource, compare the time demand requires with the time available.

**Formula:** Load (%) = Σ (demand per product × process time per unit, including setups) ÷ available time × 100

The resource with the highest load is the bottleneck candidate. Section 5 shows the calculation; the CNC cell was at 102 percent on theoretical times. Two refinements make the method reliable:

1. **Use effective, not nominal, time.** Divide the theoretical load by the share of time the resource actually produces good parts (availability × quality, or a measured productive share). A resource at 85 percent load on paper and 75 percent productive share is at 113 percent in reality (85 ÷ 0.75).
2. **Use the real product mix for the period you plan.** A resource can be the bottleneck in a B-heavy week and not in an A-heavy week.

Strength: needs only routings, demand and a calendar. Weakness: routings are often wrong, and the method ignores variability and shifting bottlenecks.

### Method 2: Queue and WIP observation

Work piles up in front of a bottleneck, and resources after it wait. Count the work in front of each resource (in hours of work, not pieces) at the same time on several days. A resource with a large, persistent queue and starved resources downstream is the likely bottleneck. Caution: a queue can also sit in front of a resource that is fed in big batches or that has a policy of building ahead.

### Method 3: The bottleneck walk

Christoph Roser recommends the bottleneck walk as the shop floor method when machine state data are missing [15]. Walk the line several times per shift and note for each process whether it is working, starved (waiting for input) or blocked (cannot pass work on). A process that is mostly working, with blocked processes before it and starved processes after it, is the bottleneck. Repeat over days to see whether it shifts.

| Walk at | Saw | CNC cell | Deburr | Assembly | Test |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 07:30 | blocked | working | starved | starved | working |
| 09:00 | working | working | working | starved | starved |
| 10:30 | blocked | working | starved | working | starved |
| 13:00 | blocked | down (tool) | starved | starved | starved |
| Pattern | often blocked | always working | often starved | often starved | often starved |

### Method 4: Utilization (percent active)

Machine data from an MES, IIoT platform or the machine control show the share of time each resource is active. The resource with the highest share of active time is the bottleneck candidate. Roser, Nakano and Tanaka call this the workload method and use it as a comparison in their 2001 paper [13]. It is easy to automate but blind to the order in which resources interrupt each other: two resources at 85 percent look the same even if one of them stops the other.

### Method 5: The active period method

The active period method was published by Roser, Nakano and Tanaka at the 2001 Winter Simulation Conference and extended to shifting bottlenecks in 2002 [13][14]. It needs a time-stamped state log for each resource.

- **Active states** are states in which the resource can make others wait: processing, but also changeover, breakdown, repair and maintenance [14][15].
- **Inactive states** are states in which the resource waits for others: starved (waiting for material or transport) and blocked [14][15].
- An **active period** is an uninterrupted run of active states [13].
- At any moment, **the resource with the longest uninterrupted active period is the momentary bottleneck** [14].
- Where the active period of the current bottleneck overlaps with that of the previous or next bottleneck, the bottleneck is **shifting** between the two; outside the overlaps, the resource is the **sole** bottleneck [14].

**Worked example (two hours of an illustrative shift log):**

| Resource | Active periods | Inactive periods |
|---|---|---|
| Saw | 08:00–08:20, 08:35–08:55, 09:10–09:30, 09:45–10:00 | blocked between them |
| CNC cell | 08:00–09:05 (65 min), 09:15–10:00 (45 min) | 09:05–09:15 starved |
| Assembly | 08:00–08:10, 08:30–09:10 (40 min), 09:40–10:00 | starved 08:10–08:30 and 09:10–09:40 |

Step by step:

1. 08:00–09:05: the CNC cell's 65-minute active period is the longest running period, so the CNC cell is the momentary bottleneck.
2. 09:05–09:10: the CNC cell is starved. The longest active period at this time is assembly's (08:30–09:10). Assembly is the bottleneck.
3. 09:10–09:15: only the saw is active (09:10–09:30). The saw is the bottleneck.
4. 09:15–10:00: the CNC cell's second period (45 minutes) is the longest. The CNC cell is the bottleneck.
5. Overlaps: the CNC period 08:00–09:05 overlaps with the assembly period 08:30–09:10 for 35 minutes (08:30–09:05), so the bottleneck shifts between CNC and assembly there. The saw period 09:10–09:30 overlaps with the CNC period 09:15–10:00 for 15 minutes, a shift between saw and CNC.

| Resource | Sole bottleneck | Shifting bottleneck | Sum | Share of 120 min (sole / sum) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| CNC cell | 08:00–08:30 and 09:30–10:00 = 60 min | 35 + 15 = 50 min | 110 min | 50% / 92% |
| Assembly | 09:05–09:10 = 5 min | 35 min | 40 min | 4% / 33% |
| Saw | 09:10–09:15 = 5 min | 15 min | 20 min | 4% / 17% |

Roser interprets the sole share as a lower bound and the sum as an upper bound for the effect of an improvement: if the CNC cell is the sole bottleneck 50 percent and the sole or shifting bottleneck 92 percent of the time, a one-minute gain at the CNC cell should improve system output by roughly 0.5 to 0.9 minutes [15]. Two hours of data are only enough to show the mechanics; a real analysis uses weeks of logs.

Compare with Method 4: the CNC cell was active 110 of 120 minutes (92 percent), the saw 80 (67 percent) and assembly 70 (58 percent). Utilization points to the same resource here, but it would rank the saw ahead of assembly although assembly is more often part of a shifting bottleneck.

**Where the data come from:** MES machine states, IIoT gateways reading the controller, or the event log of a discrete event simulation. Roser notes that the method is easiest with simulation or highly automated systems and recommends the bottleneck walk where such data are missing [15]. The [MES and ISA-95](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/mes-isa-95/) guide explains how plants collect equipment states.

### Method 6: Simulation and sensitivity tests

A discrete event simulation model of the line lets you test the question directly: if each resource in turn gets 10 percent more capacity, which change raises output? The resource whose improvement raises output most is the constraint for that mix. Simulation also shows shifting bottlenecks under variability, which static load tables miss. The [manufacturing simulation](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/digital-twin-manufacturing/manufacturing-simulation/) guide covers how to build such a model.

### Common causes of bottlenecks

A bottleneck analysis should end with a cause, not only a location. The usual causes in a plant:

- capacity below demand for the current product mix (a true capacity constraint)
- unplanned downtime and slow repairs at the critical resource
- long changeovers combined with large batch rules
- missing skills or staffing: only a few people can run or set up the resource
- quality losses: scrap and rework that use constraint time twice
- starvation: material, tools, programs or upstream work arriving late
- policies, such as utilization targets that keep non-constraints busy while work piles up in the wrong place

The cause decides the fix. The first five call for exploitation first (Section 4, step 2) and elevation only if that is not enough; starvation calls for subordination (step 3); policies call for a rule change, often with the thinking processes (Section 12).

### Which method when?

| Method | Data needed | Finds shifting bottlenecks | Effort | Best use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Capacity load | routings, demand, calendar | no | low | first check, planning, quoting |
| Queue observation | counts on the floor | partly | low | confirmation |
| Bottleneck walk | repeated walks | partly | low to medium | plants without machine data |
| Utilization | machine states | no | low once data flow | dashboards |
| Active period | time-stamped states | yes | medium | automated lines, MES data, simulation |
| Simulation | model plus data | yes | medium to high | high mix, investment decisions |

### Six questions that expose a false bottleneck

1. Does the resource really run during all available time, or is it often starved?
2. Is the queue in front of it permanent or only a batch arriving?
3. Do resources downstream wait for it, or for something else?
4. If it got 10 percent more capacity tomorrow, would shipments go up?
5. Is the "bottleneck" busy with work that is not needed now?
6. Is the real limit a rule, such as minimum batch sizes or a fixed shift pattern?

## Drum-buffer-rope scheduling with numbers

Drum-buffer-rope (DBR) is the TOC method for scheduling and controlling production. Goldratt and Robert E. Fox described it in *The Race* (1986), building on the scout troop metaphor of *The Goal* [3][8]. The *Theory of Constraints Handbook* describes the current form, including buffer management and VATI flow classification [9].

- **Drum:** the constraint's schedule. It is the only detailed schedule in the plant that must be kept.
- **Buffer:** time buffers that protect the throughput of the plant from variability. Traditional DBR uses buffers at the constraint, at assembly points where constraint parts meet non-constraint parts, and at shipping [6].
- **Rope:** the release mechanism. Material enters the plant one buffer time before it is needed at the constraint; releasing earlier only raises WIP [6].

Buffers in DBR are measured in **time**, not in pieces [6]. A 16-hour constraint buffer means that material is released 16 working hours before its scheduled start at the constraint.

### Step 1: Build the drum schedule

Back in the manifold plant, the CNC cell is the drum. Its schedule for Wednesday (shift 1: 06:00–14:00, shift 2: 14:00–22:00), with the 18-minute changeovers from Section 5:

| Order | Product and quantity | Setup + run (min) | CNC start | CNC end |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1041 | A × 10 | 18 + 250 = 268 | Wed 06:00 | Wed 10:28 |
| 1042 | B × 5 | 18 + 200 = 218 | Wed 10:28 | Wed 14:06 |
| 1043 | A × 10 | 268 | Wed 14:06 | Wed 18:34 |
| 1044 | B × 4 | 18 + 160 = 178 | Wed 18:34 | Wed 21:32 |

The sequence follows the product mix decision from Section 5 and the weekly demand, with batches small enough to keep lead times short. The 28 minutes left at the end of the shift go to a planned tool check.

### Step 2: Set the buffers

- **Constraint buffer:** 16 working hours (two shifts). The saw and material handling need less than one hour of touch time per order; the rest protects against saw problems, missing material and programs.
- **Shipping buffer:** 16 working hours after the CNC cell. Deburr, assembly and test need about one hour of touch time per order.
- **Physical buffer area:** a marked floor area in front of the CNC cell, sized for about one day of work, with FIFO lanes by fixture family. If the area overflows, release is ahead of the rope; if it is empty, the CNC cell is at risk.

How big should a buffer be at the start? Gray, Felan, Umble and Umble note that rules of thumb exist but that, in their experience, buffer sizes are usually set to achieve a stable system and then adjusted [24]; buffer management (Section 9) does the adjusting. A starting value often cited in TOC practice is about half of the current lead time for that segment [unverified]. In the example, work used to wait 2.5 days in front of the CNC cell; the team started with one day and planned to adjust.

### Step 3: Tie the rope

Release time = scheduled constraint start − constraint buffer.

| Order | CNC start | Constraint buffer | Release to saw | Ship date (CNC end + shipping buffer) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1041 | Wed 06:00 | 16 h | Tue 06:00 | Thu 10:28 |
| 1042 | Wed 10:28 | 16 h | Tue 10:28 | Thu 14:06 |
| 1043 | Wed 14:06 | 16 h | Tue 14:06 | Thu 18:34 |
| 1044 | Wed 18:34 | 16 h | Tue 18:34 | Thu 21:32 |

No material is released before its release time, however idle the saw is. This is what keeps WIP and lead time low.

### Step 4: Run the plant to buffer status

Non-constraints do not get a detailed schedule. They work in order of buffer status (Section 9): orders whose buffer is most consumed go first. The CNC cell follows its sequence.

### DBR vs. MRP push: what changes

| Topic | MRP push | DBR |
|---|---|---|
| Release | planned lead times per operation, often padded | constraint schedule minus buffer |
| Detailed schedules | for every work center | only for the constraint |
| WIP | grows when lead times are padded | limited by the rope |
| Priorities on the floor | hot lists, expediting | buffer status colors |
| Response to variability | more lead time, more WIP | buffers at a few points, sized and adjusted |

The [production scheduling and APS](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/production-scheduling-aps/) guide explains how DBR fits into finite capacity scheduling and when an APS adds value.

### DBR in assembly (A-type) plants

In an A plant, constraint parts meet non-constraint parts at assembly. Traditional DBR adds an **assembly buffer**: non-constraint parts are released so that they wait at assembly before the constraint part arrives, so the constraint's output never waits for a missing bracket [6][9]. In aerospace and machine building, where assemblies need hundreds of parts, this is often where DBR pays off most; the [aerospace production planning](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/aerospace-production-planning/) guide covers that environment.

## Simplified drum-buffer-rope (S-DBR) for make-to-order

Simplified DBR (S-DBR) was developed for make-to-order plants where the market, not an internal resource, is usually the constraint, or where the internal constraint is not severely loaded. It uses only one buffer, the **shipping buffer**, and replaces the detailed drum schedule with a **planned load** check on the capacity constrained resource [6][9].

### How S-DBR works

1. **Shipping buffer:** each order is released a fixed buffer time before its due date.
2. **Planned load:** the plant keeps a running total of the work already committed on the CCR, expressed in days.
3. **Due-date quoting:** a new order gets a due date no earlier than the date when the CCR can process it plus part of the shipping buffer for the work after the CCR. A common convention is the planned load date plus half the shipping buffer [unverified].
4. **Buffer management** sets priorities on the floor by the share of the shipping buffer consumed.

### Worked example: quoting a due date with S-DBR

Assume the manifold plant has become make-to-order and the market is the constraint most weeks. The CNC cell is treated as the CCR with 4,400 effective minutes per week, or 880 minutes per working day.

- Orders already committed on the CNC cell: 3,520 minutes. Planned load = 3,520 ÷ 880 = **4.0 working days**.
- Shipping buffer: **6 working days** (current total lead time is 12 days; the team starts with half).
- New order: 10 units of B, 40 minutes each = 400 minutes = 0.45 days on the CNC cell.

Safe due date = today + planned load + new order's CNC time + half the shipping buffer = 0 + 4.0 + 0.45 + 3.0 = **7.45, so 8 working days**. Release date = due date − shipping buffer = day 8 − 6 = **day 2**.

If sales wants to promise 5 days, the check shows the order would jump the queue on the CNC cell and push other orders late. That conversation happens at order entry, not on the shop floor two weeks later.

### DBR or S-DBR?

| Criterion | Traditional DBR | S-DBR |
|---|---|---|
| Constraint | internal and heavily loaded | market, or internal but moderately loaded |
| Buffers | constraint, assembly, shipping | shipping only |
| Constraint schedule | detailed sequence | planned load check |
| Effort | higher | lower |
| Typical fit | stable bottleneck, A or I plants | make-to-order job shops, high mix |

## Buffer management: the feedback loop

Buffer management is the control and improvement loop of DBR and S-DBR [9]. For each open order, the plant tracks how much of its buffer has been consumed and colors it accordingly. The Wikipedia summary of TOC describes the red, yellow and green coding by buffer penetration [6].

**Formula (for an order not yet at the protected point):**
Buffer penetration (%) = (1 − remaining time until scheduled arrival at the protected point ÷ buffer size) × 100

| Zone | Buffer penetration | Meaning | Response |
|---|---|---|---|
| Green | 0 to 33% | on track | none |
| Yellow | 33 to 67% | getting tight | check where the order is, prepare action |
| Red | 67 to 100% | the protected point may wait | expedite: move the order to the front, fix the cause |
| Black (some plants) | above 100% | already late | escalate, inform customer if a shipping buffer |

The split into thirds is a convention, not a law of nature.

### Worked example: buffer status at Wednesday 06:00

Constraint buffer: 16 working hours. None of the four orders has reached the CNC cell yet at the start of the shift.

| Order | Scheduled CNC start | Remaining working hours | Penetration | Zone | Action |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1041 | Wed 06:00 | 0 | 100% | red/black | CNC will wait; find the order now |
| 1042 | Wed 10:28 | 4.5 | 1 − 4.5 ÷ 16 = 72% | red | expedite at the saw |
| 1043 | Wed 14:06 | 8.1 | 1 − 8.1 ÷ 16 = 49% | yellow | check status |
| 1044 | Wed 18:34 | 12.6 | 1 − 12.6 ÷ 16 = 21% | green | none |

On a healthy day, orders due in the next few hours are already sitting in the buffer in front of the CNC cell. Order 1041 at 06:00 not being there means the CNC cell is about to lose time.

### Using buffer data to improve

Record the reason every time an order turns red: missing material, saw breakdown, program not released, quality hold. After a few weeks, a Pareto chart of the causes is the improvement list for the non-constraints. Two rules of thumb for sizing:

- If many orders are red, the buffer is too small or the upstream process too unstable. Fix the top causes first, then consider a larger buffer.
- If almost all orders stay green, the buffer is too big. Reduce it in steps; WIP and lead time fall with it.

For stock buffers in distribution, the dynamic buffer management rule summarized on Wikipedia is to reduce a buffer by one third if stock stays in the top third for a full replenishment time and to increase it by one third if it stays in the bottom third too long [6].

## Factory physics behind TOC: Little's Law and variability

TOC is sometimes taught as a philosophy. Its scheduling rules also follow from queueing theory.

### Little's Law

John D. C. Little proved in 1961 that, in a stable system, the average number of items in the system equals the arrival rate times the average time in the system [16]. In factory terms (Hopp and Spearman) [17]:

**WIP = throughput × cycle time**, or **cycle time = WIP ÷ throughput**

**Worked example (manifold plant, before and after DBR):**

| | Before (push) | After (DBR) |
|---|---|---|
| WIP between saw and shipping | 640 units | 90 units |
| Throughput | 127 units per week | 147 units per week |
| Cycle time = WIP ÷ throughput | 640 ÷ 127 = 5.0 weeks | 90 ÷ 147 = 0.61 weeks, about 3 working days |

WIP fell because the rope stopped early release; throughput rose because the constraint was exploited. Lead time fell by more than 80 percent. Little's Law also warns of the limit: if WIP in front of the constraint falls so far that it starves, throughput drops too. That is why DBR keeps a buffer at the constraint and nowhere else.

### Variability and utilization

Kingman's approximation for a single station shows why non-constraints need spare capacity [17][18]:

**Waiting time ≈ ((ca² + ce²) ÷ 2) × (u ÷ (1 − u)) × te**

where ca and ce are the coefficients of variation of arrivals and process times, u is utilization and te the mean process time. It is an approximation for a single station with general arrival and process time distributions (G/G/1) and is most accurate at high utilization [17][18].

| Utilization u | u ÷ (1 − u) | Waiting time with ca = ce = 1, te = 40 min |
|---|---|---|
| 70% | 2.3 | 93 min |
| 85% | 5.7 | 227 min |
| 95% | 19.0 | 760 min |

From 85 to 95 percent utilization, waiting time more than triples. A plant that pushes every resource toward 95 percent creates long queues everywhere. TOC's answer is to run the constraint at very high utilization, protected by a deliberate buffer, and to keep non-constraints well below it.

## Throughput accounting vs. cost accounting

Throughput accounting is the measurement system of TOC. Goldratt set out its three measures in *The Goal* and *The Haystack Syndrome* [20]; Thomas Corbett's *Throughput Accounting* (North River Press) is the standard book-length treatment [21].

### The three measures

| Measure | Definition (Goldratt, as summarized by MAAW) [20] | Practical definition in a plant |
|---|---|---|
| Throughput (T) | the rate at which the system generates money through sales | sales minus totally variable costs, usually raw material, purchased parts, commissions and outside processing per unit [6][22] |
| Inventory (I), later often "investment" | money the system invests in purchasing things it intends to sell | raw material, WIP and finished goods valued at material cost, plus, in the wider definition, equipment and buildings [22] |
| Operating expense (OE) | all the money the system spends in turning inventory into throughput | wages and salaries (including direct labor), rent, energy, maintenance, depreciation |

Direct labor is part of OE, not of T. That is the key difference from standard costing: in most plants, labor does not go up or down with each extra unit in the short run.

### Derived measures

| Measure | Formula | Manifold plant after exploitation (weekly) |
|---|---|---|
| Net profit (NP) | T − OE | $85,250 − $63,500 = $21,750 |
| Return on investment (ROI) | (T − OE) ÷ I | with I = $1.2 million: $21,750 × 52 ÷ $1,200,000 = 94% per year |
| Productivity | T ÷ OE | $85,250 ÷ $63,500 = 1.34 |
| Investment turns | T ÷ I | $85,250 × 52 ÷ $1,200,000 = 3.7 per year |

Lean Production's summary lists the same derived measures and the TOC decision priority: increase throughput first, then reduce investment, then reduce operating expense [22]. The investment figure of $1.2 million is an assumption for the example.

### Decision rule: throughput per constraint unit

When a constraint exists, products should be ranked by throughput per unit of constraint time, not by margin per unit. In accounting textbooks this is the familiar "contribution per unit of limiting factor"; the ACCA examining team describes the throughput version with the return per factory hour and the throughput accounting ratio (TPAR) [19]:

- **Return per factory hour** = throughput per unit ÷ hours per unit on the bottleneck
- **Cost per factory hour** = total factory cost ÷ total hours available on the bottleneck
- **TPAR** = return per factory hour ÷ cost per factory hour; it should normally be greater than 1 [19].

### Worked example: standard cost vs. throughput accounting

Standard costing in the manifold plant allocates operating expense to products by standard labor minutes. Budgeted standard minutes at demand: A 70 minutes per unit (8 + 25 + 12 + 15 + 10) × 100 units = 7,000; B 97 minutes (10 + 40 + 12 + 20 + 15) × 60 units = 5,820; total 12,820 minutes. Burden rate = $62,000 ÷ 12,820 = **$4.84 per standard minute**.

| | Product A | Product B |
|---|---|---|
| Price | $900.00 | $1,200.00 |
| Material | $400.00 | $450.00 |
| Labor and overhead (70 × $4.84; 97 × $4.84) | $338.80 | $469.48 |
| **Standard margin per unit** | **$161.20** | **$280.52** |
| Standard margin % | 17.9% | 23.4% |
| Throughput per unit | $500 | $750 |
| CNC hours per unit | 0.417 | 0.667 |
| **Return per CNC hour** | **$1,200** | **$1,125** |

Cost per factory hour, using the operating expense after exploitation: $63,500 ÷ (4,400 ÷ 60 = 73.3 CNC hours) = **$865.91**. TPAR A = 1,200 ÷ 865.91 = **1.39**; TPAR B = 1,125 ÷ 865.91 = **1.30**. Both products make money, but A makes more per constraint hour.

Standard costing says "push B": it has the higher margin per unit and in percent. Throughput accounting says "fill the constraint with A first." Section 5 showed the result: $1,250 more net profit per week with the throughput ranking. Standard costing is not wrong as arithmetic. It answers a different question (the average full cost of a unit over a period), and that question is not the one a product mix decision under a constraint needs.

### Worked example: an order that only uses spare capacity

A customer asks for 40 adapter plates per week at $260 each. Material is $150. The plates need the saw (15 minutes), deburr (10 minutes) and assembly (25 minutes), but **no CNC time**. Total: 50 standard minutes.

| | Standard cost view | Throughput view |
|---|---|---|
| Material | $150.00 | $150.00 |
| Labor and overhead (50 × $4.84) | $242.00 | not per unit: OE does not change, the resources have spare capacity |
| Margin per unit | $260 − $392 = **−$132** | T = $260 − $150 = **+$110** |
| Decision | reject | accept |
| Effect on weekly net profit (40 plates) | | +$4,400, as long as saw, deburr and assembly stay below their capacity |

Check the loads: saw +600 minutes (to 2,000, 42 percent), deburr +400 (to 2,320, 48 percent), assembly +1,000 (to 3,700, 77 percent). Assembly gets closer to becoming a constraint, so the order should be accepted with a volume limit and watched. Under standard costing, the plant would have turned away $4,400 a week.

### Inventory and reported profit

Under absorption costing, overhead is carried into inventory. If the manifold plant builds 50 extra units of A for stock, 50 × $338.80 = **$16,940** of the period's overhead moves into the balance sheet instead of the income statement, and reported profit rises by that amount. Cash falls by the material bought, 50 × $400 = $20,000. Under throughput accounting, net profit does not change and inventory rises by $20,000. This is why TOC practitioners warn that building inventory can make a plant look better on paper while it gets worse in cash and lead time.

External financial reporting in the US requires production overhead to be included in inventory cost (absorption costing): under ASC 330, inventory cost is the sum of the expenditures and charges directly or indirectly incurred to bring an item to its existing condition and location, and fixed production overhead is allocated to inventory based on the normal capacity of the production facilities [28][29]. Your controller decides how the plant applies this. Throughput accounting is therefore an internal decision tool. Most plants that use it keep GAAP statements for external reporting and reconcile the two.

### What the plant controller should watch

| Risk | Why it matters | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| "Truly variable" defined too narrowly or too broadly | T is overstated or understated | agree a written list: material, purchased parts, outside processing, freight-out if per unit, sales commissions |
| OE treated as fixed in the long run | large volume changes do change OE | re-check OE assumptions for decisions beyond a quarter |
| Ignoring the constraint's location | throughput per constraint minute is meaningless if the constraint moves | recompute when the load table changes |
| Pricing below full cost permanently | spare capacity disappears and OE must grow | volume caps, review each quarter |
| Two sets of numbers | confusion in management meetings | one reconciliation sheet; TA for decisions, GAAP for statements |
| Labor that really varies per unit | piece-rate pay or agency labor booked per unit is truly variable | include it in truly variable cost; regular wages stay in OE |

The [manufacturing KPIs](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/manufacturing-kpis/) guide covers inventory turns and other financial-operational measures.

## The thinking processes

The thinking processes (TP) are TOC's tools for problems that are not capacity problems: policy constraints, conflicts and change management. Goldratt's novel *It's Not Luck* (1994) brought them to a wide audience; Kim, Mabin and Davies reviewed the research literature on them from 1994, after that book, to early 2006 and found that TOC developed mainly through practice, with many application areas and multi-method approaches [12].

The TP answer three questions: what to change, what to change to, and how to cause the change.

| Tool | Question | Logic | Output |
|---|---|---|---|
| Current reality tree (CRT) | what to change? | cause and effect ("if ... then ...") from a few root causes to many undesirable effects (UDEs) | the core problem |
| Evaporating cloud (EC), conflict resolution diagram | what to change to? | necessity ("in order to ... we must ...") | the assumption that, once broken, resolves the conflict |
| Future reality tree (FRT) | what to change to? | sufficiency, with injections | the solution and its side effects (negative branches) |
| Prerequisite tree (PRT) | how to cause the change? | obstacles and intermediate objectives | the sequence of milestones |
| Transition tree (TrT) | how to cause the change? | action, reason, effect | a step-by-step plan |

### Worked example: evaporating cloud on batch size

At the manifold plant, the CNC supervisor and the sales manager argued about the batch size for B.

| Box | Content |
|---|---|
| A (common objective) | run a profitable plant |
| B (need 1) | use the CNC cell's capacity well |
| C (need 2) | deliver B orders fast |
| D (want 1, serves B) | run large B batches (40) to save setups |
| D' (want 2, serves C) | run small B batches (10) |
| Conflict | D and D' cannot both be done |

The assumption under B–D: "setups at the CNC cell take 30 minutes, so each extra setup costs 30 minutes of constraint time." The injection: cut the setup to 18 minutes with external preparation and group B variants by fixture family, so small batches cost little constraint time. The cloud "evaporates" because the assumption behind the conflict is no longer true.

### Worked example: a small current reality tree

Undesirable effects (UDEs) listed by the leadership team: late orders, high WIP, frequent expediting, overtime in assembly, supervisors fighting over priorities. Reading the tree from the bottom:

1. If supervisors are measured on utilization, then they release work early to keep their machines busy.
2. If work is released early, then WIP grows and queues in front of the CNC cell grow.
3. If queues are long, then the CNC cell runs whatever is on top, not what is due.
4. If the CNC cell runs the wrong orders, then due orders are late, and expediting and assembly overtime follow.

Core problem: the utilization measure (a policy constraint). The fix is not more capacity but a different measure and a release rule, which is DBR.

### Using the TP well

- Keep trees small at first. Five to ten UDEs are enough for one area.
- Check every arrow: does the cause really lead to the effect? Are there other causes?
- Work in a group that includes operators; they know the effects that managers do not see.
- The evaporating cloud is the easiest tool to start with and the most useful in daily conflicts.

## TOC vs. lean vs. Six Sigma

TOC, lean and Six Sigma are often presented as competitors. In practice they answer different questions.

| Topic | Theory of constraints | Lean | Six Sigma |
|---|---|---|---|
| Origin | Goldratt, 1980s, Israel and the US [1][3] | Toyota Production System, Japan | Motorola, 1980s, US [27] |
| Core question | where is the leverage? | where is the waste? | where is the variation? |
| Focus | the constraint and global throughput | flow and value for the customer across the value stream | defects and process capability |
| Main tools | five focusing steps, DBR, buffer management, throughput accounting, thinking processes | value stream mapping, 5S, SMED, kanban, standard work, kaizen | DMAIC, statistics, control charts, design of experiments |
| View of capacity | protect excess capacity at non-constraints | balance to takt, reduce overcapacity as waste | not central |
| View of inventory | buffers at a few chosen points, in time | minimize everywhere | not central |
| Financial view | throughput, inventory, operating expense | lead time, cost, quality | cost of poor quality |
| Typical weakness | narrow focus can ignore waste outside the constraint | applying tools everywhere at once | projects chosen without system view |

### How they work together

1. **TOC sets the focus.** The five focusing steps choose the place where improvement changes the plant result.
2. **Lean exploits the constraint.** SMED cuts constraint changeovers, TPM raises its availability, standard work and 5S stabilize it. See the [lean manufacturing](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/lean-manufacturing/), [SMED](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/smed-changeover-reduction/) and [TPM and OEE](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/tpm-oee/) guides.
3. **Six Sigma removes variation at and before the constraint.** Scrap at the constraint and late, unstable supply into its buffer are good DMAIC projects. See [Six Sigma and root cause analysis](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/six-sigma-root-cause-analysis/).
4. **TOC measures the result.** Throughput accounting shows what each project did to T, I and OE.

### Where lean and TOC really disagree

- **Line balancing.** Lean balances work content to takt. TOC deliberately keeps non-constraints unbalanced, with spare capacity, to protect the constraint. In a paced assembly line with takt, the line as a whole acts as the constraint; the disagreement matters more in job shops.
- **Buffers.** Lean treats inventory as waste and buffers as a temporary countermeasure. TOC treats a sized time buffer at the constraint as permanent protection. Both reduce total WIP sharply compared with a push system.
- **Pull mechanisms.** Kanban limits WIP between each pair of processes; DBR limits it between release and the constraint, similar to CONWIP, which Spearman, Woodruff and Hopp proposed as a pull alternative to kanban [23]. See the [kanban and pull system](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/kanban-pull-system/) guide.

### OEE at the constraint

OEE (availability × performance × quality) is most useful at the constraint, where every lost minute costs throughput. At a non-constraint, low OEE may simply mean it waits, as it should. If your plant reports one OEE number across all machines, separate the constraint.

## Beyond the plant: critical chain and replenishment

### Critical chain project management

In *Critical Chain* (1997), Goldratt applied TOC to projects [3]. The ideas in brief:

- **The critical chain** is the longest chain of dependent tasks, taking both task dependencies and resource dependencies into account. A critical path ignores resource conflicts.
- **Aggressive task estimates.** Safety time is removed from individual tasks.
- **Aggregated buffers.** The removed safety is pooled into a **project buffer** at the end and **feeding buffers** where non-critical chains join the critical chain.
- **Buffer management.** Progress is tracked with a fever chart: buffer consumed against chain completed.
- **No bad multitasking.** Resources finish one task before starting the next.

PMI's comparison of DBR and critical chain buffering describes the shared logic: both protect the system's constraint with aggregated time buffers rather than local safety at every step [24]. For tooling projects, plant expansions and new product introductions, critical chain is often the second TOC application after DBR.

### Replenishment and distribution

For make-to-stock items and spare parts, TOC replaces forecasts at each location with buffers that are replenished to actual consumption. The buffer is sized to the maximum expected consumption within the average replenishment time, plus a margin for unreliable supply; it is then adjusted by one third up or down depending on how long stock sits in the red or green zone [6]. The [manufacturing inventory management](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/production-scheduling-aps/manufacturing-inventory-management/) guide compares this with classic safety stock formulas, and the [S&OP and MRP](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/sop-mrp-production-planning/) guide shows where it fits into planning.

The roadmap below is for one plant or one value stream. It assumes a physical constraint inside the plant; if the analysis in month 1 shows that the market is the constraint, skip to S-DBR and the sales side.

| Mistake | Why it hurts | Better |
|---|---|---|
| Picking the constraint by gut feel | the visible problem is often a symptom (assembly was starved) | load table plus floor check, active period data if available |
| Jumping to step 4 | money spent while the constraint still loses 20 to 25% of its time | exploit and subordinate first |
| Keeping utilization targets at non-constraints | early release, more WIP, the constraint runs the wrong work | measure non-constraints on "the constraint never waits for us" |

## Criticism and limits of the theory of constraints

TOC has strong results in practice and weaker support in research. A serious guide has to cover both.

### 1. Evidence: success stories and publication bias

Mabin and Balderstone analyzed more than 80 published successful TOC applications and found significant operational and financial improvements; despite extensive searches, they found no reports of failures [10]. That is a warning as much as an endorsement. Published cases are written by consultants, vendors and enthusiastic companies; failed implementations rarely make it into print. Gupta and Snyder (2009) argued that TOC had not yet shown its effectiveness in the academic literature and called for studies that link implementation to financial performance, as summarized on Wikipedia [6]. Panizzolo's survey of 61 European plants found that some TOC practices, especially DBR, non-constraints with excess capacity and a constraint-based master schedule, were linked to better performance, but the results differed by country and relied on managers' own assessments [11].

### 2. Optimality and scheduling research

Operations researchers have criticized TOC heuristics as not optimal. Trietsch argued that DBR is inferior to competing methods, and Linhares (2009) argued that the TOC product-mix rule (rank by throughput per constraint unit) cannot guarantee optimal results in general, because the product-mix problem with several constraints is computationally hard [6]. The practical lesson: the "T per constraint minute" rule is reliable with one clear constraint. With two or more interacting constraints, use linear programming or an APS.

### 3. Originality

Critics have pointed out that many TOC ideas existed before: contribution per limiting factor in management accounting (Noreen, Smith and Mackey, 1995), elements of system dynamics and statistical process control, PERT/CPM in project management [6]. Goldratt himself credited earlier work, in particular the Toyota Production System and Ford's flow ideas, in his 2009 paper "Standing on the Shoulders of Giants" [6]. For a practitioner, originality matters less than whether the combination works; for a student, it is worth knowing where the pieces come from.

### 4. People and policy

Nave (2002) argued that TOC tends to ignore employees and does not treat unsuccessful policies as constraints, as summarized on Wikipedia [6]. In practice, the risk is that "subordinate" is read as "obey," and that the constraint's operators carry all the pressure. Section 20 covers this.

### 5. Where TOC fits less well

- **Balanced, paced lines** with takt and little variability: the line itself is the constraint, and lean line balancing is the better tool.
- **Highly shifting bottlenecks** in high-mix job shops: a single drum is hard to define. S-DBR, workload control or an APS fit better.
- **Plants where the market is the constraint** for years: internal DBR adds little; the leverage is in sales, lead time and offer design.
- **Measurement maturity:** throughput accounting needs a finance team willing to run a second view. Without it, decisions fall back to standard cost.

### 6. Commercial context

Much of the TOC literature is published by North River Press and by consultancies founded by Goldratt or his associates. That does not make it wrong, but it means many sources are not independent. This guide uses peer-reviewed sources where they exist and labels practitioner claims (such as the "30 percent hidden capacity" figure of the TOC Institute) as such [4].

## People and safety at the constraint

TOC can make work better or worse for the people at the constraint. The method itself is neutral; the way it is introduced decides.

### What operators notice first

- **More attention:** managers suddenly care about every minute at one machine. That can feel like control. Explain why, and let operators record the losses themselves.
- **Breaks:** "cover breaks" means a relief operator runs the machine, never that people skip breaks.
- **Idle time elsewhere:** operators at non-constraints may worry about their jobs when they are told not to build inventory. Make clear that spare capacity is intended and use it for training, setup improvement, 5S, cross-training for the constraint and quality work.
- **Recognition:** the constraint crew's improvements (like the 30-to-18-minute changeover) should be visible on the board, with names if they agree.

### Safety rules do not bend for the constraint

The pressure to keep the constraint running must never lead to shortcuts on safety. Servicing and maintenance on machines where unexpected energization could injure people falls under OSHA's control of hazardous energy standard (lockout/tagout, 29 CFR 1910.147) [25]. Faster changeovers must be designed into the procedure (quick-release fixtures, preset tools, staged material), not achieved by skipping lockout. The same applies to guarding and ergonomics: a relief operator needs the same training and qualification as the regular operator.

### Union and labor context

In unionized plants, staggered breaks, relief operators, Saturday shifts and changes in measurement often touch the collective bargaining agreement. Bring the union or employee representatives in during phase 0 of the roadmap, explain the goal and agree how overtime and relief roles are assigned. A TOC rollout that starts with a grievance about break schedules loses months.

### Respect for people in TOC terms

The evaporating cloud (Section 12) is a practical respect-for-people tool: it assumes that both sides of a conflict have a legitimate need and looks for the wrong assumption behind the conflict. Used in a team that includes operators, it turns "operators versus planners" into a shared problem with a shared objective.

## TOC in the digital plant: data, simulation and APS

TOC was born with software: OPT was a scheduling program before it was a philosophy [3]. Today, digital tools help in four places.

### 1. Data for bottleneck detection

MES and IIoT platforms provide the time-stamped machine states that the active period method needs (Section 6). Most plants already have the states (running, idle, down, setup) but do not separate "starved" and "blocked" from "idle." Adding those two states is often the most useful single change for TOC analysis. Agree with operators and, where applicable, the union that machine state data are used to find system losses, not to rate individual people. Collect data read-only from the controls and keep the OT network segmented; the [OT cybersecurity and ISA/IEC 62443](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/ot-cybersecurity-iec-62443/) guide covers how. Check that clocks are synchronized across machines, because the active period method compares time stamps between resources. The [MES and ISA-95](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/mes-isa-95/) and [predictive maintenance and IIoT](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/predictive-maintenance-iiot/) guides explain the data path; the [manufacturing IT with Azure](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/manufacturing-it-azure/) guide covers cloud analytics for such data.

### 2. Simulation for shifting bottlenecks and buffer sizing

A discrete event simulation lets you test buffer sizes, release rules and elevation options before you change the floor. Roser, Nakano and Tanaka developed the active period method on simulation data [13][14]. Typical questions: How often does the bottleneck shift with next quarter's mix? How big must the constraint buffer be to keep the share of red orders below 10 percent? Does a second machine move the constraint to assembly? The [digital twin in manufacturing](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/digital-twin-manufacturing/) guide explains when a simulation model becomes a digital twin.

### 3. APS for drum scheduling

An APS can schedule the drum with sequence-dependent setups, compute release dates from buffers and show buffer status. DBR gives the control logic; the APS gives the computing power, especially when the constraint shifts or several constraints interact (where the simple product-mix rule fails, see Section 19). The [production scheduling and APS](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/production-scheduling-aps/) guide covers this in depth, including when ERP is enough.

### 4. Value stream models for the system view

A digital value stream model shows the constraint in the context of the whole flow: cycle times, availability, batch sizes and inventories. One example of such software is iFAKT Polaris, which combines value stream modeling and simulation; according to the vendor, its modules include scheduling optimization [26]. That is a vendor description, not an independent test. **Note: The author works at iFAKT GmbH.** The [iFAKT Polaris](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/ifakt-polaris/) guide describes it in more detail; other value stream and simulation tools can do the same TOC analysis.

### Sequence: method first, then software

Start the constraint loss log and buffer board on paper or in a spreadsheet. Automate when the method works and people trust the numbers. A buffer status screen that nobody acts on is worse than a whiteboard that drives the daily meeting.

## FAQ: theory of constraints questions

### What is the theory of constraints in simple terms?

Every system has one or a few weak links that limit how much it can produce. The theory of constraints says: find that weak link, get the most out of it, align everything else to it, and only then invest in it. It was developed by Eliyahu M. Goldratt and introduced in *The Goal* in 1984 [1].

### What are the 5 steps of the theory of constraints?

Identify the constraint, exploit the constraint, subordinate everything else to the constraint, elevate the constraint, and prevent inertia from becoming the constraint, then start again [4].

### What is an example of the theory of constraints?

In the manifold plant in Section 5, a CNC cell limited output. Covering breaks, faster changeovers, staged material and earlier quality checks raised its productive time by 800 minutes a week (22 percent), and changing the product mix to throughput per constraint minute added profit without investment. A second machine at $650,000 turned out to be the weakest option.

### What is the difference between a bottleneck and a constraint?

A bottleneck is a resource with less capacity than the demand on it. A constraint is anything that limits the system's goal: a bottleneck, but also the market, a material, a policy or a mindset. Every bottleneck that limits output is a constraint; not every constraint is a bottleneck.

### How do you identify a bottleneck in a process?

Start with a capacity load table (demand × process time vs. available time), then confirm on the floor with queue counts and a bottleneck walk. If you have time-stamped machine states, use the active period method, which also shows shifting bottlenecks [13][14][15]. Section 6 compares six methods.

### What is bottleneck analysis?

Bottleneck analysis finds the resource that limits a process, quantifies how much output it costs and checks whether it shifts over time. In a plant, start with a capacity load calculation, confirm it with queue counts and a bottleneck walk, and use machine-state data with the active period method where you have it (Section 6).

### How do you fix a bottleneck?

Follow the five focusing steps. First exploit it (no lost minutes, no defective work, the right product mix), then subordinate the rest of the plant to its pace, and only then elevate it with overtime, outsourcing or new equipment. In the manifold example in Section 5, exploitation alone raised productive time at the constraint by 22 percent before any investment.

### What are production constraints?

Anything that limits how much a plant can produce and ship: equipment capacity, people and skills, materials and suppliers, the market, and the plant's own policies, such as batch-size or utilization rules. A production constraint is not always a machine (Section 3).

### What are the four types of constraints?

Lists differ. Grouped into four, the types in Section 3 are physical constraints (equipment, people, materials), market constraints, policy constraints and paradigm constraints. The table in Section 3 splits the physical type into three, which gives six.

### What is drum-buffer-rope?

Drum-buffer-rope is the TOC scheduling method. The drum is the constraint's schedule, the buffer is a time buffer that protects the constraint and the shipping date, and the rope releases material only one buffer time before the constraint needs it [6][8]. Section 7 shows a release schedule with numbers.

### What is simplified drum-buffer-rope (S-DBR)?

S-DBR is a variant for make-to-order plants. It uses only a shipping buffer and checks the planned load on the capacity constrained resource when due dates are promised, instead of keeping a detailed constraint schedule [6][9].

### What is buffer management?

Buffer management tracks how much of each order's time buffer has been consumed and colors orders green, yellow or red. Red orders get priority, and the causes of red orders become the improvement list [9].

### What is throughput accounting?

Throughput accounting is TOC's management accounting. It uses throughput (sales minus truly variable cost), inventory or investment, and operating expense; net profit is T − OE and ROI is (T − OE) ÷ I [20][22]. Under a constraint, it ranks products by throughput per constraint minute.

### How is throughput accounting different from cost accounting?

Standard cost accounting spreads labor and overhead over units and ranks products by margin per unit. Throughput accounting treats labor and overhead as operating expense, which does not change with each unit in the short run, and ranks products by throughput per unit of constraint time. The two can give opposite answers, as the example in Section 11 shows.

### What is the difference between lean and the theory of constraints?

Lean looks for waste across the whole value stream and creates flow and pull. TOC looks for the one constraint and focuses improvement there. Lean balances work to takt; TOC keeps spare capacity at non-constraints. Used together, TOC tells lean tools such as SMED and TPM where to work first.

### Can TOC and Six Sigma be combined?

Yes. TOC chooses the place, Six Sigma reduces the variation there, for example scrap at the constraint or unstable supply into its buffer. Throughput accounting then shows the financial effect of each project.

### What are the thinking processes in TOC?

A set of logic tools for non-physical problems: the current reality tree, evaporating cloud, future reality tree, prerequisite tree and transition tree. They answer what to change, what to change to and how to cause the change [12].

### What is critical chain project management?

Goldratt's application of TOC to projects, from his 1997 book *Critical Chain* [3]. It schedules the longest chain of task and resource dependencies, removes safety from individual tasks and pools it in project and feeding buffers.

### What is the theory of constraints in supply chain management?

In supply chains, TOC replaces forecast-driven stock at each location with buffers that are replenished to actual consumption and resized by buffer status [6]. It treats the weakest link, often a supplier, a plant or the market, as the constraint that sets the pace for the whole system. Section 14 summarizes the replenishment rules.

### What are the criticisms of the theory of constraints?

The main criticisms are publication bias in success studies, the lack of optimality of TOC heuristics when several constraints interact, the limited originality of some ideas, and a tendency to neglect people and policies [6][10]. Section 19 covers each.

### Is the theory of constraints still relevant?

Yes. Plants still have constraints, and the combination of better machine data, simulation and APS makes it easier to find shifting bottlenecks and to schedule a drum. What has changed is that TOC is rarely used alone; it is usually combined with lean, Six Sigma and digital planning.

### Who created the theory of constraints?

Eliyahu M. Goldratt (1947 to 2011), an Israeli physicist and business author. He introduced TOC in *The Goal* (1984), co-written with Jeff Cox [1][2].

### How long does it take to implement the theory of constraints?

In one plant or value stream, the first measurable results usually come from exploitation within a few weeks, because they need little money. A stable DBR or S-DBR routine with buffer management takes several months; the roadmap in Section 16 plans six months for the first full cycle. These durations are planning assumptions, not benchmarks from a study.

### Is there a theory of constraints certification?

TOCICO offers certification in three levels: fundamentals exams, practitioner exams and an implementer assessment, in areas such as operations, supply chain, project management (CCPM), thinking processes and finance [5].

## Conclusion and first steps

The theory of constraints starts from a simple observation: a plant's output is set by a very small number of constraints, usually one. That changes what managers should do. Instead of improving everything at once, find the constraint, stop wasting its time, make the rest of the plant serve it and spend money only when that is done. Drum-buffer-rope turns that logic into a schedule, buffer management turns it into daily priorities, and throughput accounting turns it into financial decisions that standard cost reports can get wrong.

The worked example shows the scale of what is often possible without investment: 22 percent more constraint time from breaks, setups, material staging and quality checks, and a product-mix change that standard costing would not have suggested. It also shows the limits: once exploited, the constraint moves, and old rules become the next problem. TOC is strongest with one clear constraint and needs other tools (optimization, APS, lean line balancing) where constraints shift or interact. The evidence base is mostly success stories, so test the claims in your own plant.

**Your first steps in the next 30 days**

- [ ] Build a load table for your main product family (demand × process time vs. available time) and name the constraint candidate
- [ ] Confirm it with queue counts and three bottleneck walks per shift for one week
- [ ] Start a constraint loss log (Template 2) at the candidate and fill it every shift for two weeks
- [ ] Calculate throughput per constraint minute for your top 10 products (Template 3) with your controller
- [ ] Pick the two biggest losses and act on them (break coverage, external setup, quality before the constraint)
- [ ] Stop at least one utilization target at a non-constraint
- [ ] Brief union or employee representatives on goals, break coverage and the role of spare capacity
