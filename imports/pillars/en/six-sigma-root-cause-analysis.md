# Six Sigma and Root Cause Analysis (RCA): DMAIC, Tools, and Calculations for Manufacturing

*Updated: November 2026 · Reading time: about 50 minutes (quick start: short answer, "Key takeaways" box, the DMAIC templates and the glossary)*

> **Short answer:** Six Sigma is a data-driven method for reducing variation and defects in a process. ASQ describes it as a method that gives organizations tools to improve the capability of their business processes, and defines Six Sigma quality as 3.4 defects per million opportunities, allowing for a 1.5-sigma shift in the mean [1]. Projects follow DMAIC (Define, Measure, Analyze, Improve, Control). The Analyze phase is a root cause analysis: it uses tools such as the 5 Whys, the fishbone diagram, Pareto charts, fault trees and statistical tests to prove why a problem occurs before anyone fixes it.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Six Sigma began at Motorola in the mid-1980s. Motorola received the Malcolm Baldrige National Quality Award in 1988, and the award profile names a target of no more than 3.4 defects per million [2][3].
> - GE made it a company-wide program from 1995 and reported more than three quarters of a billion dollars in savings beyond its investment for 1998 [4].
> - "3.4 defects per million" is not six standard deviations of a normal curve. A centered six-sigma process would produce about 0.002 defects per million; the 3.4 figure assumes the mean drifts 1.5 sigma over time, a convention that statisticians such as Donald Wheeler criticize [5].
> - DMAIC fixes an existing process. For new products or processes, Design for Six Sigma (for example DMADV) applies.
> - Root cause analysis is the core of the Analyze phase. A root cause is a factor that caused a nonconformance and should be permanently eliminated [6]. "Operator error" is almost never a root cause.
> - Capability needs stability first. Cp and Cpk are only meaningful for an in-control process with roughly 50 or more independent values [7].
> - Belts are not standardized. ASQ requires work experience and, for the Black Belt, signed project affidavits; IASSC tests knowledge without a project requirement [8][9].
> - Most programs that fail do so for organizational reasons (project selection, sponsorship, control phase), not because the statistics were wrong [5].

## What is Six Sigma? Definition in plain terms

Every process varies. Two shafts turned on the same lathe in the same hour never have exactly the same diameter. Two invoices never take exactly the same time to process. As long as the variation stays inside what the customer accepts, nobody notices. When the variation is too wide or the process drifts, defects appear: parts out of tolerance, leaks, late deliveries, warranty claims.

Six Sigma is a method for finding and reducing that variation with data. It has three layers:

1. **A metric.** The sigma level expresses how many defects a process produces per million opportunities. Higher is better; "six sigma" is the label for 3.4 defects per million opportunities under the conventions explained below [1].
2. **A method.** DMAIC (Define, Measure, Analyze, Improve, Control) is the project roadmap for improving an existing process. ASQ describes DMAIC as the steps a Six Sigma practitioner is expected to follow [1]. ISO 13053-1 describes the same five phases as an international standard [10].
3. **An organization.** Trained specialists (Green Belts, Black Belts, Master Black Belts) lead projects that executives (Champions) select and sponsor.

The Greek letter sigma (σ) is the symbol for standard deviation, the usual measure of spread. The name refers to how many standard deviations fit between the process mean and the nearest specification limit. The more sigmas fit, the smaller the share of output that falls outside the limits.

### What Six Sigma is good for, and what not

Six Sigma is strongest where a problem is chronic, measurable and has unclear causes: a scrap rate stuck at 3% for years, a dimension that drifts, a coating that fails sporadically. It is weak where the cause is obvious from walking the process (searching for tools, waiting for material), where data cannot be collected, or where the task is to invent something new. For flow problems, the tools in the [lean manufacturing guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/lean-manufacturing/) are usually faster.

### Benefits, stated carefully

Competitor pages promise "up to 50% cost savings" without a source. The documented record is narrower:

- **Motorola** set a target of no more than 3.4 defects per million products, "customer services included", and won the 1988 Baldrige Award with that program [2].
- **GE** reported more than three quarters of a billion dollars in savings beyond its investment in 1998, with 5,000 full-time Master Black Belts and Black Belts, after investing more than a billion dollars [4]. These are company-reported figures in an annual report, not audited project results.
- **Independent evidence** on company-level results is mixed (see "Criticism and limits"). Whether a given plant benefits depends on project selection and follow-through more than on the method itself.

The honest summary: Six Sigma reliably reduces defects in the processes where projects are run and controlled. Company-wide savings figures are self-reported and should be read as such.

## A short history: Motorola, GE and ISO 13053

| Year | Event | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 1920s | Walter A. Shewhart develops control charts and the idea that variation has common and assignable causes; three-sigma limits become the standard | [11][12] |
| Late 1970s to mid-1980s | Motorola leaders, including CEO Bob Galvin, push to raise quality targets; engineers start measuring defects per million opportunities | [3][11] |
| Mid-1980s | Six Sigma takes shape at Motorola; the engineer Bill Smith is commonly credited with the concept. Accounts differ on 1986 or 1987 | [3] |
| 1988 | Motorola receives the Malcolm Baldrige National Quality Award; its award profile names Six Sigma and the 3.4-per-million target | [2] |
| 1991 / 1993 | Motorola registers "Six Sigma" as a US service mark (1991) and trademark (1993) | [5] |
| 1990s | AlliedSignal under Larry Bossidy and GE under Jack Welch adopt Six Sigma | [11] |
| 1995 | GE launches its Six Sigma program; by 1998 it reports 5,000 full-time Master Black Belts and Black Belts | [4] |
| 2011 | ISO publishes ISO 13053-1, *Quantitative methods in process improvement: Six Sigma, Part 1: DMAIC methodology*; last reviewed and confirmed in 2022 | [10] |

Three points from this history matter in practice.

First, Six Sigma is built on older statistical quality control. Shewhart's control charts, Deming's work on variation and Juran's project-by-project improvement were all in place decades before Motorola. What Motorola added was a single defect metric across very different processes, a stretch goal and a training structure.

Second, the program became famous through GE, not Motorola. GE's 1998 annual report states that Six Sigma "came along in 1995" and that the company "plunged into Six Sigma with a Company-consuming vengeance" [4]. The same report mentions Design for Six Sigma for new products, the design-side counterpart to DMAIC (see the DMADV section).

Third, there is an international standard. ISO 13053-1:2011 describes the DMAIC phases, recommended practices in each phase, how projects should be managed, and the roles, expertise and training of the people involved, for manufacturing, service and transactional processes [10]. Few US plants use it, but it is useful when a customer or auditor asks what "a Six Sigma project" formally means.

On the dates: the [Lean Manufacturing guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/lean-manufacturing/) and this article both say "mid-1980s" because the sources disagree on whether the program began in 1986 or 1987 [3]. The 1988 Baldrige award is the first firm public date [2].

## Sigma levels and DPMO: the math worked through

None of the top-ranking pages shows this calculation in text, so here it is step by step.

### Units, defects and opportunities

| Term | Meaning | Example (valve body) |
|---|---|---|
| Unit | the item being produced or processed | one hydraulic valve body |
| Defect | any failure to meet a customer requirement | a leak at test, a bore out of tolerance |
| Opportunity | a distinct chance for a defect that is measured | 8 critical-to-quality characteristics checked per body |
| DPU | defects per unit = defects ÷ units | |
| DPO | defects per opportunity = defects ÷ (units × opportunities) | |
| DPMO | DPO × 1,000,000 | |

### Worked example 1: from defect counts to sigma level

A cell machines 5,000 valve bodies in a month. Each body has 8 characteristics that are inspected (opportunities). Inspection records 46 defects.

- DPU = 46 ÷ 5,000 = 0.0092
- DPO = 46 ÷ (5,000 × 8) = 46 ÷ 40,000 = 0.00115
- DPMO = 0.00115 × 1,000,000 = **1,150**

To convert DPMO into a sigma level, find the z-value of the standard normal distribution whose upper tail equals the DPO, then add the conventional 1.5:

- Yield per opportunity = 1 − 0.00115 = 0.99885
- z for 0.99885 = 3.05 (from a normal table, or `=NORM.S.INV(0.99885)` in Excel)
- Sigma level (short-term, with shift) = 3.05 + 1.5 = **4.55**

The formula behind every sigma table is DPMO = 1,000,000 × (1 − Φ(sigma level − 1.5)), where Φ is the cumulative standard normal distribution [5]. In Excel: `=NORM.S.INV(1-DPMO/1000000)+1.5`.

**Careful with opportunities.** The number of opportunities is a choice, and it drives the result. If the same 46 defects are counted against 20 "opportunities" per unit, DPMO falls to 460 and the sigma level rises to about 4.8 without anything improving. Define opportunities once per product family, count only characteristics that are actually checked, and keep the definition fixed. For comparisons between processes, DPU and first pass yield are harder to game.

### The sigma conversion table (computed)

Values computed with the formula above (long-term DPMO with the 1.5-sigma shift) and, for comparison, for a perfectly centered process with both tails counted:

| Sigma level | DPMO (with 1.5σ shift) | Yield (with shift) | Defects per million if centered, no shift |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 691,462 | 30.9% | 317,311 |
| 2 | 308,538 | 69.1% | 45,500 |
| 3 | 66,807 | 93.3% | 2,700 |
| 4 | 6,210 | 99.38% | 63 |
| 4.5 | 1,350 | 99.865% | 6.8 |
| 5 | 233 | 99.977% | 0.57 |
| 6 | 3.4 | 99.99966% | 0.002 |

The last column shows why the shift matters: without it, a six-sigma process would produce about two defects per billion, not 3.4 per million.

### Worked example 2: rolled throughput yield

A part passes four process steps. First pass yields are 98%, 95%, 99% and 97%. Rolled throughput yield (RTY), the probability that a unit passes all steps without any rework, is the product:

RTY = 0.98 × 0.95 × 0.99 × 0.97 = **0.894**, or 89.4%

Each step looks acceptable on its own; together, more than one unit in ten needs rework or scrap somewhere. RTY is often the more useful metric for a plant manager than a sigma level, because it shows the "hidden factory" of rework loops. For the definitions of first pass yield and related metrics, see the [Manufacturing KPIs guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/manufacturing-kpis/).

### Worked example 3: what one sigma level is worth

The running example in this article: a plant ships 48,000 valve bodies per year. 2.8% fail the final leak test. Each failure costs $85 in rework, retest and scrap (illustrative figure).

- Failures per year: 48,000 × 0.028 = 1,344
- Cost: 1,344 × $85 = $114,240 per year
- Sigma level: DPO = 0.028 per unit (one opportunity: leak or no leak) -> z = 1.91 -> **3.41 sigma**

If a DMAIC project cuts the failure rate to 0.6%:

- Failures: 48,000 × 0.006 = 288; cost: $24,480
- Annual saving: $89,760 (before project costs)
- Sigma level: z for 0.994 = 2.51 -> **4.01 sigma**

Moving from about 3.4 to about 4.0 sigma is a realistic one-project result. Moving from 4.5 to 6 sigma usually requires redesign, not a project.

## The 1.5-sigma shift debate

ASQ's definition includes the phrase "accounting for a 1.5-sigma shift in the mean" [1]. This is the most misunderstood part of Six Sigma.

### What the shift claims

The assumption is that a process measured over a short period performs better than it will over months, because the mean moves between batches, shifts, tool changes and material lots. Six Sigma convention models that long-term drift as a shift of the mean by 1.5 standard deviations. A process with six sigmas between mean and limit in the short term is therefore treated as having 4.5 sigmas in the long term, and the one-sided tail beyond 4.5 sigma is 3.4 per million [5].

According to the sources summarized on Wikipedia, Mikel Harry based the figure on an analysis of tolerance stacking in a stack of discs, which he called "Benderizing", and Motorola adopted it as a convention [5].

### Why statisticians object

- **It is a convention, not a law.** Real processes drift by less or by much more than 1.5 sigma. Donald J. Wheeler called the shift "goofy" because of its arbitrary nature [5].
- **It mixes two questions.** Whether a process is stable (control chart) and whether it is capable (capability index) are separate questions. A process that really shifts by 1.5 sigma is not in statistical control, and a control chart should detect and correct that shift long before it produces defects.
- **It distorts reporting.** If a plant computes a sigma level from long-term data (which already contains the drift) and then adds 1.5, it double-counts the shift and overstates performance.

### A practical rule

Report what you measured and how:

| Situation | What to report |
|---|---|
| Long-term defect data (months of production) | DPMO and yield; if a sigma level is required, state "sigma level incl. 1.5 shift (convention)" |
| Short-term capability study (one batch, one shift) | Cp and Cpk, plus the data period |
| Customer or supplier comparison | DPMO or ppm plus the definition of a defect and an opportunity |
| Internal trend over time | the same metric, same definition, every month |

Customer scorecards usually count rejected parts in ppm, and ppm has one advantage over a sigma level: no convention can inflate it.

## Lean vs. Six Sigma vs. Lean Six Sigma: what's the difference?

Lean and Six Sigma are often sold as one package, and in many US plants they now share one CI department. They still answer different questions. Lean focuses on customer value and flow, eliminating waste such as waiting, searching and rework loops; Six Sigma focuses on reducing variation and defect rates with statistical analysis. The [Lean Manufacturing guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/lean-manufacturing/) compares them in a table; the short version from the Six Sigma side:

| Question | Lean answers with | Six Sigma answers with |
|---|---|---|
| Why does the order take 12 days when work content is 3 hours? | value stream map, flow, pull, WIP limits | (rarely the right tool) |
| Why do 2.8% of parts leak? | standard work, poka-yoke after the cause is known | DMAIC: measure, prove the cause, fix, control |
| Why does the bore diameter drift over a shift? | TPM, standard work | SPC, capability study, DOE |
| Why do changeovers take 90 minutes? | SMED | (rarely the right tool) |
| Which of 14 process settings drive coating thickness? | (no tool) | design of experiments, regression |

ASQ describes Lean Six Sigma as the integration of both: lean removes waste and speeds up flow, Six Sigma reduces variation [1]. In practice, a Lean Six Sigma program runs DMAIC as the project structure and uses lean tools inside it (value stream map in Define, 5S and standard work in Improve and Control). More background is on the entity page [Lean Six Sigma](https://www.m365.fm/entity/lean-six-sigma/) and in the post [Lean vs. Six Sigma: Differences and How to Combine Them](https://www.m365.fm/community/u/mirko-p/blog/lean-vs-six-sigma-differences-combine/).

**Where Six Sigma sits in the larger system.** Neither lean nor Six Sigma is a management system on its own. [Operational excellence](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/operational-excellence/) is the frame that connects improvement methods with strategy, daily management and culture. A Six Sigma program without that frame tends to produce good project reports and little lasting change.

## The DMAIC process, phase by phase

DMAIC is a five-phase roadmap for improving an existing process whose performance can be measured. ISO 13053-1 uses the same five phases [10]. Each phase ends with a gate review in which the Champion and the Black Belt decide whether the project continues, changes scope or stops.

This section follows one illustrative project through all five phases: **leak-test failures on an aluminum hydraulic valve body**, 48,000 units per year, 2.8% failure rate, $114,240 annual cost of failures (figures from worked example 3 above).

### Overview: phases, questions, tools, outputs

| Phase | Key question | Typical tools | Output (gate deliverable) | Typical duration [unverified] |
|---|---|---|---|---|

### Define

**Purpose:** agree on the problem, the customer, the goal and the boundaries before anyone starts collecting data.

**Steps:**

1. **Write the problem statement** with numbers, not causes. Good: "From January to June 2026, 2.8% of valve bodies failed the final leak test (1,344 per year at current volume), costing $114,240 per year in rework and scrap." Bad: "The casting supplier delivers porous parts." (That is a guess about the cause.)
2. **Capture the voice of the customer (VOC)** and translate it into critical-to-quality (CTQ) characteristics. The customer wants "no leaks in the field"; the CTQ is "leak rate at 6 bar test pressure below the specified limit".
3. **Draw a SIPOC** (suppliers, inputs, process, outputs, customers) at five to seven high-level steps. It sets the scope: casting supplier -> receiving -> machining -> washing -> assembly of plugs and seals -> leak test -> shipping.
4. **Set a goal** that is specific and time-bound: "Reduce leak-test failures from 2.8% to below 1.0% by December 31, 2026."
5. **Estimate the financial benefit** with the plant controller (see the finance note below).
6. **Form the team:** Black Belt or Green Belt as project lead, process owner (production supervisor), quality engineer, a machining operator from each shift, maintenance, and purchasing for the supplier side.
7. **Get the charter signed** by the Champion.

If customers are receiving defective parts now, contain first (100% test, sorting, quarantine) and start the DMAIC in parallel. DMAIC has no formal containment step; 8D does (see below).

**Finance note.** Count only savings the controller will recognize: scrap material, rework labor at its real cost, overtime avoided, retest time, freight for replacement parts. Do not count "capacity freed" as cash unless the capacity is sold or a shift is actually cut. A separate column for "soft benefits" (customer score, less firefighting) keeps the hard number credible.

### Measure

**Purpose:** establish a trustworthy baseline and narrow the field of suspected causes.

**Steps:**

1. **Map the process in detail** (one level below the SIPOC), with every inspection and rework loop.
2. **Write a data collection plan:** what is measured, how, by whom, how often, with which gage, and how the data is stratified (shift, machine, supplier lot, operator, day of week). A template is in the templates section.
3. **Check the measurement system** before trusting any data. For continuous data, run a gage R&R study (several operators measure the same parts repeatedly). For pass/fail data such as a leak test, run an attribute agreement analysis: the same set of known good and known bad parts is tested repeatedly, blind, and the agreement with the known standard is calculated.
4. **Collect baseline data** for long enough to cover the normal sources of variation (all shifts, several supplier lots).
5. **Calculate baseline performance:** defect rate, DPMO, sigma level, first pass yield, and for continuous characteristics Cp/Cpk (see the capability section).
6. **Build a Pareto** of defect types or locations.

**Running example, Measure results.** The team pulls six weeks of leak-test records with failure locations and runs an attribute agreement study on the leak tester with 30 reference parts (20 known good, 10 with calibrated leaks), each tested three times. Result: the tester rejected 4 of the 60 good-part tests, almost all on the night shift after the test fixture seal had not been replaced. **One finding from Measure alone: part of the "defect rate" is a measurement problem.** The fixture seal gets a replacement interval, and the Pareto of 160 real failures in six weeks looks like this (illustrative):

| Failure cause (location found at teardown) | Count | Share | Cumulative |
|---|---|---|---|
| Casting porosity in port area | 74 | 46.3% | 46.3% |
| Damaged seal seat (burr, scratch) | 38 | 23.8% | 70.0% |
| Thread burr at plug bore | 21 | 13.1% | 83.1% |
| Test fixture false fail (before fix) | 14 | 8.8% | 91.9% |
| Other | 13 | 8.1% | 100.0% |
| **Total** | **160** | **100%** | |

Two causes account for 70% of failures. Note that this is not "80/20", and it does not need to be. The point of a Pareto is to rank, not to confirm a ratio.

**Measurement system acceptance.** The automotive Measurement Systems Analysis reference manual published by AIAG is the common US reference for gage R&R acceptance criteria. Widely quoted guidance, attributed to AIAG in Minitab's documentation, is that a gage R&R below 10% is acceptable, 10% to 30% may be acceptable depending on the application and the cost of the gage or repair, and above 30% is not acceptable; AIAG also asks for at least five distinct categories [32]. Check the current edition of the AIAG MSA manual and your customer's requirements before you set acceptance limits.

### Analyze

**Purpose:** prove which causes drive the problem. This is the root cause analysis phase, and the RCA tools are covered in their own sections below.

**Steps:**

1. **Generate candidate causes** with a fishbone diagram for each major Pareto bar, with operators from all shifts in the room.
2. **Drill down** on the most plausible branches with 5 Whys, and for safety- or reliability-critical failures with a fault tree.
3. **Turn candidate causes into testable hypotheses:** "Porosity rate differs between casting supplier A and supplier B." "Seal seat damage happens at the washing station, not at machining."
4. **Test the hypotheses with data:** stratified Pareto, two-proportion test, chi-square test, t-test or ANOVA for continuous data, regression, multi-vari charts, or simply a well-designed observation at the gemba.
5. **Confirm the root cause** by turning it on and off where possible: if the cause is real, removing it should remove the effect, and reintroducing it should bring the effect back.

**Running example, Analyze results.** Porosity: of 1,200 bodies from supplier A, 31 failed with porosity (2.58%); of 1,150 bodies from supplier B, 9 failed (0.78%). A two-proportion z-test gives z = 3.37 and p ≈ 0.0007. The difference is very unlikely to be chance. Supplier A's castings are the main porosity source, and a visit shows that supplier A skips vacuum impregnation on this part number since a cost-down change two years earlier (illustrative).

Seal seat damage: the team follows 50 parts with a marker and inspects after each step. All damage occurs in the washing basket, where bodies touch each other. The 5 Whys chain ends at "the basket fixture was replaced by a generic basket when the old one broke, and no one updated the work instruction."

**Statistics without overreach.** A p-value says how surprising the data would be if there were no difference. It does not say how big or important the difference is, and it does not prove causation. Combine it with the effect size (here 1.8 percentage points) and a physical explanation (missing impregnation). If you cannot explain the mechanism, keep investigating.

### Improve

**Purpose:** develop, test and implement solutions that remove the verified causes.

**Steps:**

1. **Generate solutions** for each root cause, preferably several per cause.
2. **Select solutions** with a simple matrix: effect on the root cause, cost, time to implement, risk.
3. **Run an FMEA** on the changed process: what could go wrong with the new solution?
4. **Optimize settings** where several factors interact, with design of experiments (DOE). A two-level full factorial with three factors needs 2³ = 8 runs and shows main effects and interactions; with five factors a half fraction (16 runs) is common.
5. **Pilot** the solution on a limited scope (one shift, one lot, two weeks) and measure the result with the same measurement system as the baseline.
6. **Prove the improvement statistically.** Before/after comparison with enough data: to detect a drop from 2.8% to 0.6% with 95% confidence and 80% power, a two-proportion test needs about 540 parts per group (calculation with the normal approximation).
7. **Plan the rollout** including training, documentation and the customer notification if the change affects a customer-approved process (for automotive suppliers, a PPAP change notification may be required).

**Running example, Improve results.** Supplier A reinstates vacuum impregnation (purchasing negotiates a price adjustment of $0.40 per casting, about $9,600 per year for supplier A's share). The washing basket gets a fixture with separate nests, and the work instruction is updated. The leak tester gets a fixture seal change interval and a daily check with a reference leak. Pilot: 1,100 bodies, 7 failures (0.64%).

Mistake-proofing is the strongest type of solution because it does not depend on attention: a nest that only accepts the part in one orientation, a sensor that stops the line if the reference-leak check is skipped. The [poka-yoke guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/lean-manufacturing/poka-yoke/) covers the methods.

### Control

**Purpose:** keep the gain after the project team moves on. Most failed projects fail here.

**Steps:**

1. **Write a control plan:** for each critical input and output, what is measured, how often, by whom, the limits, and the reaction plan when a limit is exceeded. A template is in the templates section.
2. **Set up control charts** for the key outputs and inputs (see SPC below).
3. **Update standard work and work instructions**, and train every shift. The [standard work guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/shop-floor-management/standard-work/) explains how to write instructions operators actually use.
4. **Add mistake-proofing** where possible, and audit it (a poka-yoke that was bypassed is worse than none).
5. **Put the KPI on the shop floor board** and the tier meeting agenda. See the [Shop Floor Management guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/shop-floor-management/).
6. **Hand over** to the process owner with a signed closure, including the final financial validation by the controller.
7. **Review after 3 and 12 months.** If the result slips, the reaction plan or the standard failed; that is a new problem to solve.

**Running example, Control results.** A daily p-chart for the leak-test failure rate with about 400 tests per day. With the new center line p = 0.6%, the control limits are: UCL = 0.006 + 3 × √(0.006 × 0.994 ÷ 400) = 0.006 + 0.0116 = **1.76%**, LCL = 0 (the calculated value is negative). Any day above 1.76% triggers the reaction plan: quarantine the day's production, check the reference-leak log, check supplier lot numbers. After six months: average 0.58%, annual saving validated by the controller at about $80,000 after the casting price increase.

**Project economics (illustrative).** Team time about 300 hours at a loaded rate of $55 = $16,500, nest fixture $6,000, tester interlock $3,500: one-time cost about $26,000. Against $80,000 per year, payback is about four months. The controller books the fixture as a small capital item or expense according to the plant's capitalization threshold; the casting price increase is a recurring cost and is already netted out of the saving.

## Statistical process control and control charts

Statistical process control (SPC) uses control charts to tell routine variation from signals that need action. ASQ defines the control chart as a graph used to study how a process changes over time, with a center line and upper and lower control limits determined from historical data [13].

### Common causes and special causes

Shewhart's insight, still the core of SPC: every process has **common-cause** (chance) variation built into the system, and occasionally **special-cause** (assignable) variation from something that changed: a worn tool, a new material lot, a new operator, a power dip [12]. Reacting to common-cause variation as if it were special (adjusting the machine after every point) makes the process worse; this is what Deming called tampering. Ignoring special causes lets defects through. The control chart decides which is which.

### Why three-sigma limits

The usual multiple is 3, which is why the limits are called three-sigma limits [12]. For a normally distributed statistic, a point falls outside them by chance only 0.27% of the time (0.00135 per side) [12]. That is a sensible balance between false alarms and missed signals. Note the difference:

| Control limits | Specification limits |
|---|---|
| calculated from the process data | set by the customer or engineering |
| describe what the process does | describe what the process should do |
| used to detect special causes | used to accept or reject parts |
| belong on the control chart | do not belong on an X-bar chart (averages are not individual parts) |

### Choosing the chart

| Data type | Subgroup | Chart | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Continuous | n = 1 | I-MR (individuals and moving range) | daily batch viscosity |
| Continuous | n = 2 to about 10 | X-bar and R | bore diameter, 5 parts per hour |
| Continuous | n above about 10 | X-bar and S | high-volume automated measurement |
| Attribute: defectives (pass/fail) | variable or constant n | p-chart (proportion), np-chart (count, constant n) | leak-test failures per day |
| Attribute: defects (count per unit) | constant area/unit | c-chart | paint defects per car body |
| Attribute: defects | variable area/unit | u-chart | defects per square foot of sheet |

### Worked example: X-bar and R chart

A machining cell measures a bore of 12.000 ± 0.020 mm. Every hour, 5 consecutive parts are measured (subgroup size n = 5). After 25 subgroups, the grand average is x̿ = 12.004 mm and the average range is R̄ = 0.010 mm. The NIST constants for n = 5 are A₂ = 0.577, D₃ = 0 and D₄ = 2.115 [14].

X-bar chart:

- UCL = x̿ + A₂ × R̄ = 12.004 + 0.577 × 0.010 = **12.0098 mm**
- Center line = **12.004 mm**
- LCL = x̿ − A₂ × R̄ = 12.004 − 0.00577 = **11.9982 mm**

R chart:

- UCL = D₄ × R̄ = 2.115 × 0.010 = **0.0212 mm**
- Center line = **0.010 mm**
- LCL = D₃ × R̄ = **0**

Estimated process standard deviation: σ̂ = R̄ ÷ d₂ = 0.010 ÷ 2.326 = **0.0043 mm** (d₂ for n = 5, derived from A₂ = 3 ÷ (d₂√n)).

Calculate limits from a stable baseline period, then freeze them. Recalculate only after a deliberate process change, not every week, or the limits will follow the drift you want to detect.

### Rules for detecting signals

Beyond a single point outside the limits, the Western Electric (WECO) rules flag non-random patterns [15]:

| Rule | Signal |
|---|---|
| 1 | one point beyond ±3σ |
| 2 | 2 of the last 3 points beyond ±2σ on the same side |
| 3 | 4 of the last 5 points beyond ±1σ on the same side |
| 4 | 8 consecutive points on one side of the center line |
| Trend rules (as listed by NIST) | 6 points in a row steadily increasing or decreasing; 14 points in a row alternating up and down |

Each added rule catches more real shifts and also raises the false-alarm rate. A common practical choice is rules 1, 2 and 4 for most characteristics, with the full set only where the cost of a missed shift is high. Write the chosen rules into the control plan so every shift reacts the same way.

### SPC on the shop floor

- **Who plots?** Ideally the operator, at the machine, because the operator can react at once. Automated SPC from gages and machine data is fine, as long as someone with authority sees the signal within minutes.
- **What happens at a signal?** The reaction plan: stop or not, who is called (andon), which parts are quarantined, what is checked first. Without a reaction plan, SPC becomes wallpaper. The [andon guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/shop-floor-management/andon/) covers the escalation side.
- **Union and workforce angle.** Charts that track an operator's output can be read as surveillance. Chart the process, not the person, explain the purpose, and involve operators in choosing the characteristics. In unionized plants, check whether new data collection on work performance needs to be discussed with the union first.

## Process capability: Cp, Cpk, Pp and Ppk with examples

Capability compares the natural variation of a stable process with the specification limits. NIST stresses two preconditions: the process must be in control, and Cp, Cpk and Cpm assume normally distributed data [7]. NIST also notes that estimates are only valid with a large enough sample, "generally thought to be about 50 independent data values" [7].

### The formulas

From the NIST/SEMATECH e-Handbook [7]:

- **Cp** = (USL − LSL) ÷ 6σ (potential capability: spread only, ignores centering)
- **Cpu** = (USL − μ) ÷ 3σ; **Cpl** = (μ − LSL) ÷ 3σ
- **Cpk** = min(Cpu, Cpl) (actual capability: spread and centering)

**Cp vs. Cpk vs. Pp vs. Ppk.** Cp and Cpk use the short-term (within-subgroup) standard deviation, typically R̄/d₂ from the control chart. Pp and Ppk use the same formulas with the overall standard deviation of all data, which includes shifts between subgroups. If Ppk is much lower than Cpk, the process drifts between subgroups: look for special causes before calling it capable.

### Worked example 1 (NIST)

NIST's example: USL = 20, LSL = 8, s = 2, process average 16 [7].

- Cp = (20 − 8) ÷ (6 × 2) = 1.0
- Cpu = (20 − 16) ÷ 6 = 0.667; Cpl = (16 − 8) ÷ 6 = 1.333
- Cpk = 0.667

The process would fit inside the specification if it were centered (Cp = 1.0), but it sits too close to the upper limit. NIST concludes the process is not good enough and recommends reducing variability and/or centering [7].

### Worked example 2 (bore from the control chart)

Bore 12.000 ± 0.020 mm, x̿ = 12.004 mm, σ̂ = 0.0043 mm (from R̄ ÷ d₂ above).

- Cp = 0.040 ÷ (6 × 0.0043) = **1.55**
- Cpu = (12.020 − 12.004) ÷ (3 × 0.0043) = 0.016 ÷ 0.0129 = **1.24**
- Cpl = (12.004 − 11.980) ÷ 0.0129 = **1.86**
- Cpk = **1.24**

Expected out-of-spec share above USL: z = 0.016 ÷ 0.0043 = 3.72, tail ≈ 99 ppm. Centering the process at 12.000 mm would raise Cpk to Cp = 1.55 without reducing variation at all, and the expected out-of-spec share would drop to a few ppm. **Centering is often the cheapest improvement**: a tool offset, not a capital project.

Suppose the long-term standard deviation of all data, including between-shift drift, is 0.0058 mm. Then Pp = 0.040 ÷ 0.0348 = 1.15 and Ppk = 0.016 ÷ 0.0174 = **0.92**. The gap between Cpk 1.24 and Ppk 0.92 says the process moves between hours or shifts; the control chart should show where.

### What is a "good" Cpk?

NIST's table relates Cp values to reject rates for a centered process: Cp 1.00 -> 0.27%, 1.33 -> 64 ppm, 1.66 -> 0.6 ppm, 2.00 -> 2 parts per billion [7]. In its own example NIST says, "We would like to have Cpk at least 1.0" [7]. Industry requirements are often higher:

| Typical requirement | Where it is commonly used |
|---|---|
| Cpk ≥ 1.33 | widely used minimum for ongoing production characteristics in automotive and general industry |
| Ppk ≥ 1.67 | initial process studies for new or changed processes in automotive supplier approval (PPAP) |
| Cpk ≥ 2.0 | "six sigma capability" in the short term; safety-critical or high-volume characteristics |

[unverified: the 1.33 and 1.67 thresholds are standard practice in AIAG manuals (PPAP, SPC) and customer-specific requirements, which were not accessible online during research. Check the customer's requirement, not a generic table.]

Under the 1.5-shift convention, a six-sigma process has a short-term Cpk of 2.0 and a long-term Cpk of 1.5 [5].

### Capability pitfalls

1. **Unstable process:** a capability index on an out-of-control process predicts nothing. Chart first.
2. **Non-normal data:** flatness, runout, roundness and other one-sided characteristics are bounded at zero and usually skewed. NIST suggests a Box-Cox transformation or non-parametric indices [7].
3. **Too few data points:** 20 parts give a Cpk with a very wide confidence interval. Use at least the roughly 50 values NIST mentions [7].
4. **Measurement error:** if the gage R&R consumes 30% of the tolerance, the capability study measures the gage as much as the process.
5. **Rounding:** a gage that reads to 0.01 mm on a ±0.020 mm tolerance gives only a handful of distinct values; the standard deviation will be badly estimated.

## Root cause analysis in manufacturing

ASQ calls root cause analysis "a collective term that describes a wide range of approaches, tools, and techniques used to uncover causes of problems" and defines a root cause as "a factor that caused a nonconformance" that should be permanently eliminated through process improvement [6]. iSixSigma describes the root cause as the base issue that starts a chain of cause and effect leading to the problem [16]. In plain terms, root cause analysis (RCA) is a systematic approach to identify the underlying causes of a problem instead of treating its symptoms.

Two remarks from ASQ are worth repeating to every team. RCA "in itself will not produce any results"; the results come from the corrective actions it leads to. And the outcome is generally better when a group works together to find causes [6].

### How to conduct a root cause analysis: the RCA process step by step

Whatever tool you use, an effective root cause analysis follows the same sequence, whether the trigger is scrap, a customer complaint, a safety incident or unplanned downtime. The DMAIC phases and the 8D disciplines each give a formal framework for root cause analysis; for a daily problem the same RCA process fits on one page (see the RCA summary template below).

1. **Define the problem.** Clearly defining the problem comes first: what, where, when and how much, measured against the standard. Do not name a suspected cause of the problem yet; that keeps the investigation open.
2. **Contain.** If customers are affected, protect them before conducting root cause analysis: sort, quarantine, 100% test.
3. **Collect data.** Gather facts about the sequence of events leading to the failure from the gemba, from records and from the parts themselves, not from memory in a meeting room.
4. **List potential causes.** Use a fishbone diagram or an is/is-not analysis to collect possible causes and contributing factors, then follow each causal chain to the system level with 5 Whys or a fault tree.
5. **Identify the root cause and verify it** with data, an on/off test or physical evidence (see "Verifying a root cause" below).
6. **Implement corrective actions** that address the root cause, not only the immediate cause or the symptom.
7. **Check effectiveness and prevent recurrence.** Review the data after implementation, update standards, FMEA and control plan, and apply the fix to similar products and lines.

RCA teams work best when they are cross-functional and include the operators who run the process. Most RCA efforts fail at steps 5 to 7: a hypothesis is treated as a fact, the only corrective action is "retrain", or nobody checks whether the problem returns. Done well, RCA is one of the main engines of continuous improvement, because each verified root cause that is removed takes recurring issues off the list.

### Symptom, direct cause, root cause

| Level | Example (seal seat damage) | Action at this level |
|---|---|---|
| Symptom | valve body leaks at test | containment: 100% test, sort |
| Direct (physical) cause | scratch on the seal seat | rework, replace part |
| Intermediate cause | bodies hit each other in the washing basket | temporary separator |
| Root cause (system) | generic basket replaced the dedicated fixture; no change control for fixtures; work instruction not updated | dedicated fixture, fixture in change control, standard updated |

A useful test: if you fix the cause you named, would the same problem be prevented on the other lines and products too? If not, you have found a local cause, not the systemic one. Many plants document three levels for each problem: why it occurred, why it was not detected, and why the system allowed it (planning and change control). The last question is where lasting fixes come from.

### "Operator error" is not a root cause

When an RCA investigation ends at "operator did not follow the work instruction" (in other words, human error), ask the next question: why was that possible, and why was it likely? Typical answers: the instruction was ambiguous, the training was a signature on a form, the fixture allows the part in two orientations, the takt left no time for the check. Blaming the person closes the report and keeps the cause. It also teaches everyone to hide problems. A no-blame rule for RCA is a precondition for honest data, and in unionized plants it is often also a condition for getting operators into the room.

This does not mean nobody is accountable. Deliberate violations of safety rules are a disciplinary matter and are handled through that process, separately from RCA. Where an incident involves injury, OSHA recordkeeping and any required investigation follow their own rules; the RCA should feed into them, not replace them.

### When RCA is required: CAPA in regulated industries

In many industries, RCA is not optional:

- **Medical devices (US):** FDA's Quality Management System Regulation (QMSR), effective February 2, 2026, amends 21 CFR Part 820 and incorporates ISO 13485:2016 by reference [17]. Corrective and preventive action (CAPA) is part of that framework: ISO 13485:2016 covers corrective action in clause 8.5.2 and preventive action in clause 8.5.3, both aimed at eliminating the causes of actual or potential nonconformities [33].
- **Automotive:** IATF 16949 requires documented problem-solving processes, and many OEMs prescribe 8D formats for supplier complaints [unverified: clause number to be checked against the standard].
- **Aerospace:** AS9100 requires corrective action with root cause determination; SAE AS13000 defines an 8D-based problem-solving requirement for aerospace suppliers [unverified: AS13000 scope to be confirmed on sae.org]. The [Aerospace Production Planning guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/aerospace-production-planning/) covers the AS9100 context.

In these settings, the quality of the root cause statement is audited. "Retrained operator" as the only corrective action is a weak answer to an auditor or customer, because it rarely addresses the system cause.

## Root cause analysis tools: 5 Whys, fishbone, Pareto, fault tree, 8D and more

The RCA techniques below range from a 15-minute 5 Whys to a fault tree that takes days. The matrix shows which fits which problem.

### Tool selection matrix

| Tool | Best for | Data needed | Team effort | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5 Whys | simple, single-chain problems; drilling down one branch | little | 15–60 min | follows one path, depends on the people asking; easy to stop too early |
| Fishbone (Ishikawa) | structuring many possible causes | little | 1–2 h workshop | produces hypotheses, not proof |
| Pareto chart | ranking problems or causes by frequency or cost | counts or costs | 1 h | shows what is frequent, not why |
| Is / is not analysis | narrowing where and when the problem occurs | observation | 1 h | needs a clearly bounded problem |
| Fault tree analysis | safety- or reliability-critical failures with several contributing events | failure logic, optionally probabilities | days | effort; needs system knowledge |
| FMEA | anticipating failure modes before they happen | engineering knowledge | days | ratings are subjective |
| Hypothesis tests, regression, DOE | proving and quantifying causes | measured data | hours to weeks | needs data quality and statistical skill |
| 8D | customer complaints, supplier problems; structured team response | mixed | weeks | can become form-filling |
| Change analysis | "it worked before": find what changed | change records | hours | needs records of changes |
| Barrier analysis | why the existing safeguards failed | process knowledge | hours | focuses on defenses, not origins |

ASQ also lists events and causal factor analysis, management oversight and risk tree analysis (MORT) and Kepner-Tregoe problem solving as approaches [6]. In discrete manufacturing, the first eight tools in the table cover most needs. Whatever the RCA method, the goal is the same: determine the root cause of a problem with evidence, then act on it.

### 5 Whys

The Lean Enterprise Institute describes 5 Whys (also written "five whys") as the practice of asking why repeatedly whenever a problem is encountered, to get beyond the obvious symptoms and discover the root cause; it cites Taiichi Ohno's example of a machine that stopped working (Ohno 1988, p. 17) [18].

**Example (seal seat damage):**

1. Why did the body leak? The seal seat was scratched.
2. Why was it scratched? Bodies hit each other in the washing basket.
3. Why did they hit each other? The basket has no nests; parts lie loose.
4. Why does the basket have no nests? The dedicated fixture broke and was replaced by a generic basket.
5. Why was a generic basket accepted? Fixtures are not under change control, and nobody checks the work instruction when equipment is replaced.

Root cause (system): no change control for fixtures. Corrective action: dedicated fixture, and fixtures added to the change control procedure.

**Rules that make 5 Whys work:**

- Start from a precise problem statement, not a vague one.
- Go to the gemba and look at the parts; do not answer from a conference room.
- Each answer must be a fact that can be checked. Mark guesses as guesses and verify them.
- Five is a guideline. Stop when you reach a cause you can act on that prevents recurrence; that may take three or seven questions.
- Branch when an answer has two causes. A 5 Whys that never branches is often oversimplified.
- Test the chain backward: "therefore" from the root cause to the symptom must make sense at every step.

**Known limits.** In BMJ Quality & Safety, Alan Card argued that 5 Whys should be dropped as an accident-investigation tool in healthcare: it follows a single causal path, insists on one root cause, and points to the most distal cause even when a proximate control (such as a forcing function) would be more effective. He recommends system-focused alternatives such as fishbone diagrams and causal tree diagrams [19]. A manufacturing plant is less complex than a hospital ward, but the warning applies to complex issues with several interacting causes: use 5 Whys on each verified branch of a fishbone or fault tree, not as the only method.

### Fishbone (Ishikawa) diagram

The fishbone diagram (also called Ishikawa diagram or cause-and-effect diagram), named after Kaoru Ishikawa, groups possible causes of a problem into categories [20]. ASQ lists the 6 Ms: materials, machinery, methods, measurement, manpower and Mother Nature (environment), with money as an optional seventh [20]. Many US plants say "people" and "environment" instead of "manpower" and "Mother Nature"; the categories are an aid, not a rule.

**Procedure (based on ASQ [20]):**

1. Agree on the problem statement and write it at the head of the fish.
2. Draw the main category branches.
3. Brainstorm causes, asking "why does this happen?" for each idea, and place it under a category.
4. Add sub-causes as smaller branches.
5. When ideas slow down, look at branches with few ideas.
6. Select the most likely causes for verification. Mark each with how it will be checked (data, experiment, observation).

**Example (porosity, abbreviated):**

| Category | Candidate causes |
|---|---|
| Materials | alloy composition variation; supplier skipped vacuum impregnation; lot-to-lot variation |
| Machinery (casting at supplier) | die temperature; shot pressure; vent blockage |
| Methods | impregnation step removed in cost-down; no porosity acceptance criterion in drawing |
| Measurement | leak tester false fails; no X-ray sampling at incoming |
| People | new die-casting crew at supplier (unconfirmed) |
| Environment | humidity in summer months |

The fishbone does not rank or prove anything. Its output is a list of hypotheses for the Analyze phase. A common mistake is to vote on the "root cause" at the end of the workshop and skip verification.

### Pareto chart

A Pareto chart is a bar chart of frequencies or costs sorted from largest to smallest, usually with a cumulative percentage line; ASQ counts it among the seven basic quality tools [21]. A Pareto analysis usually comes early in an RCA, because it shows which problem or cause to work on first. The underlying "vital few" idea comes from Joseph Juran, who named it after the economist Vilfredo Pareto and later wrote that he had "mistakenly applied the wrong name to the principle," because Pareto's models concerned the distribution of wealth [29].

**Procedure (based on ASQ [21]):** choose categories, choose the measure (frequency, cost, time), set the period, collect data, subtotal by category, draw bars from tallest to shortest, group small categories as "other", add the cumulative line on a secondary axis.

**Tips:**

- **Pareto by cost, not only by count.** 40 scratches at $5 rework rank below 6 cracked castings at $400 scrap.
- **Pareto of a Pareto.** Break the biggest bar down again (porosity by port location, by supplier, by die cavity).
- **Same period, same definitions.** A Pareto mixing two months with different defect codes misleads.
- **Do not force 80/20.** In the running example the top two causes are 70%; that is fine.

### Is / is not analysis

A simple, underused tool for bounding a problem, associated with Kepner-Tregoe problem analysis [6]:

| Dimension | Is | Is not (but could be) | Distinction | Possible cause |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| What | porosity leaks in port P2 | port P1, P3 | P2 is at the thickest wall section | shrinkage porosity at thick section |
| Where | supplier A castings | supplier B castings | A skips impregnation | missing impregnation |
| When | since March 2024 | before March 2024 | cost-down change at supplier A | process change |
| How much | 2–3% of A parts | 0.8% of B parts | | |

The "distinction" column is where causes show up. Combined with change analysis ("what changed in March 2024?"), it often finds the cause faster than a fishbone.

### Fault tree analysis

Fault tree analysis (FTA) works top-down from an undesired event and shows, with logic gates, which combinations of lower-level events cause it. It was developed in 1962 at Bell Laboratories by H. A. Watson for the US Air Force's Minuteman I launch control system; Boeing later extended it and applied it to civil aircraft, and the US Nuclear Regulatory Commission's Fault Tree Handbook (NUREG-0492, 1981) and NASA's Fault Tree Handbook with Aerospace Applications (2002) standardized it. IEC 61025 is the international standard [22].

**Gates:**

- **OR gate:** the output event occurs if any input occurs. For independent inputs: P = 1 − (1 − P₁)(1 − P₂) ≈ P₁ + P₂ for small probabilities [22].
- **AND gate:** the output occurs only if all inputs occur. For independent inputs: P = P₁ × P₂ [22].

**Worked example (illustrative probabilities per valve body):** Top event "leaking valve body ships to customer".

- Event A: porosity leak passes undetected because tester fixture seal is worn: 0.004
- Event B: plug not torqued and not detected: 0.002
- Event C: seal seat damaged (0.01) **AND** event D: leak test skipped by a fault in the test sequence logic (0.05)

Top event = A OR B OR (C AND D)

- C AND D = 0.01 × 0.05 = 0.0005
- Top = 1 − (1 − 0.004)(1 − 0.002)(1 − 0.0005) = **0.0065**, about 6,500 per million bodies, or roughly 312 escapes per year at 48,000 units if nothing were done (illustrative inputs).

What the tree shows: A and B are single points of failure that dominate the result. Adding a second, independent check (reference-leak verification each shift) to A turns it into an AND gate and reduces it by orders of magnitude. That is the value of FTA: it shows where a redundant control pays off.

FTA is worth the effort for safety- and reliability-critical problems, field failures with several contributing causes, and design reviews. For a scrap problem with one clear failure mode it is overkill.

### FMEA

Failure mode and effects analysis (FMEA) is preventive: it looks for what could fail before it does. In DMAIC it appears in Improve (to check the new process) and Control (as a living document). Each failure mode gets a severity (S), occurrence (O) and detection (D) rating, usually on 1–10 scales.

Traditional FMEAs rank risks with the risk priority number RPN = S × O × D. The AIAG & VDA FMEA Handbook (2019) replaced RPN with an "action priority" (AP) table that gives severity more weight, because RPN treats, for example, S = 10, O = 2, D = 2 (RPN 40) as less urgent than S = 4, O = 4, D = 4 (RPN 64) [unverified: AIAG & VDA FMEA Handbook (2019) not accessible online during research; confirm the AP logic against the handbook before publication].

| Failure mode | Effect | S | Cause | O | Current control | D | RPN | Action |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Porosity at port P2 | external leak in field | 8 | missing impregnation | 6 | 100% leak test | 3 | 144 | impregnation in supplier control plan; incoming audit |
| Seal seat scratch | leak, rework | 6 | loose parts in basket | 5 | leak test | 3 | 90 | dedicated nest fixture (poka-yoke) |
| Tester false pass | leaking part ships | 9 | worn fixture seal | 3 | none | 8 | 216 | reference-leak check each shift, interlock |

The highest-risk line is the one with no current control, not the one with the most defects. That is the typical FMEA insight.

### 8D problem solving

The eight disciplines (8D) model is a problem-solving approach commonly used in the automotive industry and also applied in other sectors [23]. Ford described it in its 1987 manual *Team Oriented Problem Solving* (TOPS) and later revised it as Global 8D (G8D), which added the D0 step; the history is disputed, since some sources credit earlier US military practice [24].

| Discipline | ASQ name [23] | What it means in practice |
|---|---|---|
| D0 | Plan | decide whether 8D is needed; emergency response |
| D1 | Establish a team | cross-functional team with a champion |
| D2 | Define and describe the problem | is/is not, 5W2H, quantified |
| D3 | Develop and execute an interim containment plan | sort, 100% inspection, quarantine; protect the customer now |
| D4 | Determine, identify, and verify root causes | occurrence root cause and escape (detection) root cause |
| D5 | Choose and verify permanent corrections | prove the fix works before full implementation |
| D6 | Implement and validate corrective actions | roll out, remove containment, validate with data |
| D7 | Take preventive measures | update FMEA, control plan, standards; apply to similar products |
| D8 | Congratulate your team | recognize the team, close the report |

**Why 8D is popular with customers:** D3 (containment) comes before root cause, so the customer is protected within hours, and D4 asks for both the occurrence root cause and the escape point (why it was not detected). Many customers expect D3 within 24 hours and a complete 8D within a set number of days [unverified: response times vary by customer].

### Verifying a root cause before corrective action

A root cause is verified when at least one of these holds:

1. **On/off test:** removing the cause removes the effect; reintroducing it (where safe) brings the effect back.
2. **Statistical evidence:** stratified data show the effect only where the cause is present (supplier A vs. B), with a plausible mechanism.
3. **Physical evidence:** teardown, metallography, X-ray, a scratch matching the basket wire.
4. **Logic check:** the chain from root cause to symptom holds in both directions, and the cause explains all observed facts in the is/is-not table.

If none of these is available, record the cause as "probable" and plan the verification. Corrective actions on unverified causes are a major source of repeat problems.

## DMAIC vs. 8D vs. A3 vs. PDCA: which to use

All four are structured problem-solving cycles. They differ in scope, speed and who drives them.

| Criterion | PDCA | A3 | 8D | DMAIC |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Origin | Shewhart/Deming cycle | Toyota | Ford (TOPS, 1987) [24] | Motorola, GE |
| Typical trigger | any improvement idea, daily problems | a defined problem owned by a manager or team leader | customer complaint, supplier issue, field failure | chronic, measurable problem with unknown causes |
| Containment step | no | sometimes (countermeasures) | yes, D3, early | not formally |
| Statistical depth | low | low to medium | medium | high |
| Duration | days to weeks | weeks | days to weeks (often with customer deadline) | weeks to months |
| Who leads | anyone | the problem owner, coached by a manager | cross-functional team, quality engineer | Green or Black Belt |
| Documentation | short | one 11 × 17 in sheet | 8D report (customer format) | project storyboard, gate reviews |

**Rule of thumb:**

- A customer is affected now -> **8D** (containment first).
- A chronic problem with unclear causes, enough data, and a financial impact worth a project -> **DMAIC**.
- A problem a supervisor or engineer can own and solve with structured thinking and a coach -> **A3**. See the [A3 problem solving guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/lean-manufacturing/a3-problem-solving/).
- Small, fast improvements and daily learning -> **PDCA**. See the [PDCA cycle guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/lean-manufacturing/pdca-cycle/).

They combine well. An 8D's D4 can run a short DMAIC Analyze phase; a DMAIC Improve phase runs PDCA cycles in the pilot; a Black Belt project can be summarized on an A3 for the leadership review. Small improvements found along the way belong in [kaizen](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/lean-manufacturing/kaizen/), not in the project charter.

## DMADV and Design for Six Sigma

DMAIC improves a process that exists. When the process or product does not exist yet, or the existing one cannot reach the target by improvement, Design for Six Sigma (DFSS) applies. GE's 1998 annual report already mentions new products designed with Design for Six Sigma methods [4].

The most common DFSS roadmap is DMADV:

| Phase | Purpose | Typical tools |
|---|---|---|
| Define | goals, customer, scope of the new design | charter, VOC, market data |
| Measure | translate customer needs into measurable CTQs and targets | CTQ tree, quality function deployment (QFD) |
| Analyze | develop and evaluate design concepts | concept selection (Pugh matrix), design FMEA, simulation |
| Design | detail design, optimize parameters and tolerances | DOE, tolerance analysis, reliability modeling |
| Verify | confirm performance in pilot and production | pilot runs, capability studies, PPAP or first article inspection |

Other variants (IDOV, DMADOV) exist; the names matter less than the principle: build capability into the design instead of inspecting quality in later. Process simulation and digital twins are useful in the Analyze and Design phases, see the [Digital Twin in Manufacturing guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/digital-twin-manufacturing/).

## Six Sigma belts, roles and certification in the US

### The roles

| Role | Typical responsibilities | Time on Six Sigma |
|---|---|---|
| Executive leadership | sets strategic priorities, funds the program | small |
| Champion (sponsor) | selects and sponsors projects, removes barriers, chairs gate reviews | a few hours per project per month |
| Process owner | owns the process before, during and after; signs off the Control phase | part-time |
| Master Black Belt | coaches Black Belts, owns method and training, reviews statistics | full-time |
| Black Belt | leads complex, cross-functional projects; coaches Green Belts | often full-time |
| Green Belt | leads smaller projects within their own area, alongside the regular job | part-time |
| Yellow Belt | team member with basic knowledge; contributes data and process knowledge | occasional |
| White Belt (some programs) | awareness level | occasional |

ASQ describes Black Belts as key people with extensive training in advanced statistics and project management [1]. There is no single standard for what each Six Sigma belt must know or how many training hours it takes; the content depends on the employer or certification body. GE's 1998 report shows the scale a large program can reach: 5,000 full-time Master Black Belts and Black Belts [4].

### Certification: ASQ vs. IASSC vs. company programs

The two best-known independent certifying bodies in the US are ASQ and IASSC. Their models differ fundamentally: ASQ requires work experience and, for the Black Belt, project affidavits; IASSC tests knowledge only.

| Item (as of November 2026) | ASQ Green Belt (CSSGB) | ASQ Black Belt (CSSBB) | ASQ Master Black Belt (CMBB) | IASSC Black Belt (ICBB) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Experience requirement | 3 years full-time paid work in one or more areas of the Body of Knowledge; no educational waivers [25] | full-time paid role; 3 years in the BoK areas plus one completed project with signed affidavit, or two projects with affidavits [8] | current ASQ Black Belt; portfolio showing at least 5 years as BB/MBB or 10 completed BB projects; Green Belt work not counted [26] | none [9] |
| Exam | 110 questions (100 scored), 4 h 18 min exam time, open book (bound references) [25] | 165 questions (150 scored), 4 h 18 min, open book [8] | 110 multiple-choice items plus a performance-based section, 5 h 20 min, Prometric test centers in July and November [26] | 150 questions, 240 minutes, closed book with formulas provided, pass mark 70% [9] |
| Exam fee | $483 (members save $100) [25] | $585 (members save $100) [8] | see ASQ | see PeopleCert |
| Renewal | via ASQ recertification | via ASQ recertification | via ASQ recertification | every 3 years [9] |

ASQ also offers a Six Sigma Yellow Belt (CSSYB) [27]. Fees and formats change; check the providers' pages before you book.

**Which one?** For an employer, ASQ's project affidavit is evidence that the person has completed real projects. IASSC is accessible to people without project access, for example students or career changers, and proves knowledge, not application. Many large companies certify belts internally against their own project standard, which is often the most relevant credential for promotion inside that company. A certificate from a short online course with no exam and no project has little weight with US employers.

**Is certification worth it?** Whether a course is called Six Sigma Green Belt or Lean Six Sigma Green Belt matters less than whether it includes an exam and a real project. For quality engineers, CI specialists and operations leaders in manufacturing, a Green or Black Belt is a common job requirement or preference. Salary claims on certification sites are usually self-reported surveys by training sellers; treat them with care. The stronger argument is practical: a Green Belt project that saves a measurable amount is a better résumé line than the certificate itself.

**Paying for it.** Employers often cover training as part of a development plan. NIST MEP centers support small and midsize manufacturers with process improvement training and coaching, and NIST's lean and process improvement page lists Six Sigma among the methods centers can help apply [31]; whether a given state center offers belt training is worth asking directly [unverified: offers differ by center]. Support resources are listed in the [operational excellence guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/operational-excellence/). Skills planning for these roles is covered in the [manufacturing workforce skills guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/operational-excellence/manufacturing-workforce-skills/).

## How to implement Six Sigma: a 12-month roadmap

This Six Sigma implementation roadmap is for a single plant of roughly 200 to 1,000 employees starting a program. It is a practitioner sequence, not a standard [unverified: phase durations are typical experience values].

| Phase | Months | Key activities | Exit criteria |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0. Readiness | 1 | leadership decides why (strategy link), names a Champion and a deployment lead; checks data availability; agrees how savings are validated with finance; informs union or employee representatives | written deployment charter |
| 1. Project selection | 1–2 | builds a project pipeline from cost of poor quality, customer complaints, scrap Pareto and KPIs; scores projects on impact, data availability and scope | 3–5 scoped pilot projects with Champions |
| 2. First wave training | 2–5 | trains 1–2 Black Belts (external or MEP) and 6–10 Green Belts, each with a live project; Yellow Belt awareness for team members | each trainee on a chartered project |
| 3. First projects | 3–8 | runs projects with monthly gate reviews; coach supports statistics | at least 3 projects through Control, savings validated |
| 4. Integration | 6–10 | links project results to the KPI system and shop floor boards; adds RCA standard (8D, 5 Whys sheet) for daily problems; builds a project database | RCA standard used in tier meetings |
| 5. Second wave and review | 9–12 | trains second wave; reviews program results against charter; decides on scale-up | program review with leadership; plan for year 2 |

**Five decisions that determine success:**

1. **Project selection by the business, not by the trainee.** Projects should come from strategic goals and measured losses, not from whatever the Green Belt finds interesting.
2. **Champions who show up.** A gate review that the Champion skips is a signal to everyone.
3. **Finance in the loop from day one.** Agree in advance how savings are counted. Inflated savings destroy credibility quickly.
4. **Data before training.** If the plant cannot pull scrap by cause from its systems, fix that first or the projects will spend months on data collection.
5. **Protect the Control phase.** Reward closed and sustained projects, not started ones.

**Small and midsize plants.** A 120-person shop does not need a Master Black Belt. Two trained Green Belts, an external coach for statistics (an MEP center or a consultant), one project per quarter and a disciplined 8D/5 Whys standard for daily problems are a realistic program.

| KPI | Formula | Example | Note |

|---|---|---|

| Validated hard savings | controller-validated, annualized | compare with program cost |

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | What happens | Better |
|---|---|---|
| Solution in the problem statement | the team proves what the manager already believed | state the gap in numbers; causes come in Analyze |
| Projects too big ("reduce scrap plantwide") | project runs for a year and stalls | one product family, one failure mode, 4–6 months |
| Skipping measurement system analysis | the project optimizes noise; in the running example, part of the "defect rate" was the tester | MSA in every Measure phase |
| Sigma level from long-term data plus 1.5 | performance overstated | report DPMO and the method used |
| Capability on an unstable process | Cpk predicts nothing | control chart first |
| Fishbone vote as "root cause" | corrective action on an unverified hypothesis | verify with data or on/off test |
| "Operator error" as root cause | problem returns; workforce stops reporting | ask why the system allowed it |
| Statistics as a show | complex tests nobody understands; process owner does not trust the result | simplest analysis that answers the question; graphs before p-values |
| Weak Control phase | gains fade within months | control plan, reaction plan, audits, 12-month review |
| Inflated savings | finance stops believing the program | controller validates every number |
| Belts trained without projects | certificates, no results | no training seat without a chartered project |
| Six Sigma as a separate department | improvement becomes "their job" | belts work in line organization; results on line KPIs |

## Criticism and limits of Six Sigma

Six Sigma has serious, published critics.

### 1. "Nothing new"

Joseph Juran described Six Sigma as "a basic version of quality improvement", and Philip Crosby argued that the 3.4-per-million standard does not go far enough [5]. Much of the toolbox (control charts, capability, DOE, Pareto, fishbone) predates Motorola by decades. The fair reply: Six Sigma's contribution was organizational (roles, project selection, financial validation), not statistical.

### 2. The 1.5-sigma shift

The shift is a convention with a thin empirical basis, criticized by Wheeler and others [5]. See the section above. The practical risk is misreporting, not the method itself.

### 3. Mixed financial evidence

A 2006 Fortune article reported an analysis by Charles Holland of the consulting firm Qualpro (which sells a competing process) that 91% of 58 large companies with Six Sigma programs had trailed the S&P 500 since starting them [5]. The source has an obvious conflict of interest, and stock performance has many causes. It is still a useful warning against reading GE-style savings claims as proof of company-level success.

### 4. Project failure rates

A 2010 Wall Street Journal report stated that more than 60% of such projects fail [5]. A systematic literature review of 56 papers from 1995 to 2013 identified 34 common failure factors for Lean, Six Sigma and Lean Six Sigma; the top ones were lack of top management commitment and involvement, lack of communication, lack of training and education, and limited resources [30]. The practitioner view, consistent with the mistakes table above, is that programs fail on leadership, project selection and the Control phase far more often than on statistics.

### 5. Innovation and research

The editor of Design News argued in 2007 that Six Sigma is inappropriate for research environments, because its focus on reducing variability conflicts with the experimentation that discovery needs [5]. Variation reduction is the right goal for a stable production process and the wrong goal for an R&D lab.

### 6. Bureaucracy and the belt economy

Gate reviews, templates and certification can turn into paperwork, and a market of cheap online "certificates" has diluted what a belt means. The fix is in the design of the program: fewer, better projects; project affidavits; savings validated by finance.

### 7. Workforce effects

If Six Sigma is run as an expert program, operators experience it as something done to them: data collected on their work, changes decided in conference rooms. That damages trust and the quality of the information projects depend on. Operators belong on the team, especially in Measure and Analyze, where they know which shift does what differently. Where cost savings translate into headcount reductions, people stop contributing ideas; a stated employment-security policy for improvement-driven productivity gains is a common answer in lean and Six Sigma programs. In unionized plants, involve the union early in how data is used. Safety improvements and ergonomics are legitimate Six Sigma project goals; OSHA's ergonomics resources are a good starting point for the latter [28].

### When not to use Six Sigma

- The cause is visible on a gemba walk: fix it with lean tools or a PDCA cycle.
- There is no data and no way to get it within weeks.
- Volume is too low for statistics (a few units per year); use engineering analysis, FTA or FMEA.
- The constraint is capacity at one bottleneck, not variation; see the [Theory of Constraints guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/theory-of-constraints/) (planned).
- The task is innovation, not stabilization.

## Six Sigma, data and AI: the digital angle

Six Sigma was designed for a world of manual data collection: check sheets, a few subgroups per shift, a Black Belt with statistics software. Plants now have machine data, MES records and test logs with millions of rows. This changes the method in useful and in risky ways.

### What changes for the better

- **Measure gets faster.** If scrap, test results and process parameters are already recorded per part or batch, the baseline that used to take six weeks of manual collection may be available in a day. The precondition is a data model that links results to process context (machine, tool, material lot, shift) and part genealogy, so that a failed serial number can be traced back to its casting lot and machining spindle. That link is the job of an MES; see the [MES and ISA-95 guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/mes-isa-95/) (planned).
- **SPC becomes continuous.** Gages and sensors can feed control charts automatically, with alerts to the team leader's phone. The [manufacturing IT with Azure guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/manufacturing-it-azure/) describes typical architectures.
- **Analyze has more candidates.** With hundreds of process parameters recorded, regression and machine learning models can rank which inputs correlate with defects. This is a strong way to generate hypotheses.
- **Process mining** reconstructs the real flow of orders and parts from system event logs, which helps in Define and Measure for transactional and order-flow problems. See the [process mining guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/digital-twin-manufacturing/process-mining-manufacturing/) (planned).
- **Simulation and digital twins** let a team test Improve options virtually before a pilot, especially when the change affects flow and capacity, not only quality. See the [Digital Twin in Manufacturing guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/digital-twin-manufacturing/).
- **Predictive maintenance** extends the Control logic from part characteristics to equipment condition; see the [Predictive Maintenance and IIoT guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/predictive-maintenance-iiot/) (planned).

### What stays the same, or gets riskier

- **Correlation is not a root cause.** A model that finds coolant temperature correlated with porosity has produced a hypothesis. Verification (on/off test, physical mechanism) is still required. With hundreds of variables, some will correlate by chance.
- **Measurement system analysis applies to sensors too.** A drifting sensor feeding an automatic control chart produces confident nonsense. Calibration and MSA belong in the data pipeline.
- **Data quality decides.** If a defect code is chosen from a long drop-down list at the end of a shift, the Pareto shows the habits of the person typing, not the defects.
- **More alarms are not better control.** An automatic chart with all WECO rules on 400 characteristics generates false alarms every hour; operators learn to ignore them. Select characteristics and rules deliberately.
- **Generative AI** can draft a 5 Whys, an 8D report or a fishbone from a problem description. That is useful as a checklist of possible causes. It cannot see the part, and it tends to produce plausible chains that nobody verified. Treat AI output as brainstorming input, never as the root cause.
- **OT security.** Connecting test stands and machines to cloud analytics creates attack surface. Segment networks and follow ISA/IEC 62443; see the [OT cybersecurity guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/ot-cybersecurity-iec-62443/) (planned).

### Microsoft 365 and everyday tools

For many plants, the realistic digital stack for Six Sigma is modest: Excel or a statistics package (Minitab, JMP, or R/Python) for analysis, Power BI for Pareto and trend dashboards, Microsoft Lists or the quality system for 8D and CAPA tracking, Teams channels for project teams. Excel's `NORM.S.INV`, `NORM.S.DIST` and the Analysis ToolPak cover the calculations in this article. A Black Belt who can explain a control chart to a supervisor is still worth more than any tool.

## Frequently asked questions (FAQ)

### What is Six Sigma in simple terms?

Six Sigma is a method for reducing defects by reducing variation. A team measures how a process performs, proves with data why it produces defects, fixes the causes and puts controls in place so the problem does not return. The name comes from the statistical goal of fitting six standard deviations between the process average and the nearest specification limit.

### What are the 5 steps of DMAIC?

Define (problem, goal, scope), Measure (baseline and measurement system), Analyze (verified root causes), Improve (tested solutions) and Control (sustain the gain with control plans, SPC and standards) [1][10].

### What does 3.4 defects per million mean, and why is it called Six Sigma?

ASQ defines Six Sigma quality as 3.4 defects per million opportunities, accounting for a 1.5-sigma shift in the mean [1]. A process with six standard deviations between mean and limit in the short term is assumed to drift by 1.5 sigma over time; the remaining 4.5 sigma leave a one-sided tail of 3.4 per million. Without the shift, a centered six-sigma process would produce about 0.002 per million.

### How do you calculate sigma level?

Calculate DPMO = defects ÷ (units × opportunities) × 1,000,000. Then sigma level = NORM.S.INV(1 − DPMO ÷ 1,000,000) + 1.5. Example: 46 defects in 5,000 units with 8 opportunities each gives DPMO = 1,150 and a sigma level of about 4.55.

### What is the 1.5 sigma shift, and is it valid?

It is a convention that models long-term drift of the process mean as 1.5 standard deviations. It has a thin empirical basis and has been criticized, notably by Donald Wheeler [5]. Use it only to convert to the conventional sigma table, and always state when you have applied it.

### What is the difference between Cp and Cpk?

Cp compares the specification width with the process spread and ignores where the process is centered. Cpk also accounts for centering: it uses the distance from the mean to the nearer specification limit. If Cp is high and Cpk low, center the process; if both are low, reduce variation [7].

### What is a good Cpk value?

NIST's example uses at least 1.0 as a minimum wish [7]. In industry, 1.33 is a common minimum for ongoing production and 1.67 for new processes in automotive supplier approval, but customer requirements decide [unverified: see capability section].

### What is root cause analysis?

ASQ describes root cause analysis as a collective term for approaches, tools and techniques used to uncover the causes of problems, and the root cause as the factor that caused a nonconformance and should be permanently eliminated [6]. In practice, it means finding the underlying cause of a problem and fixing that instead of the symptom.

### What are the steps of root cause analysis?

Define the problem in numbers, contain it if customers are affected, collect data, list potential causes (fishbone, is/is-not), follow the causal chain (5 Whys, fault tree), verify the root cause with evidence, implement corrective actions, then check effectiveness and prevent recurrence on similar products and lines. Sources count five to eight steps; the order is what matters.

### Is root cause analysis part of Six Sigma?

Yes. The Analyze phase of DMAIC is a root cause analysis, supported by data from the Measure phase and tested in the Improve phase. RCA is also used outside Six Sigma: in 8D reports (D4), A3 problem solving and CAPA in regulated industries.

### How long does a root cause analysis take?

It depends on the problem and the tool. A 5 Whys on a simple, single-chain problem takes 15 to 60 minutes, a fishbone workshop one to two hours, a fault tree days, an 8D days to weeks, and a DMAIC project weeks to months. Plan the time for verification: that is the step most often cut short.

### What are the main root cause analysis tools?

5 Whys, fishbone (Ishikawa) diagram, Pareto chart, is/is-not analysis, fault tree analysis, FMEA, statistical tests and 8D as a framework. The selection matrix in this article shows which fits which problem.

### When should I use 5 Whys vs. a fishbone diagram?

Use a fishbone when many causes are possible and you need to structure them. Use 5 Whys to drill down one verified branch to the system cause. For anything complex, combine them: fishbone first, then 5 Whys on the branches the data supports.

### What is 8D problem solving, and how does it differ from DMAIC?

8D is an eight-discipline team method common in automotive, with containment (D3) before root cause analysis (D4) [23]. It is the standard response to customer complaints. DMAIC is a longer, more statistical project method for chronic problems. Many companies use 8D for complaints and DMAIC for the recurring problems the 8Ds reveal.

### What is the difference between lean and Six Sigma?

Lean focuses on reducing waste and improving flow; Six Sigma reduces variation and defects with statistics. Lean Six Sigma combines them [1]. Rule of thumb: if the cause is visible in the workflow, start with lean; if it is hidden in the data, use Six Sigma.

### Should I learn lean or Six Sigma first?

For most people in manufacturing, lean first. Lean principles such as flow, pull, standard work and 5S are visible on the shop floor and need little statistics, and many problems disappear once the waste is gone. Six Sigma then adds the statistical analysis for the problems that stay hidden in the data. Combined Lean Six Sigma courses teach both; which credential counts depends on what employers in your field ask for.

### What are the Six Sigma belts?

The usual belt levels are White, Yellow, Green, Black and Master Black Belt, plus Champions as sponsors. Green Belts lead projects part-time, Black Belts lead complex projects and coach, Master Black Belts own the method. Requirements are not standardized; they depend on the certifying body or employer.

### Is Six Sigma certification worth it?

For quality, CI and operations roles in manufacturing, a Green or Black Belt is a frequent requirement or preference. Choose a credential with an exam, ideally with a project requirement (ASQ asks for project affidavits for the Black Belt [8]). Completed projects with validated results matter more than the certificate.

### How much does Six Sigma certification cost?

ASQ lists $483 for the Green Belt exam and $585 for the Black Belt exam, with $100 off for members (November 2026) [25][8]. Training is extra and varies widely; employers and NIST MEP centers often fund or subsidize it.

### Is Six Sigma still relevant?

Yes, for chronic quality problems with measurable variation, which every plant has. What has changed is the setting: more automatic data, closer integration with lean and daily management, and less tolerance for large expert bureaucracies. The statistical core (SPC, capability, DOE, hypothesis testing) is as valid as ever.

### What is DMADV?

DMADV (Define, Measure, Analyze, Design, Verify) is the Design for Six Sigma roadmap for creating new products or processes, used when an existing process cannot reach the target by improvement or does not exist yet.

## Conclusion and first steps

Six Sigma is three things at once: a metric (defects per million opportunities and the sigma level), a method (DMAIC) and an organization (Champions and belts). The metric is the least important of the three and the most misunderstood; the 1.5-sigma shift is a convention, and honest reporting uses DPMO, ppm and Ppk with stated definitions. The method is where the value lies, and its center is the Analyze phase: a root cause analysis that proves causes with data before anyone spends money on solutions.

The tools are old and proven. Control charts tell routine variation from signals. Capability indices tell whether a stable process fits the specification. The 5 Whys, fishbone, Pareto, is/is-not and fault tree structure the search for causes; 8D organizes the response to customer problems. None of them works if the measurement system is not checked, the root cause is not verified or the Control phase is skipped. And none of them works if operators are treated as the cause instead of as the people who know the process best.

**Your first steps in the next 30 days**

- [ ] Pull the scrap and rework Pareto for the last six months, by cost, not only by count
- [ ] Pick one chronic, measurable problem from the top three bars and write a problem statement with numbers and without a cause
- [ ] Ask the plant controller to agree on how savings from that problem would be counted
- [ ] Check the measurement system for that problem (gage R&R or attribute agreement)
- [ ] Calculate the baseline: defect rate, DPMO, and for one key characteristic Cp and Cpk after checking stability on a control chart
- [ ] Introduce one RCA standard for daily problems: a 5 Whys sheet with a "verified?" column and an escape-cause question
- [ ] Review the last ten closed corrective actions: how many ended at "operator error" or "retrained"? Reopen the ones that recurred
- [ ] Decide on the first Green Belt candidates and give each a chartered project before training starts
- [ ] Check what your state's NIST MEP center offers for Six Sigma training and coaching
