# Supply Chain Resilience in Manufacturing: Risk Scoring, Buffers and a Plant-Level Playbook

*Updated: November 20, 2026 · Reading time: about 20 minutes*

*A cluster article of the m365.fm [operational excellence guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/operational-excellence/).*

> **Short answer:** Supply chain resilience is a manufacturer's ability to keep supplying customers when a supplier, a route or a region fails, and to recover quickly afterward. In a plant it comes down to three questions per critical part: how long can we keep shipping without it (time to survive), how long until supply is back (time to recover), and what closes the gap at the lowest cost.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Disruptions are frequent. A McKinsey Global Institute analysis found that companies face supply disruptions lasting a month or longer about every 3.7 years on average [1].
> - Score risks per part, not per supplier: likelihood × impact × recoverability gives a ranking you can act on in a week.
> - Statistical safety stock covers demand and lead-time variability. It does not cover a 20-week supplier outage. That needs a separate, deliberately sized strategic stock or a second source.
> - Compare the yearly cost of a buffer or second source with the expected loss. In the worked example below, both a strategic stock and a second source cost less per year than the expected loss.
> - Small and mid-sized plants can get help: NIST MEP centers offer supply chain mapping, risk assessment and a national supplier scouting service [2].

## What supply chain resilience means for a plant

Ponomarov and Holcomb's review describes resilient supply chains as ones that prepare for unexpected events, respond to disruptions efficiently and recover to their original state or a better one [6]. Their main finding is that the term had been defined in many disciplines, from developmental psychology to ecology, without a unified theory [6]. For operations, the practical version has three capacities: absorb the first impact, adapt while the disruption lasts, and restore normal output afterward [7].

Three related terms get mixed up in meetings:

| Term | Question it answers | Plant example |
|---|---|---|
| Robustness | Can we absorb a shock without changing anything? | four weeks of a critical resin on site carry the plant through a short supplier outage |
| Resilience | Can we absorb, adapt and recover? | after a supplier fire, the plant reallocates stock, qualifies a second source and is back at full output in ten weeks |
| Agility | Can we respond quickly to changes in demand or supply? | the scheduler resequences orders within a day when a part is late |

Resilience is not the opposite of lean. A lean plant with a stable flow, short changeovers and a clear view of its value stream recovers faster because it sees the problem sooner and can resequence work. What lean thinking does not tell you is how much buffer to hold against rare, long disruptions. That is a risk decision, and it belongs in the same management system as quality and safety.

## How often disruptions happen and what they cost

The most cited figures come from a 2020 McKinsey Global Institute report based on industry research and a survey of supply chain executives [1]:

| Finding | Figure |
|---|---|
| Frequency of disruptions lasting a month or longer | about every 3.7 years on average |
| Expected losses over a decade | almost 45% of one year's profits (the report's exhibit gives 42% of one year's EBITDA) |
| Single 100-day production shock (scenario) | 30 to 50% of one year's EBITDA for most industries |
| Products where one country supplies 70% or more of exports | 180 |
| Executives (survey, May 2020) planning to make supply chains more resilient | 93% |

These are averages across industries and scenarios, not a forecast for your plant. They are useful for one thing: they move the discussion from "this will not happen to us" to "how much is it worth to be ready."

## A framework: SCOR DS processes as a resilience checklist

ASCM's Supply Chain Operations Reference model in its current digital version, SCOR DS, describes seven processes: Orchestrate, Plan, Order, Source, Transform, Fulfill and Return [5]. It also benchmarks performance attributes that include reliability, resilience and responsiveness through ASCM's SCORmark evaluation [5]. The process list makes a good checklist for resilience work because it forces you to look beyond purchasing:

| SCOR DS process | Resilience question for a plant | Typical owner |
|---|---|---|
| Orchestrate | Who owns supply risk, and how often does leadership review it? | operations director |
| Plan | Do S&OP scenarios include "supplier X down for 12 weeks"? | supply chain planning |
| Order | Can we allocate scarce output across customers by a rule agreed in advance? | sales operations |
| Source | Which parts are single- or sole-sourced, and where are tier-2 risks? | purchasing |
| Transform | Can we run alternative routings, materials or product variants? | engineering, production |
| Fulfill | Do we have alternative carriers, routes and ports? | logistics |
| Return | Can we recover usable material from returns and rework? | quality |

The Plan row links directly to the planned [S&OP, MRP and production planning guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/sop-mrp-production-planning/). The Transform row is where a plant's own capabilities matter most, for example short changeovers as described in the planned [SMED guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/smed-changeover-reduction/).

## Worked example 1: a supplier risk scoring matrix

*Illustrative example with fictional values.* A mid-sized maker of industrial controls scores six purchased items. Each is rated 1 to 5 on three criteria:

- **Likelihood (L):** chance of a disruption of more than two weeks in the next 12 months (1 = very unlikely, 5 = likely). Inputs: single or multiple sources, supplier financial health, region, past delivery performance.
- **Impact (I):** share of revenue that stops if the part is missing (1 = under 2%, 5 = over 40%).
- **Recoverability (R):** time to qualify and ramp an alternative (1 = under 2 weeks, 3 = 6 to 12 weeks, 5 = over 26 weeks).

Risk score = L × I × R (range 1 to 125).

| Item | Sourcing | L | I | R | Score | Action class |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A Microcontroller | single source, offshore fab | 4 | 5 | 5 | **100** | act now |
| D Wire harness | single source, cross-border | 3 | 4 | 4 | **48** | act now |
| E Custom gasket | sole source, small domestic supplier | 3 | 3 | 5 | **45** | act now |
| B Machined housing | two domestic suppliers | 2 | 4 | 2 | 16 | monitor quarterly |
| C Engineering resin | three suppliers, commodity | 3 | 3 | 1 | 9 | review annually |
| F Corrugated cartons | many local suppliers | 2 | 2 | 1 | 4 | review annually |

**Thresholds (set by the leadership team):** 40 or more = mitigation plan within 90 days and monthly review; 15 to 39 = quarterly review; under 15 = annual review.

**How to read it:**

1. The custom gasket (E) ranks almost as high as the wire harness although it costs cents. Cheap sole-sourced parts are the classic blind spot, because spend-based supplier reviews never look at them.
2. Recoverability is what separates A from C. Both have similar likelihood, but a resin can be bought elsewhere in days, while a new microcontroller may need redesign and requalification.
3. Multiplying ordinal scores is a heuristic, the same as the risk priority number in an FMEA. Use the score to rank and start discussions, not as a precise measure. Two items with 45 and 48 are equally urgent.

Run the scoring per part number for everything that can stop a line, not only for the top suppliers by spend. A bill of materials export and two hours with purchasing, engineering and planning are enough for a first pass.

## Worked example 2: time to survive vs. time to recover

Research with Ford Motor Company produced a risk-exposure model that estimates the effect of a disruption anywhere in the supply network without requiring a probability for each specific event first [3]. A plant can use the core logic without the full model by comparing two durations that the resilience literature calls time to recover and time to survive [7]:

- **Time to recover (TTR):** how long a supplier site needs to return to full output after a disruption.
- **Time to survive (TTS):** how long your plant can keep meeting demand with what it has: stock on hand, stock in transit, alternative sources that can ramp, and finished goods.

If TTS is shorter than TTR, the difference is the period in which you lose output.

*Illustrative example with fictional values* for item A, the microcontroller:

| Element | Weeks of demand covered |
|---|---|
| Stock on hand at the plant | 4 |
| Stock in transit | 3 |
| Finished goods that contain the part | 2 |
| **Time to survive (TTS)** | **9** |
| Supplier's estimated time to recover after a fab fire | 20 |
| **Gap (TTR − TTS)** | **11 weeks** |

**What the gap costs.** The product family that uses the part generates $600,000 revenue per week at a 30% contribution margin: $180,000 per week. An 11-week gap means $1.98 million of lost contribution, before penalties and lost customers.

**Expected loss.** If the team estimates a 5% chance per year of an outage that long (an assumption, documented as such), the expected loss is 5% × $1.98 million = **$99,000 per year**.

**Option 1: strategic stock of 11 weeks.** Weekly demand is 5,000 parts at $4.20, so 11 weeks = 55,000 parts = $231,000 of inventory. At an assumed annual carrying cost of 20% of inventory value (capital, storage, handling, obsolescence risk; use your controller's rate), the buffer costs **$46,200 per year**. It ties up $231,000 of cash, and electronic parts can age.

**Option 2: qualify a second source.** One-time engineering and qualification: $120,000. Price premium on 40% of volume: $0.15 × 104,000 parts = **$15,600 per year**. Spread the one-time cost over three years ($40,000 per year; engineering time is usually expensed, so check the accounting treatment with your controller) and the option costs about **$55,600 per year** in the first three years and $15,600 after that.

**Decision.** Both options cost less than the $99,000 expected loss. A common choice is a smaller bridge stock (for example 4 weeks, $84,000 of inventory, $16,800 per year) while the second source is qualified, then reduce the bridge once the second source delivers. Note what the calculation leaves out: customer penalties, reputational damage and the chance that a competitor wins the account. These push the decision further toward action.

Have your plant controller own the carrying-cost rate and the margin figures. A resilience decision without finance in the room tends to be reversed at the next inventory reduction target.

## Worked example 3: why safety stock is not a resilience buffer

The textbook safety stock formula with demand variability only is:

**Safety stock = z × σd × √LT**

with z the service factor (1.65 for about 95% cycle service level under a normal distribution), σd the standard deviation of weekly demand and LT the replenishment lead time in weeks.

*Illustrative values:* average weekly demand d = 5,000 parts, σd = 900 parts, LT = 8 weeks.

- Safety stock = 1.65 × 900 × √8 = 1.65 × 900 × 2.83 = **4,200 parts**, about 0.84 weeks of demand.

If lead time varies too (standard deviation σLT = 2 weeks), the common extended formula is:

**Safety stock = z × √(LT × σd² + d² × σLT²)**

- = 1.65 × √(8 × 900² + 5,000² × 2²) = 1.65 × √(6,480,000 + 100,000,000) = 1.65 × 10,319 = **17,000 parts**, about 3.4 weeks.

Two lessons:

1. **Lead-time variability dominates.** In this example it raises safety stock fourfold. Working with suppliers on delivery reliability often frees more inventory than any demand-forecasting project.
2. **Safety stock covers normal variation, not disruptions.** Neither 0.84 nor 3.4 weeks comes close to the 11-week gap in example 2. Keep strategic stock as a separate, named quantity with its own owner and review date, so the next inventory reduction push does not cut it by accident.

## Mitigation levers and what they cost

| Lever | Reduces | Typical cost | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Strategic stock of critical parts | TTS gap | carrying cost, cash, obsolescence | aging (electronics, chemicals, shelf life); must be separate from cycle and safety stock |
| Second source (dual sourcing) | likelihood and TTR | qualification effort, price premium, split volume | in regulated industries, qualification can take months (customer approval, first article inspection) |
| Alternative design or material | TTR | engineering time, testing | design changes need customer approval in automotive, aerospace and medical |
| Supplier development | likelihood | engineering and quality time | works best with long-term relationships |
| Capacity reservation or longer contracts | likelihood | minimum purchase commitments | demand risk shifts to you |
| Regionalizing or reshoring | likelihood (some risks), lead time | higher unit cost in many cases, investment | trades one risk set for another; check total cost, not unit price |
| Visibility into tier 2 and 3 | detection time | supplier surveys, data services | suppliers may not share; start with critical parts |
| Flexible plant (short changeovers, cross-trained staff) | TTR inside your fence | lean and training effort | needs the skills described in the [workforce skills cluster](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/operational-excellence/manufacturing-workforce-skills/) |

## Public programs and policy

**NIST MEP.** The Manufacturing Extension Partnership network offers supply chain mapping and risk assessment, plans such as alternate suppliers or safety stock, a national Supplier Scouting service that searches for domestic suppliers with the needed capabilities, supplier development across tiers and procurement strategy support [2]. For a small manufacturer without a risk team, the local MEP center is the obvious first call.

**Executive Order 14017.** Signed on February 24, 2021, "America's Supply Chains" stated the policy "to strengthen the resilience of America's supply chains" [4]. It ordered 100-day reviews of four product areas (semiconductors and advanced packaging, high-capacity batteries, critical minerals and strategic materials including rare earths, and pharmaceuticals and active ingredients) and one-year reviews of six industrial bases: defense, public health, information and communications technology, energy, transportation, and agricultural commodities and food [4]. For plant leaders, the reviews are useful background on where national concentration risks sit. As of November 2026 the Federal Register shows no revocation, but a March 2025 order (EO 14239) listed it among policies to be reviewed for modification [4][10]. Check its current status before relying on it in planning documents.

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Why it hurts | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Reviewing only the top suppliers by spend | cheap sole-sourced parts stop lines too | score by part and impact, not spend |
| Counting safety stock as disruption cover | buffer is too small and gets cut in inventory drives | separate strategic stock with its own owner |
| Ignoring tier 2 | your two "independent" suppliers buy from the same casting house | ask critical suppliers for their critical sources |
| Reshoring for unit cost alone | higher cost without the intended risk reduction | compare total landed cost and risk score |
| No allocation rule before the crisis | sales and operations argue while stock runs out | agree allocation rules now |
| A risk register nobody reviews | risks age, mitigations stall | monthly review in the operations meeting |
| Leaving finance out | buffers removed at the next working capital target | controller co-owns the decision memo |
| Treating resilience as a purchasing project | plant flexibility (changeovers, skills) is ignored | include engineering, production and HR |

## FAQ: supply chain resilience

### What is supply chain resilience?

It is the ability of a supply chain to prepare for unexpected events, respond to disruptions and recover to its original state or a better one [6]. For a manufacturer, it means continuing to ship when a supplier, route or region fails, and getting back to full output quickly.

### Why is supply chain resilience important?

Because disruptions are frequent and expensive. McKinsey Global Institute estimated that companies face disruptions of a month or longer about every 3.7 years on average and can expect losses of almost 45% of one year's profits over a decade [1].

### What is the difference between supply chain resilience and agility?

Agility is the speed of response to changes, such as resequencing orders when a part is late. Resilience is broader: absorbing a shock, adapting during it and recovering afterward. An agile plant recovers faster, but agility alone does not replace buffers or second sources for long outages.

### How do you measure supply chain resilience?

At plant level, use time to survive (TTS) and time to recover (TTR) per critical part, the gap between them, the share of "act now" risks with a funded mitigation, and supplier delivery reliability. Track the trend of your top risk scores monthly.

### What are examples of supply chain disruptions?

Well-documented ones include the Suez Canal blockage in March 2021 [8], the Renesas plant fire in March 2021 and the automotive chip shortage of 2020 to 2023 [9]. Plant-level disruptions are more common: a supplier insolvency, a quality hold, a fire at a tier-2 supplier or a cyberattack.

### How do you build a resilient supply chain?

List the parts that can stop your lines, score them (likelihood × impact × recoverability), compare TTS and TTR for the top risks, cost the options (strategic stock, second source, redesign) against the expected loss, write short playbooks and review them monthly. NIST MEP centers support small manufacturers with mapping and risk assessment [2].

### Is dual sourcing worth the cost?

For parts with a long recovery time and high impact, usually yes. In the worked example, qualifying a second source cost about $55,600 per year for three years against an expected loss of $99,000 per year. For commodity parts with many suppliers, it rarely pays.

### Does lean manufacturing make supply chains fragile?

Lean done well makes plants faster to detect and respond to problems. The fragility comes from cutting all buffers without a risk assessment, or from single sourcing chosen only for unit price. Size strategic stock deliberately and keep it separate from cycle and safety stock.

### What was Executive Order 14017?

A US presidential order signed on February 24, 2021, titled "America's Supply Chains". It ordered 100-day reviews of semiconductors, high-capacity batteries, critical minerals and pharmaceuticals, plus one-year reviews of six industrial bases [4].

### What are the four pillars of supply chain resilience?

Several guides group resilience into four pillars; IBM names them contingency, flexibility, visibility and collaboration [12]. In plant terms: contingency means playbooks and strategic stock for the top risks, flexibility means second sources and alternative capacity, visibility means knowing your tier-2 suppliers and their sites, and collaboration means sharing forecasts and risk data with key suppliers. The risk matrix in this article shows where each pillar matters most.

### What are the main strategies for supply chain resilience?

A systematic review of 91 studies, summarized in [7], found four recurring strategies: flexibility, redundancy, collaboration and agility. In plant terms: flexible capacity and skills, deliberate buffers and second sources, information sharing with suppliers and customers, and fast rescheduling.

### How can small manufacturers improve supply chain resilience?

Start with the critical parts list and the risk matrix from this article; it takes a few days, not a software project. Then ask your local NIST MEP center about supply chain risk assessment and the Supplier Scouting service for alternative domestic sources [2].

## Conclusion

Supply chain resilience becomes manageable when you make it concrete: a list of the parts that can stop your lines, a score that ranks them, and for the top items a comparison of how long you can survive with how long recovery takes. The gap has a price, and so do the options to close it. Put the numbers in front of finance and leadership, decide, and review monthly.

Resilience and lean are compatible. A plant with stable flow, short changeovers and cross-trained people recovers faster. What lean does not decide for you is how much strategic stock or second sourcing a rare but long disruption is worth. That decision belongs in your operational excellence system, owned by named people, with dates.
