# Sustainable Manufacturing: A Plant Leader's Guide to Energy per Unit, Scope 1-3 and Lean

*Updated: November 20, 2026 · Reading time: about 20 minutes*

*A cluster article of the m365.fm [operational excellence guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/operational-excellence/).*

> **Short answer:** Sustainable manufacturing is making products through economically sound processes that minimize negative environmental impacts while conserving energy and natural resources, as the US EPA defines it [1]. In a plant, that comes down to three measurable things: energy per good unit, greenhouse gas emissions by scope, and material losses such as scrap. All three respond to the same lean and TPM tools you already use.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Start with energy per good unit, normalized for volume. Raw monthly kWh misleads because a large share of plant energy is fixed baseload.
> - Scope 1 (fuels you burn) and Scope 2 (electricity and steam you buy) are a spreadsheet exercise with EPA emission factors. Scope 3 needs supplier data and takes longer.
> - Scrap is a carbon multiplier. In the value stream example below, raising one coating step from 92% to 98% yield cuts CO2 per good unit by about 6% without touching an energy asset.
> - US federal rules are in retreat (the SEC proposed rescinding its climate rule in May 2026), but California's SB 253 Scope 1 and 2 reporting went live in November 2026 and EU customers still pass CSRD and CBAM data requests down the chain.
> - Free help exists: DOE's 50001 Ready, Better Plants, university-run Industrial Training and Assessment Centers and NIST MEP centers.

This article is a cluster of the [operational excellence guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/operational-excellence/) on m365.fm. The pillar explains how a plant builds a management system for continuous improvement. This deep dive applies that system to energy and carbon: how to measure them per unit, where the losses hide in a value stream, which rules matter in 2026, and how to run a first 90 days without a sustainability department.

## What sustainable manufacturing means on the plant floor

The EPA definition quoted above has three parts: economically sound processes, lower environmental impact, and conservation of energy and resources [1]. Note that the EPA page is now an archived snapshot of the agency website as of January 19, 2025, so treat it as the reference definition rather than a live program page [1].

For a plant leader, the definition turns into a short list of things you can count:

| Dimension | What you measure | Typical unit | Lean link |
|---|---|---|---|
| Energy | electricity, natural gas, steam, compressed air | kWh, MMBtu, kBtu per good unit | waiting, overprocessing, idle running |
| Emissions | CO2e by Scope 1, 2 and 3 | metric tons CO2e, kg CO2e per good unit | follows energy and material use |
| Materials | scrap, rework, packaging, yield | % first-pass yield, lb per unit | defects, overproduction |
| Water | intake and discharge | gallons per unit | overprocessing (rinsing, cooling) |
| Hazardous waste | regulated waste streams | lb per month | defects, inventory (expired material) |

The research literature calls the overlap of these goals with lean "lean and green"; a systematic review in the *Journal of Cleaner Production* maps the state of that literature [2]. Faulkner and Badurdeen's Sustainable Value Stream Mapping (Sus-VSM) adds environmental and social metrics, including energy and material use, to the classic map [3]. The EPA's own Lean & Environment Toolkit takes the same route: it covers identifying environmental waste, value stream mapping, kaizen events and 6S (5S plus safety), and it targets waste related to energy, water and materials [4].

In practice, this means adding an energy and carbon row to the tools you already run: the value stream map, the daily board, the kaizen charter and the KPI tree described in the [manufacturing KPIs guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/manufacturing-kpis/).

## Why it matters in 2026: cost, customers and the rules

**Cost.** Energy is a controllable cost line. The DOE's Industrial Training and Assessment Centers (ITAC) report average recommended savings of $144,582 per year per assessment across 23,240 assessments in their database [5]. That is an average of recommendations, not of realized savings, but it shows the order of magnitude a no-cost assessment usually finds.

**Customers.** Large customers that report their own Scope 3 emissions ask suppliers for product-level or plant-level data. The data request often arrives before any law applies to the supplier itself.

**Rules.** The legal picture changed a lot between 2024 and late 2026. Here is the status as of November 20, 2026:

| Rule | Who is covered | Status (November 20, 2026) | What a plant should do |
|---|---|---|---|
| SEC climate disclosure rules (adopted March 2024) | SEC registrants | Stayed since April 4, 2024; the SEC voted to end its defense on March 27, 2025; the Eighth Circuit held the case in abeyance on September 12, 2025; on May 29, 2026 the SEC proposed to rescind the rules in their entirety [6] | No action for the rule itself. Public parents may still report voluntarily or under other regimes |
| California SB 253 (Climate Corporate Data Accountability Act) | US entities doing business in California with annual revenue above $1 billion | CARB's initial regulations were approved on October 12, 2026; the first Scope 1 and 2 report was due November 10, 2026 [7]. On November 9, 2026, the Ninth Circuit let Scope 1 and 2 reporting proceed and found Scope 3 likely unconstitutional in a preliminary-injunction ruling [8] | If your group is in scope: Scope 1 and 2 data by plant, documented methods. Scope 3 timing depends on the district court |
| California SB 261 (climate-related financial risk) | entities with revenue above $500 million doing business in California | Enforcement suspended; the Ninth Circuit found it likely violates the First Amendment (preliminary ruling) [8] | Watch the litigation; no plant-level action today |
| EU CSRD after the Omnibus I directive | EU companies with more than 1,000 employees and more than EUR 450 million net turnover; non-EU groups with more than EUR 450 million EU turnover and an EU subsidiary or branch above EUR 200 million | Omnibus I published in the EU Official Journal on February 26, 2026, in force March 18, 2026; member states transpose by March 19, 2027. Requests to value-chain companies with fewer than 1,000 employees are limited [9] | US groups with large EU sales check the thresholds. Smaller suppliers can expect fewer, but not zero, data requests |
| EU CBAM | EU importers of cement, iron and steel, aluminum, fertilizers, electricity and hydrogen | Definitive regime since January 1, 2026; importers of 50 metric tons or less per year are exempt (not for electricity and hydrogen); certificates on sale from February 2027; first annual declaration for 2026 imports due September 30, 2027 [10] | US exporters of covered goods: expect your EU importer to ask for installation-level emissions data |

Two lessons for operations leaders. First, the federal rule is not the driver anymore; California, the EU and customer contracts are. Second, every one of these regimes starts with Scope 1 and 2 data that a plant can produce from utility bills and fuel records. That work is useful whatever happens in court.

This table is a status snapshot, not legal advice. Have counsel confirm scope for your legal entities.

## Scope 1, 2 and 3 translated to a plant

The GHG Protocol defines Scope 1 as emissions from operations owned or controlled by the reporting company, Scope 2 as emissions from the generation of purchased or acquired electricity, steam, heating or cooling consumed by the company, and Scope 3 as all other indirect emissions in the value chain, split into 15 categories [11].

| Scope | Plant sources | Data you already have | Typical data owner |
|---|---|---|---|
| Scope 1 | natural gas for boilers, ovens, furnaces; propane for forklifts; diesel for generators and fire pumps; refrigerant leaks; process emissions | gas bills, fuel purchase records, refrigerant service logs | facilities / maintenance |
| Scope 2 | purchased electricity, purchased steam or chilled water | utility bills, interval meter data | facilities / finance |
| Scope 3 | purchased materials and parts, inbound and outbound freight, waste sent off site, business travel, employee commuting, use and end of life of sold products | purchase volumes by material, freight invoices, waste manifests | purchasing / logistics / EHS |

For Scope 2, the GHG Protocol's Scope 2 Guidance requires "dual reporting" where contractual instruments exist: a location-based figure using average grid emission factors for the location, and a market-based figure using emission factors from the contracts you buy electricity under, such as certificates or direct contracts [12]. A plant that buys renewable energy certificates will show two different Scope 2 numbers. Both are correct; they answer different questions.

For a single plant, Scope 3 is usually the largest and least precise piece. A pragmatic order: purchased materials by weight (steel, aluminum, resin, packaging) first, freight second, waste third. Use supplier-specific data where suppliers can provide it and average factors where they cannot, and label which is which.

## Worked example 1: energy per unit and the baseload trap

*Illustrative example with fictional values.* A metal parts plant tracks electricity per unit. Two months:

| Month | Good units | Electricity (MWh) | kWh per unit |
|---|---|---|---|
| March | 180,000 | 1,180 | 6.56 |
| April | 220,000 | 1,320 | 6.00 |

April looks 8.5% better. Did the plant improve? Separate fixed and variable energy:

- Variable energy per unit = (1,320 − 1,180) MWh ÷ (220,000 − 180,000) units = 140 MWh ÷ 40,000 = **3.5 kWh per unit**
- Fixed baseload = 1,180 MWh − (180,000 × 3.5 kWh) = 1,180 − 630 = **550 MWh per month**

So 47% of March's electricity (550 ÷ 1,180) did not depend on output: compressors holding pressure, HVAC, lighting, idle machines, furnaces kept hot. The "improvement" in April is mostly volume spreading the baseload. Nothing changed on the floor.

With 12 or more months of data, run a linear regression of energy against output (and heating or cooling degree days if weather matters) instead of a two-point estimate. Then track each month's actual against the expected value from the model:

- Expected May energy at 200,000 units = 550 + 200,000 × 0.0035 = **1,250 MWh**
- Actual May energy = 1,205 MWh → **45 MWh (3.6%) real saving**

This is the logic behind an energy baseline and energy performance indicators (EnPIs) in an ISO 50001-style energy management system [13]. A cumulative sum (CUSUM) chart of "actual minus expected" shows when a change took hold and whether it holds, once the regression model itself is stable.

**What to do with the 550 MWh baseload:** walk the plant on a Sunday with production off and read the meters. Every kW still running is either necessary (safety systems, freezers, servers) or waste. That walk often finds the cheapest savings of the year.

## Worked example 2: CO2e per unit for Scope 1 and 2

*Illustrative example, continued.* In May, the plant used 1,250 MWh of electricity and 3,000 MMBtu of natural gas for 200,000 good units.

**Scope 1 (natural gas).** EPA's 2025 GHG Emission Factors Hub lists 53.06 kg CO2 per MMBtu for natural gas, plus 1.0 g CH4 and 0.10 g N2O per MMBtu [14].

- 3,000 MMBtu × 53.06 kg = 159,180 kg = **159.2 metric tons CO2**
- CH4 and N2O: 3 kg CH4 and 0.3 kg N2O. Even after multiplying by their global warming potentials, they add only a fraction of a percent here. Include them in a formal inventory; ignore them for a first estimate.

**Scope 2 (electricity, location-based).** EPA's eGRID2023 (revision 2, June 2025) gives a US average total output emission rate of 767.2 lb CO2 per MWh [15].

- 1,250 MWh × 767.2 lb = 959,000 lb ÷ 2,204.6 lb per metric ton = **435.0 metric tons CO2**

For a real inventory, use the eGRID subregion factor for the plant's location, not the national average. Regional factors differ widely. For the market-based figure, use your supplier's or contract's factor [12].

**Per unit.**

- Total Scope 1 + 2 = 159.2 + 435.0 = 594.2 metric tons CO2
- Per good unit = 594,200 kg ÷ 200,000 = **2.97 kg CO2 per unit**

To convert electricity to the same energy unit as gas for a total energy intensity, use 3,412 Btu per kWh: 1,250 MWh = 4,265 MMBtu. Total site energy = 7,265 MMBtu, or **36.3 kBtu per unit**.

Keep the electricity and gas lines separate on the board anyway. They have different emission factors, different prices and different owners.

## Worked example 3: carbon along the value stream

Plant totals tell you how much energy you use; a value stream view tells you where. EPA's Lean & Environment training material uses the value stream map as the place to add environmental data [16], and Sus-VSM adds energy and material rows under each process box [3].

*Illustrative example with fictional values.* A bracket goes through five steps. Energy is measured per part processed at each step (including idle running during the shift, divided by parts processed), for example with a submeter or a clamp-on power logger for one week per machine. First-pass yield is the share of parts that pass to the next step; scrapped parts are lost. Reworked parts are ignored here for simplicity; in reality they add energy too.

| Step | Electricity per part processed (kWh) | Gas per part processed (kBtu) | First-pass yield |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Saw cutting | 0.40 | 0 | 99.0% |
| 2 CNC machining | 2.10 | 0 | 97.0% |
| 3 Heat treat (gas furnace) | 0.60 | 25 | 98.0% |
| 4 Powder coat (gas cure oven) | 0.80 | 12 | 92.0% |
| 5 Assembly and test | 0.50 | 0 | 99.5% |

**Step 1: parts that must enter each step per good unit shipped.** Work backward from the end. Input to a step = required output ÷ yield.

- Step 5: 1 ÷ 0.995 = 1.005
- Step 4: 1.005 ÷ 0.92 = 1.092
- Step 3: 1.092 ÷ 0.98 = 1.115
- Step 2: 1.115 ÷ 0.97 = 1.149
- Step 1: 1.149 ÷ 0.99 = 1.161

**Step 2: CO2 per good unit by process.** Electricity at 0.348 kg CO2 per kWh (767.2 lb/MWh [15]); gas at 53.06 kg CO2 per MMBtu, which is 0.05306 kg per kBtu [14].

| Step | Parts in per good unit | kWh per good unit | Gas kBtu per good unit | kg CO2 per good unit | Share |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Saw | 1.161 | 0.46 | 0 | 0.16 | 4% |
| 2 CNC | 1.149 | 2.41 | 0 | 0.84 | 22% |
| 3 Heat treat | 1.115 | 0.67 | 27.9 | 1.71 | 44% |
| 4 Powder coat | 1.092 | 0.87 | 13.1 | 1.00 | 26% |
| 5 Assembly | 1.005 | 0.50 | 0 | 0.17 | 4% |
| **Total** | | **4.92** | **41.0** | **3.89** | 100% |

**What the map shows:**

1. **The hot spot is heat treat** (44%), driven by gas. Furnace loading, door-open time, idle temperature on weekends and burner tuning are the levers.
2. **Scrap is a carbon multiplier.** With 100% yield everywhere, the bracket would cause 3.49 kg CO2. Yield losses add 0.39 kg, or 11%.
3. **Quality is a carbon project.** If the coating yield rises from 92% to 98%, the total drops from 3.89 to 3.66 kg CO2 per good unit, about 6%, because every scrapped coated part carried the energy of cutting, machining and heat treat. A Six Sigma or [poka-yoke](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/lean-manufacturing/poka-yoke/) project at the coating line has an energy payback nobody put in its charter.
4. **Overproduction has the same effect.** Parts made too early still consume the full energy chain, and some of them end up obsolete.

Add these two rows (kWh and kg CO2e per good unit) under the timeline of your current-state map. The [value stream mapping guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/value-stream-mapping/) explains the base map; the energy rows are an extension of the same data walk.

## Where plants lose energy: lean wastes with an energy lens

The table maps the classic wastes to their energy and material effects. It is an editorial mapping for practitioners, not a taxonomy from a standard.

| Lean waste | Energy and environmental effect | Plant example | First countermeasure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Waiting | machines and ovens idle at full temperature or pressure | cure oven held at 400°F during a 2-hour material shortage | idle and standby modes in standard work; shutdown checklist |
| Defects | energy and material of all upstream steps lost | scrapped coated parts (example above) | root cause analysis, poka-yoke |
| Overproduction | energy spent on parts nobody needs yet | batch built to "keep the press busy" | pull system and kanban |
| Overprocessing | tighter tolerances, longer cycles, extra rinses than required | heat treat cycle 20% longer than spec | validate cycle against specification |
| Inventory | heated and lit warehouse space, expiring material, rework | climate-controlled storage of safety stock nobody uses | right-size stock |
| Motion and transport | forklift fuel and battery energy | propane forklifts on long routes | layout, milk runs |
| Unused talent | operators see leaks and idle machines every day | no channel for energy ideas | energy item on the daily board and in kaizen |

**Compressed air deserves its own line.** The Compressed Air Challenge reports that leaks can waste 20 to 30% of a compressor's output and that proactive leak detection and repair can bring leaks below 10% of output [17].

*Illustrative leak calculation (fictional values):* on a Sunday walk with all production off, the compressor still draws 45 kW. That load is leaks plus any legitimate demand (assume none here).

- Leak energy per year = 45 kW × 8,760 hours = 394,200 kWh (leaks run whenever the system is pressurized, also during production)
- Cost at an assumed $0.09 per kWh = **$35,478 per year**
- CO2 at 0.348 kg per kWh = **137 metric tons per year**
- A leak program that cuts the leak load by two thirds saves about $23,650 and 91 metric tons per year. With $15,000 (about $5,000 capex for an ultrasonic detector, the rest opex for tags, labor and fittings), simple payback is about 7.6 months.

Meter by asset, not by person: energy data should point at machines and routines, not at individual operators. Leak repair needs lockout/tagout and a safe depressurization procedure. Put it into the maintenance plan with the same rigor as any other job, and let operators tag leaks during autonomous maintenance as described in the planned [TPM and OEE guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/tpm-oee/).

## Standards and free US programs

| Program or standard | What it is | Cost and effort | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| ISO 50001 | voluntary international standard for energy management systems; third-party certification available [13][20] | certification audits; internal effort for baseline, EnPIs, targets, reviews | plants that want a certified system or have customers asking for it |
| DOE 50001 Ready | DOE recognition for an energy management system aligned with ISO 50001: 25 tasks in the online 50001 Ready Navigator, then self-attestation; no third-party audit, and DOE states it is not a certification program [13] | free; internal effort | plants that want ISO 50001 structure without certification cost |
| DOE Better Plants | voluntary partnership; partners commit to cut energy intensity by 25% over 10 years across their US operations and can add water, waste and carbon goals; DOE reports over 315 industrial organizations and cumulative savings of $14.1 billion and 2.8 quads of energy [18] | free; annual reporting | multi-plant companies with a corporate commitment |
| DOE Industrial Training and Assessment Centers (ITAC) | no-cost assessments by university teams; eligibility: US manufacturer, annual energy bills of $100,000 to $3.5 million, revenue under $250 million, within 150 miles of a center [5] | free; one plant visit plus report | small and mid-sized plants |
| EPA Lean & Environment Toolkit | guidance for adding environmental waste to VSM, kaizen and 6S [4][16] | free | lean teams that want an environmental lens |
| NIST MEP centers | the national Manufacturing Extension Partnership network; local centers offer improvement services, often including energy and lean projects [19] | fee-based, often subsidized | small manufacturers that need hands-on help |

A sensible path for a single plant: ITAC assessment (if eligible) → 50001 Ready tasks → ISO 50001 certification only if customers or corporate policy require it.

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Why it hurts | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Reporting kWh per unit without volume normalization | volume swings look like improvements or failures | regression baseline, actual vs. expected |
| Using the national grid factor for every plant | Scope 2 off by a wide margin for low- or high-carbon regions | eGRID subregion factor; market-based figure from contracts |
| Treating sustainability as an EHS-only topic | energy is lost in operations: idle time, scrap, changeovers | put energy on the daily board and in the KPI tree |

| No owner for the baseload | weekend loads stay on for years | named owner, shutdown checklist, monthly baseload review |
| Ignoring the workforce | operators know where the waste is | energy ideas in suggestion systems, recognition |

## FAQ: sustainable manufacturing

### What is sustainable manufacturing?

The EPA defines it as creating manufactured products through economically sound processes that minimize negative environmental impacts while conserving energy and natural resources [1]. In practice, plants track energy per good unit, greenhouse gas emissions by scope and material losses.

### What are examples of sustainable manufacturing?

Fixing compressed air leaks, turning off ovens and furnaces during long stops, raising first-pass yield to cut scrap, heat recovery from compressors or ovens, efficient motors and drives, using recycled material, and redesigning packaging. The best examples combine a cost saving with a lower footprint.

### What are the challenges of sustainable manufacturing?

In plants, the hard part is rarely the idea; it is measurement and ownership. Typical obstacles are energy data without volume normalization, wrong emission factors, Scope 3 data that take months to collect, baseloads without an owner, and sustainability handled as an EHS topic while the energy is lost in operations. The common mistakes table gives a fix for each. Low-cost first projects, such as compressed air leak repair, help where capital is tight.

### What are Scope 1, 2 and 3 emissions in manufacturing?

Scope 1 covers fuels burned on site and other direct emissions, such as natural gas in boilers and furnaces. Scope 2 covers purchased electricity, steam, heat and cooling. Scope 3 covers the rest of the value chain, such as purchased materials, freight and the use of sold products, in 15 categories [11].

### How do you calculate energy per unit?

Divide energy use by good units produced in the same period. Because much plant energy is fixed baseload, compare actual energy with the expected value from a regression of energy against output. In the example above, the baseload was 550 MWh per month and the variable part 3.5 kWh per unit.

### Is lean manufacturing sustainable?

Largely, because the same wastes (defects, overproduction, waiting) also consume energy and material; the lean and green literature reviewed in [2] builds on this overlap. Lean alone can miss energy, though, so add energy and carbon rows to your value stream maps and boards.

### What are the three pillars of sustainable manufacturing?

Environmental, economic and social. The EPA definition covers the first two directly (economically sound processes with lower environmental impact) [1]. The social pillar covers worker safety, training and the community around the plant.

### Does the SEC climate disclosure rule apply to my company?

As of November 2026, no. The rules have been stayed since April 2024, the SEC stopped defending them in March 2025 and proposed to rescind them on May 29, 2026 [6]. California SB 253 and EU rules may still apply to your group.

### Who has to report under California SB 253?

US entities doing business in California with annual revenue above $1 billion. Scope 1 and 2 reports were first due November 10, 2026 [7]. A Ninth Circuit preliminary ruling of November 9, 2026 left Scope 1 and 2 in place and found Scope 3 likely unconstitutional [8].

### Do US manufacturers have to comply with CSRD?

Only large groups. After the Omnibus I directive, non-EU groups are in scope if they have more than EUR 450 million EU turnover and an EU subsidiary or branch above EUR 200 million [9]. Smaller US suppliers mainly see data requests from EU customers.

### Does CBAM affect US exporters?

Indirectly. The obligations sit with EU importers of cement, iron and steel, aluminum, fertilizers, electricity and hydrogen above 50 metric tons per year [10]. US producers of these goods should prepare installation-level emissions data for their EU customers.

### Is ISO 50001 certification worth it?

It depends on who asks for it. If customers or corporate policy require certification, yes. If not, DOE's free 50001 Ready program gives you the same management structure without third-party audits [13].

## Conclusion

Sustainable manufacturing is operational excellence applied to energy, carbon and materials. Start with numbers you already have: two years of utility bills and output give you a baseline, a baseload figure and Scope 1 and 2 emissions per unit. Put energy rows on your value stream map and you will usually find that the biggest carbon lever is a quality or flow problem, not a new machine.

US rules shifted from federal to state and customer-driven in 2025 and 2026, and the EU narrowed CSRD to large companies. The plant-level work stays the same: measure per good unit, fix the baseload, cut scrap, and make energy part of the daily routine with the people who run the equipment.
