# OT Cybersecurity and ISA/IEC 62443: A Practical Guide for Manufacturers

*Updated: November 2026 · Reading time: about 50 minutes (quick start: short answer, "Key takeaways" box, the worked zone example and the roadmap)*

> **Short answer:** OT cybersecurity (operational technology security) protects the systems that run physical production, such as PLCs, SCADA, HMIs, robots, drives and safety systems, so that a plant stays safe, available and producing good parts. The international reference for it is the ISA/IEC 62443 series: it assigns duties to asset owners, integrators and product suppliers, groups assets into zones connected by conduits, sets a target security level (SL 1 to 4) per zone, and checks every zone against seven foundational requirements [1][2]. In the US you usually combine it with the NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 and NIST SP 800-82 [9][11].

> **Key takeaways**
> - In OT, availability and safety come first. A firewall rule or a patch that stops a line can do more damage than the attack it was meant to prevent.
> - ISA/IEC 62443 is a series of standards, not a single document. Parts 2-1 (security program), 3-2 (risk assessment and zones), 3-3 (system requirements), 4-1 and 4-2 (supplier requirements) do most of the work in a plant [1].
> - The core method is simple to state: list your assets, group them into zones, define the conduits between zones, set a target security level per zone, and close the gap between target and achieved level [2].
> - Most production stoppages in the documented cases started in office IT, not in the controllers. Colonial Pipeline, Norsk Hydro and Clorox all shut down or slowed operations after attacks on their IT environments [17][20][35].
> - A practical first year for a mid-size plant: asset inventory, a zone and conduit model, a segmented industrial DMZ, controlled remote access, offline backups and a rehearsed OT incident playbook. Detection and supplier requirements follow in year two.
> - NIST SP 800-82 Rev. 3 (2023) is the current US OT security guide; NIST published the initial public draft of Rev. 4 on September 21, 2026, restructured around CSF 2.0, with comments open until November 30, 2026 [9][10].
> - Certification exists for products (ISASecure CSA, SSA, SDLA) and, since June 2025, for operating sites (ISASecure ACSSA, based on 62443-2-1, 2-4, 3-2 and 3-3) [7][8].
> - Budget the program like maintenance: a stable yearly line with clear KPIs, not a one-time project.

## What is OT cybersecurity?

Operational technology (OT) is the hardware and software that monitors and controls physical processes. NIST describes OT as "a broad range of programmable systems and devices that interact with the physical environment" and names industrial control systems, building automation, transportation systems, physical access control and environmental monitoring as examples [9]. In a factory, that means the programmable logic controllers (PLCs) on a stamping line, the distributed control system (DCS) of a paint shop, the supervisory control and data acquisition (SCADA) server for utilities, the human-machine interfaces (HMIs) at the stations, robot controllers, drives, vision systems, the engineering workstations that program all of it, and the safety instrumented systems (SIS) that bring the process to a safe state.

OT cybersecurity is the set of policies, technical controls and routines that keep those systems from being disrupted, manipulated or used as a path into the rest of the company. The goal is different from classic IT security. In the office, the first question is usually "is the data confidential?" On the plant floor, the first questions are "can anyone get hurt?" and "will the line keep running and produce good parts?"

### OT, ICS, IACS, SCADA: sorting out the terms

The field uses several overlapping terms. They matter because the standards use them precisely.

| Term | Meaning | Where you meet it |
|---|---|---|
| OT (operational technology) | Umbrella term for systems that interact with the physical world | NIST SP 800-82 Rev. 3 uses it in its title [9] |
| ICS (industrial control system) | Control systems in industry: PLC, DCS, SCADA and their networks | Older NIST and CISA documents, MITRE ATT&CK for ICS [15] |
| IACS (industrial automation and control system) | The term used by ISA/IEC 62443: people, hardware, software and policies involved in operating an industrial process | All 62443 parts [1][2] |
| SCADA | Supervisory control over distributed sites (pipelines, utilities, water) | Utilities, plant-wide energy systems |
| DCS | Distributed control system, typical in continuous process plants | Chemicals, food, pharma, paint shops |
| PLC | Programmable logic controller, the workhorse of discrete manufacturing | Machines, conveyors, assembly lines |
| SIS | Safety instrumented system; executes safety instrumented functions | Process safety, presses, burners |
| IIoT | Industrial Internet of Things: sensors and devices that send data to edge or cloud platforms | Predictive maintenance, energy monitoring; IEC PAS 62443-1-6 [5] |

In practice, "OT security", "OT cybersecurity", "ICS security" and "industrial cybersecurity" are used as synonyms. This guide uses "OT security" for the discipline and "IACS" when it refers to what 62443 requires.

### Why manufacturing is in scope at all

Many plant managers still assume that cyberattacks are an IT problem and that their machines are "air-gapped". In most plants that has not been true for years. MES, historians, ERP interfaces, remote support for machine builders, cloud dashboards and IIoT sensors connect the plant floor with the enterprise and the internet. CISA lists critical manufacturing (primary metals, machinery, electrical equipment and transportation equipment) as a US critical infrastructure sector, with the Department of Homeland Security as its sector risk management agency [32]; it is commonly counted as one of 16 sectors [unverified: the cited CISA page does not state the count].

The cost of a stopped plant is easy to understand for anyone who has run one. Every hour of downtime costs contribution margin, overtime to catch up, scrap from interrupted batches, expediting fees and, at worst, customer penalties. The cases in section 3 show that attackers know this. Ransomware groups do not need to understand a PLC to stop production; they only need to encrypt the systems the plant cannot run without.

For deeper context on the data side of the plant, see the guides on [MES and ISA-95](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/mes-isa-95/) (planned) and [Azure for manufacturing](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/manufacturing-it-azure/) (planned), which describe how plant data reaches enterprise and cloud systems. Every one of those connections is a conduit in the sense of 62443.

## IT vs. OT security: why the office playbook fails on the plant floor

IT and OT security use many of the same tools: firewalls, accounts, logging, backups. But the systems, the priorities and the consequences differ enough that copying the office playbook to the plant floor causes outages. The ISAGCA quick start guide puts the plant priority into one concept: the **essential functions** that must be maintained "to maintain health, safety, the environment, and availability of the Equipment Under Control". It lists the safety instrumented function, the control function and the operator's ability to view and manipulate the equipment; losing them means loss of protection, loss of control or loss of view [2].

| Aspect | Typical office IT | Typical plant OT |
|---|---|---|
| Top priority | Confidentiality and integrity of data | Safety, availability and integrity of the process; confidentiality matters, but usually comes later |
| Consequence of failure | Data loss, fines, reputational damage | Injuries, environmental release, equipment damage, scrap, lost output |
| Lifetime of systems | 3 to 5 years for laptops and servers | 15 to 25 years for controllers and machines is common in practice [practitioner range, unverified as an industry statistic] |
| Change windows | Nightly or weekly | Planned shutdowns, sometimes once or twice a year |
| Patching | Automated, monthly | Only after vendor approval and testing; some devices cannot be patched at all |
| Reboots | Acceptable | A reboot can stop a line or ruin a batch |
| Protocols | Authenticated, encrypted (HTTPS, Kerberos) | Many legacy industrial protocols were designed without authentication or encryption |
| Antivirus and agents | Standard on every endpoint | Often not supported by the vendor of the HMI or controller; allowlisting is safer |
| Performance | Delays of seconds are annoying | Delays of milliseconds can trip a process |
| Ownership | IT department | Maintenance, controls engineering, production, often the machine builder |
| Who changes things | Administrators | Technicians, integrators, vendors, sometimes operators |

### What this means in practice

Three rules follow from the table.

1. **No active scanning of controllers without a test.** A vulnerability scanner that is harmless on a file server can crash an older PLC or HMI. Asset discovery in OT starts passive (listening to network traffic) or uses the vendor's engineering tools.
2. **Security changes need production's sign-off.** Every firewall rule, account change or patch in a zone with essential functions follows management of change (MOC), just like a change to a recipe or a safety interlock.
3. **The compensating control is the normal case.** When a Windows 7 HMI cannot be replaced until the next line rebuild, you protect it by putting it into a small zone, restricting its conduits, removing USB access and watching its traffic. Accepting a compensating control in that situation is exactly what the 62443 method intends.

### Where IT and OT must work together

The reverse mistake is to declare OT security a plant matter that IT should stay out of. The incidents in the next section show that attackers often enter through identity systems, email or help desks, which IT runs. An effective program has one risk owner at site level, a joint team of IT security and controls engineering, and clearly assigned responsibilities for each zone. The roles table in section 18 shows one way to split them.

### Patterns behind the cases

Read side by side, the cases show four patterns that shape the rest of this guide.

- **Entry through IT, impact on OT.** In none of the five cases do the cited sources describe a direct attack on controllers. Operations stopped because shared networks, shared identities or dependent business systems failed. That is why the zone boundary between enterprise and plant (the industrial DMZ) and identity hygiene sit at the top of the roadmap.
- **Operator-initiated shutdowns.** Clorox took systems offline and halted manufacturing after detecting the intrusion, then handled orders manually [20]; Colonial's pipeline was shut down after the ransomware attack [35]. A plant that knows which business systems it depends on, and can run in a degraded mode, has more choices.
- **Recovery takes weeks.** Even well-prepared companies needed weeks to restore normal output. Backups, rebuilt engineering workstations and verified controller logic decide how long the gap is.
- **Third parties are part of the attack surface.** Software updates (NotPetya), outsourced help desks (Clorox) and integrators (see the CISA/FBI fact sheet in section 12) all appear in the record [14][18][20].

### Attacks aimed at controllers

Attacks that manipulate the industrial process itself are rarer, but they exist and they are the reason security levels 3 and 4 exist in 62443. MITRE maintains a public knowledge base of adversary behavior against industrial control systems, ATT&CK for ICS. Its matrix (content version 20.1) groups techniques into 12 tactics, from Initial Access through Inhibit Response Function and Impair Process Control to Impact [15]. Two tactics are specific to OT: **Inhibit Response Function** (preventing safety and protection functions from reacting) and **Impair Process Control** (manipulating the control of the process) [15].

For defenders, ATT&CK for ICS is useful in three ways:

1. As a checklist for detection: which techniques could your monitoring see?
2. As a shared vocabulary between IT security analysts and controls engineers.
3. As input to the threat scenarios in the 62443-3-2 risk assessment (section 8).

This guide stays on the defensive side. It does not describe how to carry out any technique.

## The framework landscape: 62443, NIST, CISA, CMMC, NIS2 and the CRA

US manufacturers face a crowded field of frameworks. They do not compete as much as it seems; they answer different questions.

### How the pieces fit together

A workable combination for a US manufacturer looks like this:

- **CSF 2.0 for governance and the board.** It gives management, auditors, insurers and customers a common language. The new Govern function covers roles, risk appetite and supplier risk [11].
- **62443 for the plant design and the supplier chain.** It is the only framework in the table that assigns concrete duties to asset owners, integrators and product suppliers and defines zones, conduits and security levels [1][2].
- **SP 800-82 as the bridge.** It translates OT specifics into the NIST control world. The Rev. 4 draft is restructured around CSF 2.0 and adds guidance on asset management, network monitoring and zero trust principles for system management functions [10].
- **CPGs as a sanity check.** They are short and measurable; useful for a quick baseline before you start the full 62443 program [13].
- **CMMC, SEC, CIRCIA, NIS2 and the CRA** only where they apply to you (section 19).

The NIST Manufacturing Extension Partnership (MEP) helps small and mid-sized manufacturers with CSF-based self-assessments, gap analyses and incident response plans [30]. For many plants with fewer than 500 employees, that is the most affordable way to get started.

## ISA/IEC 62443 explained: structure, roles and status

### Where the standard comes from

The International Society of Automation (ISA) set up its ISA99 committee in 2002 to write cybersecurity standards for automation and control systems. Around 2010 the committee began working closely with the International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC), and the documents were renumbered as ISA/IEC 62443 [3]. The ISA documents are published as ANSI/ISA-62443-x-y; the IEC publishes the same content as IEC 62443-x-y. ISA states that the series is endorsed by the United Nations, and the IEC designated it a horizontal standard in 2021, meaning it applies across sectors rather than to one industry [1].

For a US reader, the practical point is that you can buy the ANSI/ISA editions from ISA and the IEC editions from the IEC webstore, and they are technically aligned. Dates and edition numbers sometimes differ between the two publishers, so always cite the exact edition in contracts.

### The four groups of documents

The series is organized in four groups [1][2]:

1. **General (1-x):** terminology, concepts, models, metrics.
2. **Policies and procedures (2-x):** what the asset owner and service providers must do as an organization.
3. **System (3-x):** risk assessment, zones and conduits, and technical requirements for the whole automation solution.
4. **Component (4-x):** secure development processes and technical requirements for individual products.

### The roles: who has to do what

62443 assigns responsibilities to roles, not to departments. The ISAGCA quick start guide defines them as follows [2]:

| Role | Definition (ISAGCA) | Typical company | Main 62443 parts |
|---|---|---|---|
| Asset owner | "The organization that is accountable and responsible for the IACS" | The manufacturer that runs the plant | 2-1, 2-2, 2-3, 3-2 |
| Integration service provider | "The organization that provides integration activities for an Automation Solution" | System integrator, machine builder delivering a line, in-house engineering | 2-4, 3-2, 3-3 |
| Maintenance service provider | Provides support activities for an Automation Solution under the asset owner's policies | Service contractor, OEM service, in-house maintenance | 2-4 |
| Product supplier | "The organization that manufactures and supports a hardware and/or software product" | PLC, HMI, network, drive and software vendors | 4-1, 4-2 |

One company can hold several roles. A machine builder is a product supplier for its control cabinet software and an integration service provider when it installs the line. A large manufacturer with its own controls engineering department is asset owner and integrator at the same time. The value of the role model is that the contract and the audit can say exactly which requirements each party owns.

### The two lifecycles

62443 distinguishes the **product development lifecycle** of the supplier from the **automation solution lifecycle** at the plant, which has an integration phase and an operation and maintenance phase [2]. A secure PLC (4-2) does not make a secure line; the integrator has to configure it correctly (3-3), and the asset owner has to operate it securely for twenty years (2-1). Most plant problems arise in the third step.

## Foundational requirements, security levels and maturity levels

### The seven foundational requirements

All technical requirements in 3-3 (system) and 4-2 (component) are grouped under seven foundational requirements (FRs) [2]. Learn them; they are the backbone of every gap analysis.

| FR | Name | What it asks in plant language | Typical plant measures |
|---|---|---|---|
| FR 1 | Identification and authentication control (IAC) | Do we know who or what is connecting? | Personal accounts instead of shared "operator" logins, MFA for remote access, device certificates for OPC UA |
| FR 2 | Use control (UC) | Can each person only do what their role requires? | Role-based rights on HMIs and engineering tools, separate admin accounts, audit of who changed logic |
| FR 3 | System integrity (SI) | Can we detect or prevent unauthorized changes to software, logic and data? | Application allowlisting, signed firmware, controller key switch in RUN, logic change detection |
| FR 4 | Data confidentiality (DC) | Is sensitive information protected in use, transit and storage? | Encrypted remote sessions, protection of recipes and drawings, CUI handling for defense work |
| FR 5 | Restricted data flow (RDF) | Is traffic limited to what is necessary between zones? | Segmentation, firewalls at conduits, industrial DMZ, one-way gateways where justified |
| FR 6 | Timely response to events (TRE) | Do we notice incidents and react in time? | Log collection, OT network monitoring, alarm handling, incident playbook |
| FR 7 | Resource availability (RA) | Do the essential functions keep running under stress or attack? | Redundancy, backups, tested restores, protection against network floods, manual fallback |

### Security levels (SL 1 to 4)

A security level describes how strong an attacker a zone or component can withstand. 62443-3-3 defines four levels [2]:

| SL | Protection against | Example threat in a plant |
|---|---|---|
| SL 0 | No special protection needed | Rarely justified; not a design target |
| SL 1 | Casual or coincidental violation | A technician plugs a laptop into the wrong switch port |
| SL 2 | Intentional violation using simple means, low resources, generic skills and low motivation | Commodity ransomware, an opportunistic attacker with standard tools |
| SL 3 | Intentional violation using sophisticated means, moderate resources, IACS-specific skills and moderate motivation | A criminal group that studies your control system before acting |
| SL 4 | Intentional violation using sophisticated means, extended resources, IACS-specific skills and high motivation | A well-funded, state-backed team targeting a specific process |

Each security level is not one number for the whole plant but a vector across the seven FRs. A zone might need SL 3 for FR 3 (integrity, because manipulated logic could hurt someone) but only SL 1 for FR 4 (confidentiality, because the data is not sensitive). The secondary overview notes that SLs are assessed per technical requirement, not as a general rating of a product [3].

There are also three types of security level [2]:

- **SL-T (target):** the desired level for a zone or conduit, set by the risk assessment.
- **SL-C (capability):** what a system (3-3) or component (4-2) can provide when properly integrated and configured.
- **SL-A (achieved):** the actual level of the installed solution.

The whole program can be described in one sentence: choose components with SL-C at least equal to SL-T, configure them so that SL-A reaches SL-T, and use compensating measures where it does not.

**How most mid-size manufacturers set targets.** SL 2 is the common target for production zones in discrete manufacturing, with SL 3 for zones whose compromise could cause injuries, large environmental releases or long outages, such as safety systems and critical process control. This is practitioner experience, not a rule of the standard [unverified as a statistic]. The decision belongs in your risk assessment, documented and approved by the asset owner.

### Maturity levels

Security levels measure technical strength. Maturity levels measure how well processes are run. The 62443 maturity model is based on CMMI and used in parts 2-1, 2-2, 2-4 and 4-1 [2]. The secondary overview describes ML 1 (initial, ad hoc), ML 2 (managed, documented and repeatable), ML 3 (defined, practiced across the organization with evidence) and ML 4 (improving, measured and continuously improved) [3].

A practical reading: an integrator whose 2-4 processes are at ML 1 does good work when the right engineer is on site and poor work otherwise. Ask for ML 2 or higher in contracts, which means written procedures, trained staff and evidence.

### The design principles behind the requirements

The quick start guide names four design principles that run through the series: secure by design, reduce the attack surface (access control, segmentation, least function, least privilege), defense in depth, and protection of essential functions [2]. Defense in depth is defined as "the provision of multiple security protections, especially in layers, with the intent to delay or prevent an attack" [2]. No single firewall is supposed to carry the load.

## Zones and conduits and the Purdue model

### Definitions

- **Zone:** "a grouping of logical or physical assets based upon risk or other criteria" [2].
- **Conduit:** "a logical grouping of communication channels that share common security requirements" [2].

Assets in one zone share the same security requirements; traffic between zones only flows through defined conduits, and each conduit gets its own controls (firewall rules, protocol restrictions, authentication, monitoring). A zone can be a single cell with a robot and its PLC, a whole production line, the utilities system, or the industrial DMZ.

### How to draw zones

Group assets by these criteria, in roughly this order:

1. **Function and consequence:** safety systems in their own zone, separate from basic process control.
2. **Physical or logical location:** line, building, site.
3. **Ownership and lifecycle:** a turnkey line from one machine builder often forms a natural zone.
4. **Technical capability:** legacy systems that cannot be hardened may need their own small zone with tight conduits.
5. **Connectivity needs:** systems that must talk to the enterprise or cloud go near the boundary, not deep inside.

Avoid two extremes. One zone for the whole plant gives you no containment. A hundred zones make rules unmanageable and create a firewall nobody dares to touch. For a mid-size plant, 8 to 20 zones are typical in practice [practitioner range, unverified].

### The Purdue model and its limits

Many plants still describe their architecture with the Purdue Enterprise Reference Architecture, which the ISA-95 standard adopted as a functional hierarchy. Its levels are a useful starting map for zones:

| Level | Content | Example systems | Typical zone |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4-5 | Enterprise: business planning and logistics, corporate IT, internet | ERP, email, Microsoft 365, enterprise identity | Enterprise zone |
| 3.5 | Industrial DMZ (not in the original model; common in practice) | Historian replica, patch server, remote access gateway, file transfer | IDMZ |
| 3 | Site operations: manufacturing operations management | MES, site historian, OT domain controller, backup server | Site operations zone |
| 2 | Area supervisory control | SCADA, HMI, engineering workstations | Line or area zones |
| 1 | Basic control | PLCs, DCS controllers, robot controllers, drives | Cell or line zones |
| 0 | Physical process | Sensors, actuators, motors, valves | Inside the cell zones |
| Safety | Safety instrumented systems | Safety PLC, burner management, press safety | Separate safety zone |

The Purdue model is a starting map, not the zone model itself. Its limits show up in three places:

- **IIoT and cloud.** Wireless sensors that send data straight to a cloud platform skip levels. IEC PAS 62443-1-6 (December 2025) gives guidance on applying the series to IIoT systems [5]; the NIST SP 800-82 Rev. 4 draft also adds coverage of IIoT with cloud convergence [10].
- **Flat lines.** Many machines combine level 1 and level 2 in one cabinet. Zones follow risk, not the drawing.
- **Shared services.** An Active Directory that serves both office and plant connects levels 4 and 3 in a way the drawing does not show. Map the real dependencies, not the intended ones.

For the data architecture side (ISA-95 levels, unified namespace, edge clusters in the industrial DMZ), see the guides on [MES and ISA-95](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/mes-isa-95/) (planned) and [Azure for manufacturing](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/manufacturing-it-azure/) (planned).

## The 62443-3-2 risk assessment, step by step

62443-3-2 describes how to go from "we have a system" to "we know the zones, conduits and target security levels". The quick start guide summarizes the workflow in seven zone and conduit requirements (ZCR) [2]:

| Step | ZCR | Activity | Output |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | ZCR 1 | Identify the system under consideration (SUC) | Boundary, asset list, entry points |
| 2 | ZCR 2 | Perform an initial cybersecurity risk assessment | Worst-case consequences assuming the system is compromised |
| 3 | ZCR 3 | Partition the SUC into zones and conduits | Zone and conduit diagram |
| 4 | ZCR 4 | Decide: does the initial risk exceed tolerable risk? | List of zones that need a detailed assessment |
| 5 | ZCR 5 | Perform a detailed cybersecurity risk assessment | Threats, vulnerabilities, likelihood, consequences, SL-T per zone and conduit |
| 6 | ZCR 6 | Document cybersecurity requirements, assumptions and constraints | Cybersecurity requirements specification (CRS) |
| 7 | ZCR 7 | Asset owner approval | Signed-off CRS |

(The quick start guide labels the documentation step "ZCR 4" in one place; in the sequence it is the sixth step.)

### How to run it in practice

**Step 1: Define the system.** Pick a scope that one team can handle in a few weeks, such as one production building or one line plus its supporting systems. Include the remote access paths and cloud connections; they are part of the system even if the servers are elsewhere.

**Step 2: Initial assessment with the "assume breach" view.** For each candidate zone, ask what is the worst plausible outcome if an attacker gains full control. Use your process hazard analysis (PHA) or HAZOP as the input: the safety team already knows which deviations can hurt people. The initial assessment uses unmitigated risk: no credit for existing security controls.

**Step 3: Zones and conduits.** Draw them using the criteria in the previous section. Every connection that crosses a zone boundary becomes part of a conduit.

**Step 4: Tolerable risk.** Your company needs a risk matrix that says which combinations of likelihood and consequence are tolerable. Many plants already have one for process safety or enterprise risk management; reuse it rather than inventing a new one.

**Step 5: Detailed assessment.** For zones above the tolerable line, list threat scenarios (ATT&CK for ICS helps [15]), existing controls and vulnerabilities, then estimate likelihood and consequence. Derive SL-T per FR.

**Step 6: Cybersecurity requirements specification.** Write down per zone and conduit: SL-T, required measures, assumptions (for example, "the cabinet is locked and the hall has badge access") and constraints ("HMI vendor does not support antivirus"). The CRS is the document you hand to an integrator or a machine builder.

**Step 7: Approval.** The plant manager or the named asset owner signs it. This step matters: it makes residual risk a management decision, not an engineering preference.

### A simple scoring scheme

Use whatever scale your enterprise risk management uses. If you have none, the following 5x5 scheme works for a first pass.

| Score | Likelihood (with current controls) | Consequence (worst plausible, per zone) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Not plausible in the system lifetime | No safety effect, under 4 hours of downtime, under $50,000 |
| 2 | Plausible but unlikely | First aid case, up to 1 shift of downtime, up to $250,000 |
| 3 | Could happen within 10 years | Recordable injury, 1 to 3 days of downtime, up to $1 million |
| 4 | Could happen within 3 years | Serious injury or environmental release, 1 to 2 weeks of downtime, up to $5 million |
| 5 | Expected within a year | Fatality or major release, more than 2 weeks, more than $5 million |

The dollar and time thresholds above are illustrative and must be set by each company to match its own risk appetite.

Risk = likelihood x consequence. A common mapping: 1 to 6 tolerable, 8 to 12 needs measures and approval, 15 to 25 not tolerable. For the SL-T, one practical heuristic is: zones whose worst consequence scores 4 or 5 get SL-T 3 for FR 1 to FR 3 and FR 7; zones with consequence 2 or 3 get SL-T 2; consequence 1 gets SL-T 1. This heuristic is our simplification for a first pass; the standard requires a documented rationale, not a formula.

## Worked example: zones and conduits for a mid-size plant

Abstract definitions only go so far. This section applies 62443-3-2 to a fictional but typical plant. All names and numbers are illustrative.

### The plant

"Midwest Components" makes metal housings and assemblies for agricultural and construction equipment. One site, 420 employees, three shifts, annual revenue about $150 million. Production:

- **Press shop:** four servo presses with transfer automation; one press line has a safety PLC with light curtains and a two-hand control.
- **Welding cell area:** twelve robotic welding cells from two machine builders, each with its own PLC, robot controller and HMI.
- **Paint shop:** powder coating line with a curing oven (natural gas burner with a burner management system), run by a small DCS.
- **Assembly:** two manual assembly lines with torque tools, label printers and a line PLC for the conveyor.
- **Utilities:** compressed air, cooling water, HVAC and energy metering on a SCADA system.
- **Site systems:** MES with a server and 40 clients, a historian, a quality lab with a coordinate measuring machine (CMM), an OT Active Directory, a backup server.
- **Enterprise:** ERP hosted by corporate IT, Microsoft 365, corporate identity.
- **Remote access:** the two welding machine builders and the press manufacturer each currently use their own remote access box with a cellular modem. The paint line integrator connects through a vendor VPN.
- **Cloud:** an energy monitoring service receives meter data over the internet from a gateway in the utilities network.

### Step 1 and 2: initial assessment

The team (plant engineering manager, controls lead, IT security lead, EHS manager and the maintenance supervisor) walks through each area with the question "what is the worst plausible outcome if an attacker controls this?"

| Area | Worst plausible outcome (assume breach) | Consequence score |
|---|---|---|
| Press line safety PLC | Safety function disabled during operation, risk of serious injury | 5 |
| Press control | Damaged dies and presses, weeks of downtime | 4 |
| Paint oven burner management | Unsafe burner conditions, fire or explosion risk | 5 |
| Paint DCS | Ruined batches, several days of downtime | 3 |
| Welding cells | Damaged fixtures, quality escapes, 1 to 3 days per cell | 3 |
| Assembly lines | Wrong torque or labels, quality escape to customers | 3 |
| Utilities SCADA | Loss of compressed air and cooling stops the whole plant | 4 |
| MES and historian | Plant can run in degraded manual mode for a few days | 3 |
| Quality lab | Wrong measurement data, quality escape | 2 |
| Enterprise IT | Business disruption; plant stops if order and shipping data are unavailable | 4 |

### Step 3: the zone model

| Zone | Content | SL-T (FR 1-3, FR 5-7 / FR 4) | Rationale |
|---|---|---|---|
| Z0 Enterprise | Corporate IT, ERP, Microsoft 365 | Managed by corporate IT under CSF 2.0 | Outside the IACS but the main source of threats |
| Z1 Industrial DMZ | Historian replica, patch and antivirus update server, remote access gateway, file transfer server, jump hosts | SL 3 / SL 2 | Only zone with direct contact to Z0 and to external vendors |
| Z2 Site operations | MES server, site historian, OT Active Directory, backup server, OT monitoring sensor management | SL 2 / SL 2 | Consequence 3, but central services for all lines |
| Z3 Press control | Press PLCs, transfer automation, HMIs, press engineering workstation | SL 2, SL 3 for FR 3 / SL 1 | Consequence 4: integrity of logic matters most |
| Z4 Press safety | Safety PLC, light curtains, two-hand controls | SL 3 / SL 1 | Consequence 5; separate zone with minimal conduits |
| Z5 Welding cells (machine builder A) | Six cells | SL 2 / SL 1 | Consequence 3, turnkey from one supplier |
| Z6 Welding cells (machine builder B) | Six cells | SL 2 / SL 1 | Same, different supplier and remote access path |
| Z7 Paint DCS | DCS controllers, operator stations | SL 2 / SL 1 | Consequence 3 |
| Z8 Burner management | Burner management system of the curing oven | SL 3 / SL 1 | Consequence 5; separate safety zone |
| Z9 Assembly | Line PLCs, torque controllers, label printers, HMIs | SL 2 / SL 1 | Consequence 3 |
| Z10 Utilities | SCADA, compressors, chillers, HVAC controls, meters | SL 2, SL 3 for FR 7 / SL 1 | Consequence 4: availability matters most |
| Z11 Quality lab | CMM, lab PCs | SL 2 / SL 2 | Measurement data and customer drawings |
| Z12 IIoT energy gateway | Meter gateway with cloud connection | SL 2 / SL 1 | Direct internet path, isolated from Z10 control |

Twelve zones inside the plant, plus the enterprise. Note what happened to the cellular remote access boxes: they disappear. All vendor access moves to the remote access gateway in Z1.

### The conduits

| Conduit | Between | Allowed traffic | Controls |
|---|---|---|---|
| C1 | Z0 Enterprise <-> Z1 IDMZ | ERP to MES order interface via IDMZ broker; users to historian replica (read only); update downloads | Firewall pair (enterprise side and plant side), no direct traffic from Z0 to Z2 or below, logging |
| C2 | Z1 IDMZ <-> Z2 Site operations | Historian replication (Z2 to Z1), MES interface, patch distribution, jump host sessions | Firewall with explicit allow rules, MFA for jump hosts, session recording |
| C3 | External vendors <-> Z1 IDMZ | Remote access sessions | Remote access gateway with personal accounts, MFA, approval per session, time limits, recording |
| C4 | Z2 <-> Z3, Z5, Z6, Z7, Z9, Z10, Z11 | MES to line PLCs and HMIs (defined industrial protocols), historian data collection, backups, engineering access from jump host | Internal firewall or ACLs per zone; only named protocols and hosts |
| C5 | Z3 Press control <-> Z4 Press safety | Status signals only | Hardwired or dedicated safety network; no routable connection to Z2 |
| C6 | Z7 Paint DCS <-> Z8 Burner management | Status and permissive signals | Hardwired I/O; no engineering access except locally with MOC |
| C7 | Z12 IIoT gateway <-> internet (cloud service) | Outbound encrypted telemetry only | Gateway in its own VLAN, outbound only, no inbound connections |
| C8 | Z10 Utilities -> Z12 | Meter values | One-direction data flow (data diode or unidirectional firewall rule), no path from Z12 back into Z10. Check first that the meter protocol works one-way; request-response protocols need a gateway that polls on the Z10 side |

### Steps 4 to 7: from gaps to a requirements specification

The detailed assessment for Z4 Press safety shows: initial risk 5 (consequence) x 3 (likelihood: the safety PLC is reachable from the press HMI network and from a machine builder's cellular modem) = 15, not tolerable. With the planned measures (separate zone, no routable conduit, cellular modem removed, key switch protected in RUN, logic checksum checked weekly), the team estimates likelihood 1 and residual risk 5, tolerable. The asset owner signs off.

For Z5 and Z6 the gap is mainly FR 1 and FR 2: shared accounts on all twelve HMIs and the robot teach pendants. The CRS for the next cell purchase requires named accounts and an SL-C 2 certified HMI; for the existing cells the compensating measure is a jump host with personal accounts in front of them and a ban on direct laptop connections.

### What the result looks like after one year

| Metric | Before | After 12 months |
|---|---|---|
| Uncontrolled remote access paths | 4 (3 cellular boxes, 1 vendor VPN) | 0; one gateway with MFA and recording |
| Zones | 1 flat plant network, office VLAN routed to it | 12 plant zones plus enterprise, IDMZ in between |
| Assets with known owner and firmware version | Unknown | 96% of the inventoried 640 network-connected assets |
| Controllers with offline backup of current logic | About half (estimate) | 100% of the 58 PLCs and controllers, restore tested on 6 |
| Incident playbook | None | Written, tested in one tabletop exercise with production |

These numbers are illustrative. The point is the shape of the result: fewer paths, known assets, recoverable logic.

## OT asset inventory

You cannot protect what you do not know. The asset inventory is step zero of 62443-3-2 (identify the system under consideration) and of every NIST profile. The NIST SP 800-82 Rev. 4 draft expands its guidance on asset management [10].

### What to record

A useful inventory goes beyond a list of IP addresses. The table is a template; start with the bold fields and add the rest over time.

| Field | Example | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Asset ID and name** | PR3-PLC01 | Unique reference in tickets and drawings |
| **Type** | PLC, HMI, robot controller, switch, server, workstation | Determines which controls apply |
| **Manufacturer, model** | (vendor), (model) | Match against vendor advisories |
| **Firmware / software version** | v32.011 | Vulnerability and patch decisions |
| **Zone** | Z3 Press control | Ties the asset to the zone model |
| **Location** | Press line 3, cabinet +A1 | Physical access and maintenance |
| **Owner** | Controls engineering, press | Who decides on changes |
| **Criticality** | Consequence score 4 | Prioritization |
| Network addresses and protocols | 10.30.3.11, EtherNet/IP | Firewall rules, monitoring baselines |
| Communication partners | HMI PR3-HMI01, MES server | Conduit definition |
| Remote access | Via jump host only | Exposure |
| Backup status | Logic backed up 2026-11-05, restore tested 2026-06 | Recovery readiness |
| Vendor support status | Supported until (date) or end of life | Lifecycle planning |
| Accounts | Named accounts yes/no; default password changed yes/no | FR 1 and FR 2 gaps |
| Known vulnerabilities | Advisory reference, decision | Patch and compensating measures |
| Spare parts and replacement | Spare in stores yes/no | Recovery time |

### How to build it without breaking anything

1. **Start from what exists.** Electrical drawings, PLC project files, network switch configurations, maintenance management (CMMS) records, purchase orders and the machine builders' documentation already contain most of the data.
2. **Passive network discovery.** Commercial OT monitoring tools listen to a copy of network traffic (via a SPAN port or network TAP) and identify devices, firmware versions and communication patterns without sending packets to the controllers.
3. **Vendor tools and controlled queries.** Engineering software can read device information from controllers safely. Active queries are acceptable when the vendor supports them and production agrees on a time window.
4. **Walk the floor.** Every inventory finds devices that are on no drawing: a forgotten cellular router, a laptop in a cabinet, a wireless access point installed by a contractor.
5. **Keep it alive.** Tie updates to management of change: no new device goes live without an inventory entry. Reconcile monthly with what monitoring sees.

### A note on tools

Specialized OT security platforms automate passive discovery and vulnerability matching. Microsoft announced that it will retire the OT security component of Defender for IoT in October 2029 and points customers to partner solutions [31]; the [Azure for manufacturing guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/manufacturing-it-azure/) (planned) covers that transition. Whatever tool you choose, the inventory belongs to the asset owner and should be exportable; do not lock it into one vendor's console.

## Network segmentation and the industrial DMZ

Segmentation implements FR 5 (restricted data flow). It is the single measure that would have limited the spread in most of the cases in section 3, and it is where most plants find the biggest gap.

### Target architecture

- **Two-layer boundary between enterprise and plant.** An industrial DMZ (IDMZ) between enterprise and site operations, with firewalls on both sides. No traffic passes directly from enterprise to the plant; every exchange terminates in the IDMZ (data broker, historian replica, file transfer, jump host, patch server).
- **Internal segmentation by zone.** Inside the plant, each zone sits in its own VLAN or physical network, with a firewall or access control lists at the conduit.
- **Safety systems separated.** Safety zones have no routable connection to the rest, or only through a tightly restricted conduit approved by process safety.
- **No dual-homed computers.** A PC with one network card in the office and one in the plant bypasses every firewall.
- **Internet only through defined paths.** Cloud connections leave through the IDMZ or from a dedicated IIoT zone, outbound only.

### A segmentation project in seven steps

1. **Map real traffic first.** Run passive monitoring for two to four weeks to see who talks to whom. Plants are always surprised.
2. **Draw the target zone model** (section 9) and agree on it with production.
3. **Build the IDMZ** and move cross-boundary services into it: historian replica, remote access gateway, patch server, file transfer.
4. **Set firewalls to "allow and log" first.** Install firewalls at conduits with rules that permit current traffic and log everything.
5. **Tighten rules in steps.** Replace broad rules with specific ones (source, destination, protocol, port), one zone at a time, during planned windows with controls engineers on site.
6. **Switch to "deny by default"** once the logs show only expected traffic.
7. **Put rules under management of change.** Every new rule needs a requester, a business reason, an expiry date and an approval.

### Firewall rule template

| Field | Example |
|---|---|
| Rule ID | C4-017 |
| Conduit | C4 (Z2 Site operations -> Z3 Press control) |

### Wireless and cellular connections

Wi-Fi for handheld scanners, wireless sensors and the cellular modems in machine cabinets all create paths that do not appear on the wired network drawing. Treat each wireless network as its own zone or as part of a conduit: separate SSIDs for plant devices and guests, certificate-based authentication for plant devices, no bridging between wireless and control networks, and a regular radio survey to find unauthorized access points and modems. Wireless IIoT sensors that send data directly to a cloud service belong in an IIoT zone such as Z12 in the worked example.

### Common segmentation problems

- **Engineering laptops** that are carried between zones and the office. Answer: fixed engineering workstations per zone or a jump host, and laptops that never leave the plant network.
- **Flat machine networks** delivered by machine builders, where the HMI, PLC and a remote access router sit on the same unmanaged switch. Answer: treat the machine as one zone and control the conduit at its edge.
- **Shared Active Directory.** An enterprise domain extended into the plant gives an attacker who owns the domain the plant too. Answer: a separate OT domain or local accounts with a defined trust model.
- **Broadcast-heavy protocols** that do not route well. Answer: plan VLAN boundaries with the controls vendor; some protocols need layer-2 neighbors.

## Secure remote access for vendors and engineers

In many plants, remote access is the largest uncontrolled path in: machine builders need it for support, and production wants fast help when a machine stops. Banning it rarely works; the practical answer is one controlled path that is easier to use than the workarounds.

In September 2026, CISA and the FBI published a fact sheet for critical infrastructure operators working with third-party ICS integrators. It cites an FBI analysis of a 2025 intrusion at a US automation company and recommends limiting integrator access to the minimum necessary, writing cybersecurity requirements (including remote access and patch management) into contracts, reducing internet exposure of devices, and monitoring and logging remote access, using on-demand access where possible [14].

### Requirements for remote access (template)

| Requirement | Detail | 62443 FR |
|---|---|---|
| One path | All remote sessions through a gateway in the IDMZ; no cellular modems, no vendor VPNs ending in production zones | FR 5 |
| Personal accounts | One account per person, no shared vendor accounts | FR 1 |
| Multifactor authentication | For every remote session | FR 1 |
| Approval per session | Plant contact approves each session; access is off by default | FR 2 |
| Least privilege | Access only to the assets the vendor supports, for the time needed | FR 2 |
| Session recording and logging | Recorded and kept for a defined period | FR 6 |
| Supervision for changes | Logic changes only with a plant engineer watching and a work order | FR 3 |
| File transfer | Files only through a scanned transfer zone | FR 3 |
| Contract | Remote access rules, notification duties and liability in the service contract | 2-4 |

### Practical tips

- **Make the secure path easier than the insecure one.** If approval takes two hours at night, someone will plug in a cellular router. Define an on-call approver per shift.
- **Remove the boxes.** Count every remote access device in the inventory and remove or disable those not routed through the gateway. Many machine builders deliver lines with their own router; put that into the purchase specification (section 17).
- **Review accounts quarterly.** Delete accounts of vendor staff who left.

## Patch and vulnerability management in OT

In office IT, the rule is "patch fast". In OT, the rule is "decide deliberately". A patch can require a vendor approval, a controller restart, a revalidation in regulated industries, or a full line test. In FDA-regulated plants (pharma, medical devices, food) and under automotive or aerospace customer requirements, a change to a validated system usually triggers your change control and validation procedure; plan the quality team's review time into the patch window. ISA-TR62443-2-3 covers patch management in the IACS environment [1].

### Sources of vulnerability information

- Vendor security advisories and product security portals of your PLC, HMI, drive and software suppliers.
- CISA's ICS advisories, which publish vulnerabilities in industrial products, often coordinated with the vendors [unverified as to current volume; check the CISA advisories page].
- Your OT monitoring tool, which matches firmware versions from the inventory against advisories.

### A decision table for each advisory

| Question | If yes | If no |
|---|---|---|
| Is the affected product in our inventory? | Continue | Close, document |
| Is the vulnerable function used or reachable in our configuration? | Continue | Document reason; recheck at next change |
| Is it known to be exploited or is a public exploit available? | Priority high | Priority by consequence score |
| Is the asset reachable from a lower-trust zone or remotely? | Priority high | Priority medium |
| Does the vendor approve the patch for our version? | Plan patch in next window | Apply compensating measures |
| Can the patch be applied without a shutdown? | Schedule in normal maintenance | Schedule in planned shutdown |

### Compensating measures when you cannot patch

When the patch is not available, not approved or not possible before the next shutdown:

- Restrict the conduit: allow only the hosts and protocols that need the asset.
- Disable unused services and ports.
- Put the controller key switch in RUN to block remote program changes where the controller supports it.
- Increase monitoring of the asset (alerts on new connections or program downloads).
- Document the residual risk and get the asset owner's approval.

### Example decision

An advisory describes a remotely exploitable denial-of-service vulnerability in the web server of the PLC model used in Z3 Press control. Inventory shows four affected PLCs. The web server is enabled but not used. The firmware fix is available but requires a controller restart and the vendor recommends a functional test. Decision: disable the web server on all four PLCs during the next weekend window (30 minutes per press), block HTTP and HTTPS to the PLCs in conduit C4, and install the firmware at the planned shutdown in eight weeks. The interim risk is documented and approved by the plant engineering manager.

### Legacy and end-of-life systems

Every plant has systems that will never get another patch: HMIs on unsupported Windows versions, controllers whose vendor has discontinued them. Treat them as a lifecycle problem with a budget, not as a security exception to be renewed forever. The inventory field "vendor support status" feeds a replacement plan aligned with line rebuilds and capital planning.

## Accounts, hardening and removable media

FR 1 to FR 3 are where plants have the most visible gaps and the cheapest fixes.

### Accounts and identities

- **Retire shared logins where the system supports it.** "Operator", "maint" and "admin" accounts shared by a whole shift make every change untraceable. Many current HMIs and SCADA systems support named accounts or badge login; older ones may not.
- **Separate admin accounts.** Engineers use a normal account for email and a separate one for OT administration, never the same.
- **Change default passwords** on every device that has them: switches, routers, HMIs, robot controllers, cameras. Make it a commissioning checklist item.
- **Use MFA where a human logs in remotely or to the IDMZ.** On the plant floor, MFA on an HMI is often impractical; compensate with physical access control and short screen locks where the process allows it.
- **Protect the help desk.** The Clorox case shows that a social engineering call to an IT help desk can lead to privileged access [20]. Require identity verification for password and MFA resets, with stricter checks for privileged accounts.

### Hardening of Windows-based OT systems

HMIs, SCADA servers, historians and engineering workstations are mostly Windows computers. Hardening them follows vendor guidance:

1. Remove software and services the system does not need (least function).
2. Use application allowlisting instead of, or in addition to, antivirus, where the vendor supports it.
3. Disable or restrict USB ports.
4. Restrict local administrator rights.
5. Keep a known-good image and the installation media for fast rebuild.

### Controllers

- Keep the key switch or software protection in RUN where the controller offers it, and log every change to PROGRAM mode.
- Store the current logic offline and compare checksums or project versions regularly to detect unauthorized changes (FR 3).
- Disable unused services (web servers, FTP, SNMP write access).

### Removable media

USB drives and laptops bring malware across every network boundary. A simple rule set:

| Rule | Implementation |
|---|---|
| No private media | Written policy, training, signage at stations |
| Scan before use | A scanning kiosk at the plant entrance or in the IDMZ |
| Company media only | Encrypted, inventoried USB drives for engineering |
| Prefer the network path | File transfer through the IDMZ server instead of USB |
| Technical enforcement | USB ports disabled or restricted by policy on HMIs and servers |

## Monitoring and detection

FR 6 (timely response to events) requires that you notice incidents. In OT, detection has two sources: logs and network traffic.

### What to log

| Source | Events | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Firewalls at conduits | Allowed and denied connections | Unexpected traffic between zones |
| Remote access gateway | Logins, approvals, session recordings | Vendor activity review |
| OT Active Directory and jump hosts | Logins, privilege changes, new accounts | Account misuse |
| Engineering workstations | Software starts, project downloads | Unauthorized logic changes |
| Controllers (where supported) | Mode changes, program downloads, firmware updates | Integrity |
| Backup system | Job success and failure | Recovery readiness |

### Passive OT network monitoring

Specialized OT monitoring tools decode industrial protocols and learn the normal communication pattern of a plant. They then alert on deviations: a new device, a new connection between zones, a program download outside a maintenance window, or a command sent from an unusual host. Because they only listen to copied traffic, they do not affect the process. The NIST SP 800-82 Rev. 4 draft expands its guidance on network monitoring and detection [10].

### Who watches the alerts

An alert nobody reads is a cost without benefit. Options for a mid-size plant:

- **Corporate security operations center (SOC)** with an OT playbook and a named controls engineer on call at the plant.
- **Managed detection service** from a provider with OT experience.
- **Plant-level review** of a small number of high-value alerts (new device, program download, remote session outside approval), with the rest forwarded to the SOC.

Whatever you choose, define who may take which action. A SOC analyst should never be able to shut down a line on their own; the playbook states who decides.

## Backup, recovery and OT incident response

FR 7 (resource availability) and the "Respond" and "Recover" functions of CSF 2.0 come together here [11]. The Norsk Hydro case is the classic example: the company kept large parts of production running with manual procedures and later estimated its total cost at about NOK 800 million [17].

### What to back up

| Item | Frequency | Storage | Restore test |
|---|---|---|---|
| PLC, safety PLC and robot programs | After every change and monthly | Offline and offsite copy | Annually per critical controller, on a spare or simulator |
| HMI and SCADA projects | After every change | Offline and offsite | Annually |
| Server images (MES, historian, SCADA, OT domain controller) | Weekly full, daily incremental | Immutable or offline copy | Twice a year, full rebuild of one server |
| Network device configurations | After every change | Offline | Annually |
| Firmware and installation media | When updated | Offline library | Check completeness annually |
| Documentation (drawings, zone model, inventory, passwords vault export) | Monthly | Offline, printed copy of essentials | Check during tabletop exercise |

### The OT incident playbook

An OT playbook differs from an IT playbook in who decides and what comes first.

| Phase | OT-specific content |
|---|---|
| Preparation | Contact list incl. machine builders and controls vendors; decision rights; spare engineering laptop with clean images; manual operating procedures per area |
| Detection and analysis | Joint call of IT security, controls lead and production manager within a defined time; first question: is safety affected? |
| Containment | Pre-agreed options: disconnect enterprise from IDMZ, disconnect IDMZ from plant, isolate single zones. Who may pull which plug, at which risk to production |
| Safe state | Process safety decides whether to run, run in manual or shut down safely; safety systems are never disabled to "help" recovery |
| Eradication and recovery | Rebuild from known-good images; verify controller logic against offline backups before restart; staged restart by zone |
| Reporting | Legal and communications: SEC materiality assessment for public companies, customer notifications, insurer, law enforcement, NIS2 or CRA reports for EU obligations (section 19) |
| Lessons learned | Root cause, updated zone model and playbook |

### Practice it

Run a tabletop exercise at least once a year with production, maintenance, IT, EHS, communications and management. A useful first scenario: "Ransomware has encrypted the MES and the ERP. The lines are running. Do we keep running, and how do we track what we build and ship?" The answer reveals whether manual fallback procedures exist.

The CISA CPGs emphasize governance and measurable foundational practices, including incident response planning; CPG 2.0 added a governance component in December 2025 [13].

## Supplier and procurement requirements

Most of a plant's security level is bought, not built. Every new machine, line and control system is a chance to raise it. 62443 gives you the vocabulary for the purchase specification: 4-1 and 4-2 for products, 2-4 for integrators and service providers, and 3-3 for the delivered system.

### Certification you can ask for

ISASecure, a wholly owned subsidiary of ISA founded in 2007, runs certification programs based on 62443 [7]:

| Program | What is certified | Based on |
|---|---|---|
| ISASecure CSA | Components (controllers, HMIs, network devices, software) | IEC 62443-4-2 [7] |
| ISASecure SSA | Systems | IEC 62443-3-3 [7] |
| ISASecure SDLA | A supplier's secure development lifecycle | IEC 62443-4-1 [7] |
| ISASecure ACSSA | An operating IACS at a site: policies, procedures, service providers and technical controls, starting with the asset owner's risk assessment | IEC 62443-2-1, 2-4, 3-2 and 3-3; announced June 18, 2025 [8] |

Certificates from other conformity assessment schemes also exist (the IEC's IECEE cybersecurity program is the main alternative to ISASecure, as an ISASecure white paper published by ISA describes [37]). A certificate is evidence, not a guarantee: check the certified product version and security level against what you are buying.

### Clauses for the purchase specification (template)

| Topic | Example requirement |
|---|---|
| Standard | Supplier complies with IEC 62443-4-1 (product) and IEC 62443-2-4 (integration) in the current edition at order date; evidence: certificate or self-assessment against the listed requirements |
| Security level | Components and the delivered system meet SL-C 2 (SL-C 3 for zones Z4, Z8) per IEC 62443-4-2 / 3-3 |
| Zone fit | Machine delivered as one zone with a defined conduit interface; documented list of all ports, protocols and services |
| Remote access | No built-in modems or routers; remote support only through the asset owner's gateway |
| Accounts | Named user accounts with role-based rights; no hard-coded or undocumented accounts; default passwords changed at commissioning |
| Hardening | Unused services disabled; hardening guide delivered |
| Patching | Supplier publishes security advisories, tests OS and firmware patches within a stated time, supports the product for a stated period |
| SBOM | Software bill of materials for delivered software and firmware |
| Backups | Delivered programs and projects in editable form, with restore instructions |
| Documentation | Network diagram, asset list in the asset owner's template, firewall rule requirements |
| Testing | Security acceptance test (port scan in a test environment, account review) as part of factory and site acceptance tests |
| Incident duties | Supplier notifies the asset owner of vulnerabilities and incidents affecting the delivered system within a stated time |

The CRA (section 19) makes several of these requirements legal obligations for products placed on the EU market, including security updates and an SBOM [22][23]. US buyers benefit from that even for US-only plants, because the same products are sold worldwide.

### Integrators and service providers

The CISA/FBI integrator fact sheet of September 2026 adds the operator's view: put cybersecurity and supply chain requirements into contracts and service agreements, define where data and devices are hosted, and limit and monitor integrator access [14]. Ask service providers for their 62443-2-4 evidence, and review their remote access and data handling like you would review a contractor's safety program.

## People, safety and the plant floor

OT security fails when it is designed in the IT office and imposed on the floor. The people who run the machines are both the first line of defense and the first to suffer from badly designed controls.

### Roles and responsibilities (example for a mid-size site)

| Role | OT security responsibility |
|---|---|
| Plant manager | Asset owner; approves risk assessments and residual risks; owns the budget |
| OT security lead (often a controls engineer, part-time at first) | Zone model, inventory, rules, incident coordination |
| Corporate IT security / CISO | Enterprise zone, identity, SOC, policies, alignment with CSF 2.0 |
| Controls engineering | Secure configuration, backups, logic change control |
| Maintenance | Physical access, removable media rules, spare parts |
| EHS / process safety | Links PHA/HAZOP to security consequences; signs off on safety zones |
| Purchasing | Security clauses in specifications and contracts |
| Production supervisors and operators | Report anomalies, follow access and media rules, run manual fallback |

### Safety and security together

Safety engineers and security engineers often use different words for similar things: hazards and threats, SIL and SL, PHA and risk assessment. Bring them into one room. Two rules are non-negotiable:

- Security measures must never disable or degrade a safety function.
- A security incident response must never bypass the safety team's decision on safe state.

### Training that operators accept

- Make reporting safe. An operator who admits plugging in a phone charger should get thanks, not discipline; otherwise the next incident goes unreported.
- Short, role-specific modules: what an operator should report (unknown USB drives, unexpected HMI behavior, strangers asking for access), not a generic phishing course.
- Explain why. Operators understand lockout/tagout because they know what it prevents. Security rules need the same explanation.
- Involve the workforce in designing controls that affect them, such as badge login on HMIs or screen locks. In unionized plants, changes to work rules or monitoring may need to be discussed with the union; involve HR and labor relations early [general guidance; check your collective bargaining agreement].

For the broader routine of daily problem solving on the floor, see the guide on [shop floor management](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/shop-floor-management/) (planned); a security anomaly is one more item for the daily tier meeting.

## US and EU obligations: CMMC, SEC, CIRCIA, NIS2 and the CRA

62443 itself is voluntary. The obligations below are not. Which ones apply depends on your customers, your ownership and where you sell.

### CMMC 2.0 for defense suppliers

If you make parts for the US Department of Defense, the Cybersecurity Maturity Model Certification (CMMC) applies to systems that process, store or transmit Federal Contract Information (FCI) or Controlled Unclassified Information (CUI). The program rule (32 CFR Part 170) took effect on December 16, 2024 [28]. The DFARS rule that puts CMMC into contracts took effect on November 10, 2025, with a phased rollout over three years: phase 2 started on November 10, 2026, and phase 3 is scheduled for November 10, 2027 [29]. Level 2 is based on NIST SP 800-171 [28][29].

What this means for the plant floor:

- **Scope is about data, not about machines.** A CNC machine that receives drawings or programs marked as CUI may be in scope. A controller that only runs a conveyor usually is not. The CUI markings and the contract decide.
- **Use zones to limit scope.** A well-segmented zone model can keep the CMMC assessment boundary small. That is one of the clearest financial arguments for segmentation in defense supply chains.
- **Special cases.** For Level 2, 32 CFR 170.19(c)(1) lists OT, IIoT and IoT devices, test equipment, government-furnished equipment and restricted information systems as "Specialized Assets": assets that can process, store or transmit CUI but cannot be fully secured. They must be in the asset inventory, in the SSP, managed under your risk-based security policies and shown in the network diagram; the assessor reviews the SSP but does not assess them against the other CMMC requirements [38].

For the planning side of defense work, the [aerospace production planning guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/aerospace-production-planning/) (planned) covers CMMC, ITAR and DFARS in the context of AS9100 supply chains.

### SEC rules for public companies

Under the SEC rules adopted on July 26, 2023, registrants must disclose a material cybersecurity incident on Form 8-K within four business days after determining it is material, and describe their cybersecurity risk management, strategy and governance in the annual report [26]. A ransomware attack that stops a plant is a typical candidate for a materiality assessment, so the OT playbook needs a fast path to the disclosure committee.

### CIRCIA

The Cyber Incident Reporting for Critical Infrastructure Act will require covered entities to report covered cyber incidents to CISA within 72 hours and ransom payments within 24 hours, once CISA's final rule takes effect. As of October 2026, the final rule had gone to the White House for review and had not been published [27]; on November 9, 2026, it was still pending there after arriving on October 1, 2026 [39]. Critical manufacturing is a critical infrastructure sector [32], so track which manufacturers the final rule covers.

### NIS2 for US companies with EU sites

The EU NIS2 Directive applies through national laws to essential and important entities in the EU, including many manufacturing sectors. A US company is affected through its EU subsidiaries and plants. Germany's implementation act took effect on December 6, 2025; Germany's Federal Office for Information Security (BSI) states that it now supervises about 29,500 entities instead of about 4,500 [24]. In Germany, companies are in scope if they fall under the listed entity types and have at least 50 employees or more than EUR 10 million in both annual revenue and balance sheet total; manufacturing entries include medical devices, computers and electronics, electrical equipment, machinery and vehicles [25][40]. Significant incidents need an early warning within 24 hours, a notification within 72 hours and a final report within one month [25]. Other member states have similar rules with local details; check each country where you have a plant.

### Cyber Resilience Act for machine builders and component makers

If you sell machines, controllers or software with digital elements on the EU market, the Cyber Resilience Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/2847) applies, including to US manufacturers. Since September 11, 2026, manufacturers must report actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe incidents through ENISA's Single Reporting Platform: an early warning within 24 hours, a notification within 72 hours, a final report within 14 days after a corrective measure is available (vulnerabilities) or within one month (incidents) [22]. The main product requirements apply from December 11, 2027 [23]. For machine builders, that turns 62443-4-1 style secure development into product compliance.

### Cyber insurance

Insurers increasingly ask detailed questions about OT: segmentation, remote access, offline backups, MFA. The Merck case shows how much can depend on policy wording; a New Jersey appellate court ruled that the war exclusion in Merck's property policies did not apply to NotPetya before the parties settled [18]. Review with your broker whether your policies cover production losses from cyber events and under which conditions [general guidance].

## A 12 to 24 month roadmap

The roadmap below is for a single mid-size site like the one in section 9, with an OT security lead at about half of a full-time position in year 1 (our planning assumption), support from corporate IT and an external partner for specific tasks such as the risk assessment workshop and the segmentation design. Multi-site companies run phase 0 centrally and phases 1 to 4 per site, starting with the plant that has the highest risk or the most upcoming capital projects.

| Phase | Months | Goals | Main deliverables | 62443 / CSF anchor |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0. Mandate | 0-1 | Ownership and scope | Named asset owner and OT security lead, steering group, scope of the first system under consideration, budget for year 1 | 2-1; CSF Govern |
| 1. Know and stop the bleeding | 1-6 | Visibility and quick risk reduction | Asset inventory (first pass), traffic map, list of remote access paths; remove or route uncontrolled remote access; offline backups of all controller logic; default passwords changed; removable media rules; first tabletop exercise | 3-2 ZCR 1-2; CSF Identify, Protect |
| 2. Architecture | 4-12 | Containment | Zone and conduit model with SL-T, approved by the asset owner; IDMZ built; enterprise-to-plant traffic only through IDMZ; remote access gateway with MFA and recording; OT domain or account separation | 3-2 ZCR 3-7; 3-3 FR 5; CSF Protect |
| 3. Detect and respond | 9-18 | Seeing and reacting | Passive OT monitoring on key conduits; log collection; SOC playbook with decision rights; written OT incident playbook; restore tests | 3-3 FR 6, FR 7; CSF Detect, Respond, Recover |
| 4. Harden and buy right | 12-24 | Raise SL-A, prevent new gaps | Internal segmentation completed; hardening of Windows OT systems; patch decision process running; security clauses in all new purchase specifications; integrator requirements per 2-4; KPI reporting to management | 2-3, 2-4, 4-1, 4-2; CSF Govern |
| 5. Run and improve | from 24 | Program in operation | Annual risk assessment update, yearly tabletop, internal audit, optional ACSSA or other site assessment | 2-1; CSF all functions |

### What to do in the first 30 days

1. Name the asset owner (usually the plant manager) and the OT security lead.
2. List every remote access path you know of and ask each machine builder how they connect.
3. Check whether you have offline copies of the current programs of your most critical controllers.
4. Find out whether the office network and the plant network are routed to each other without a firewall.
5. Ask NIST MEP or your corporate security team for a CSF-based self-assessment [30].
6. Schedule a two-hour tabletop with the scenario "MES and ERP are encrypted".

None of these steps needs new technology. They produce the facts on which the budget for phase 2 is based.

### Fitting the roadmap into plant reality

- **Use planned shutdowns.** Segmentation and firmware changes need windows; plan them a year ahead with maintenance.
- **Ride on capital projects.** A new line is the cheapest moment to build a clean zone with secure remote access and named accounts.
- **Do not start with the tool.** Buying a monitoring platform before the inventory and zone model exist produces alerts nobody can interpret.

## Budget and ROI: how to make the case

OT security does not produce revenue. It reduces the probability and the impact of a loss. The case therefore has three parts: expected loss avoided, enabling value, and obligations.

### Cost categories

| Category | Typical items | Capex or opex |
|---|---|---|
| Architecture | Firewalls for IDMZ and conduits, managed switches, IDMZ servers, remote access gateway | Mostly capex (hardware), opex for subscriptions |
| Visibility | OT monitoring platform, sensors, network TAPs | Capex or subscription |
| People | OT security lead (part- or full-time), training, external specialists | Opex |
| Services | Risk assessment support, segmentation design, SOC or managed detection | Opex |
| Replacement | Upgrade of end-of-life HMIs and controllers | Capex, ideally inside line rebuilds |
| Recovery | Backup systems, spare engineering workstations, spare controllers | Capex |

We do not give generic dollar figures here because no verifiable US source offers a reliable benchmark for a plant of a given size; prices depend on the number of zones, the age of the equipment and the vendors. Get two or three scoped offers after phase 1.

### Expected loss: a worked example

Annualized loss expectancy (ALE) is a simple way to put risk into the language of the finance team:

ALE = annual rate of occurrence (ARO) x single loss expectancy (SLE)

For Midwest Components (section 9), the finance and operations team estimates the cost of a ransomware event that stops the plant:

| Item | Assumption | Amount |
|---|---|---|
| Contribution margin lost | 10 production days x $160,000 contribution margin per day | $1,600,000 |
| Overtime and expediting to recover backlog | Estimate | $350,000 |
| Scrap and rework from interrupted batches | Estimate | $120,000 |
| Incident response and recovery services | Estimate | $400,000 |
| Customer penalties | Contract terms with two OEM customers | $250,000 |
| **SLE (single loss expectancy)** | | **$2,720,000** |

Without the program, the team estimates the annual likelihood of such an event at 10% (ARO 0.10); with phases 1 to 3 complete, it expects the event to be both less likely (ARO 0.04, because segmentation and controlled remote access close the most common paths) and shorter (5 days instead of 10, because backups and playbooks are tested), which reduces the SLE to about $1,640,000 (5 days x $160,000 = $800,000, half the overtime at $175,000, unchanged scrap of $120,000, response costs of $300,000 and unchanged penalties of $250,000, rounded).

- ALE before: 0.10 x $2,720,000 = $272,000 per year
- ALE after: 0.04 x $1,640,000 = $65,600 per year
- Risk reduction: $272,000 - $65,600 = $206,400 per year

If the program costs $450,000 over two years plus $120,000 per year in running costs, the simple payback on risk reduction alone is: ($450,000) / ($206,400 - $120,000) = about 5.2 years. That looks modest, and it should be presented honestly. The assumptions are estimates; the ARO in particular is uncertain. Present a range (for example ARO 0.05 to 0.20) and let management see how the result changes. With ARO 0.20 before and 0.06 after, the yearly reduction is $544,000 - $98,400 = $445,600, and the payback drops to about 1.4 years.

All figures in this example are illustrative assumptions, not industry benchmarks.

### Value beyond loss avoidance

- **Customer and contract requirements:** CMMC for defense work [28][29], security questionnaires from OEM customers, EU obligations for EU sites and products [22][24].
- **Insurance:** better answers on segmentation, MFA and backups affect insurability and terms [general guidance; ask your broker].
- **Enabling digital projects:** a clean IDMZ and conduit model make MES, historian and cloud projects faster and safer to approve. See [predictive maintenance and IIoT](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/predictive-maintenance-iiot/) (planned) and [digital twin in manufacturing](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/digital-twin-manufacturing/) (planned) for the projects that depend on it.
- **Fewer self-inflicted outages:** asset inventory, change control and tested backups also shorten recovery from ordinary failures, such as a failed hard drive in an HMI.

### Capex and opex

Firewalls and servers are usually capitalized; subscriptions, managed services and staff are opex. Moving to subscription tools shifts spending from capex to opex, which matters for plants with a tight capital budget but a flexible operating budget. Discuss with the plant controller early how the program is classified; it affects which budget owner has to approve.

## OT security KPIs

Measure a few indicators that drive behavior. Report them monthly to the steering group and quarterly to management. For KPI design in general, see the guide on [manufacturing KPIs](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/manufacturing-kpis/) (planned).

| KPI | Formula | Example target | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|---|
| Inventory coverage | Assets with complete mandatory fields / assets seen by monitoring x 100 | > 95% | Basis for everything else |
| Uncontrolled remote access paths | Count of remote paths not through the gateway | 0 | Most common entry path |
| Zones with approved SL-T | Zones with signed risk assessment / total zones x 100 | 100% | Shows the method is applied |
| Conduit rule hygiene | Firewall rules with owner, reason and valid review date / all rules x 100 | > 90% | Prevents rule sprawl |
| Critical controllers with tested restore | Controllers with restore tested in the last 12 months / critical controllers x 100 | 100% | Recovery readiness |
| Backup currency | Controllers whose offline backup matches the running program / all controllers x 100 | > 98% | Detects drift and unauthorized change |
| Mean time to decide on advisories | Average days from advisory publication to documented decision | < 30 days | Patch process works |
| Shared account reduction | Systems still using shared logins / systems in scope | Falling each quarter | FR 1 and FR 2 progress |
| Alert triage time | Median time from high-priority OT alert to triage | < 1 hour | Detection is useful |
| Tabletop exercises | Exercises per year with production present | At least 1 | Response is practiced |
| New equipment with security clauses | Purchase orders for automation with 62443 clauses / all automation purchase orders x 100 | 100% | Prevents new gaps |

**Example calculation.** Monitoring sees 640 assets; 598 have all mandatory fields. Inventory coverage = 598 / 640 x 100 = 93.4%. Target missed by 1.6 points; action: complete the 42 records, mostly in the welding area.

Avoid vanity metrics such as "number of blocked connections". A rising number can mean better protection or more misconfiguration.

## Common mistakes

1. **Copying IT controls one-to-one.** Active scanning, automatic patching and endpoint agents on controllers have caused outages. Test everything with the vendor first.
2. **Starting with a tool purchase.** Without inventory and zone model, a monitoring platform only produces noise.
3. **One giant zone.** A firewall between office and plant is necessary, but a flat plant network behind it lets malware reach every line.
4. **Too many zones.** Rules nobody understands get opened wide "temporarily" and stay that way.
5. **Ignoring remote access boxes.** The cellular router in a machine cabinet bypasses all of the architecture.
6. **Backups that were never restored.** Many plants find during an incident that the backup is incomplete or old.
7. **No decision rights.** In an incident, the question "who may disconnect the plant?" must already have an answer.
8. **Security without production.** Controls designed without operators and maintenance get bypassed.
9. **Treating 62443 as a certificate project.** The value is in the method and the ongoing routine, not the paper.
10. **Forgetting the help desk and identity.** Clorox shows that an identity reset process can be the weakest link [20].
11. **Buying new gaps.** Every machine ordered without security clauses adds a legacy problem for the next 20 years.
12. **Assuming "air gap".** Most plants that believe they are air-gapped have at least one unknown connection.

## Limits of IEC 62443 and of OT security programs

62443 is the best available framework for industrial automation security, but it has limits that you should know before you build a program on it.

- **Cost and access.** The documents are paid publications, and Industrial Cyber puts the full series at more than 800 pages [34]. Small plants rarely read them in full; quick start guides and consultants fill the gap, with varying quality.
- **Age and revision.** 3-3 dates from 2013, and the second editions of 3-2, 3-3 and 4-2 are still in work [1][6]. Some topics (cloud, IIoT, containerized edge) are only addressed by newer PAS documents such as 1-6 [5].
- **Judgment, not formula.** The standard tells you to set SL-T by risk, but it does not tell you exactly which SL a press line needs. Two qualified teams may reach different answers.
- **Security levels are not guarantees.** An SL-C 3 component configured badly gives SL-A 1. Certification covers specific versions and configurations.
- **Legacy reality.** Many installed systems cannot meet SL 2 requirements for FR 1 and FR 2. Compensating measures work, but they add complexity.
- **Organizational demand.** 2-1 and 2-4 require documented processes; ML 2 and above are a real effort for a small plant and its integrators.
- **No protection against everything.** Segmentation and monitoring reduce risk; they do not prevent a determined, well-resourced attacker. Recovery capability remains essential.
- **Framework overlap.** Mapping 62443 to CSF 2.0, SP 800-82, CMMC and NIS2 takes effort. The SP 800-82 Rev. 4 draft, restructured around CSF 2.0, should make the NIST side easier once final [10].

## FAQ: OT cybersecurity and IEC 62443

### What is OT cybersecurity?

OT cybersecurity protects the systems that monitor and control physical processes, such as PLCs, SCADA, HMIs, robots and safety systems, against disruption and manipulation. Its priorities are safety and availability first, then integrity and confidentiality. NIST SP 800-82 Rev. 3 is the main US guide [9].

### What is the difference between IT and OT security?

IT security protects data and business systems, usually with confidentiality first. OT security protects physical processes, where a failed control can injure people or stop production. OT systems live longer, are harder to patch, often use protocols without authentication, and change only in planned windows (section 2).

### What is ISA/IEC 62443?

A series of international standards for the security of industrial automation and control systems, developed by the ISA99 committee and the IEC. It is organized in four groups (general, policies and procedures, system, component) and defines roles, zones and conduits, security levels and foundational requirements [1][2].

### What is the difference between OT security and ICS security?

In everyday use, none. ICS security is the older term and refers to industrial control systems such as PLC, DCS and SCADA. OT security is broader and also covers building automation, transportation and physical access systems, which is how NIST SP 800-82 Rev. 3 defines OT [9]. IIoT security is the part that deals with connected sensors and gateways that send data to edge or cloud platforms; IEC PAS 62443-1-6 applies the 62443 series to IIoT [5].

### What are the roles in IEC 62443?

The asset owner (accountable for the IACS), the integration service provider, the maintenance service provider, and the product supplier [2]. Each role has its own parts of the series, which makes it possible to write clear contracts (section 5).

### Is IEC 62443 mandatory in the US?

No US law requires 62443 in general. It becomes binding through customer contracts, purchase specifications, insurance conditions or sector rules. EU laws such as the CRA create obligations that 62443-based processes help meet [22].

### What are the security levels in IEC 62443?

SL 1 protects against casual or coincidental violation, SL 2 against intentional violation with simple means, SL 3 against sophisticated attackers with IACS-specific skills and moderate resources, and SL 4 against sophisticated attackers with extended resources and high motivation [2]. Each zone gets a target level, often per foundational requirement.

### What are the seven foundational requirements of IEC 62443?

Identification and authentication control, use control, system integrity, data confidentiality, restricted data flow, timely response to events, and resource availability [2].

### What are zones and conduits?

A zone groups assets with similar security requirements; a conduit groups the communication channels between zones and carries the controls for that traffic [2]. Section 9 shows a full example for a mid-size plant.

### Which part of IEC 62443 should an asset owner start with?

With 62443-2-1 for the security program and 62443-3-2 for the risk assessment and zone model. 3-3 then defines the system requirements per security level, and 2-4 and 4-1/4-2 go into contracts with integrators and suppliers [1].

### How does IEC 62443 relate to the NIST Cybersecurity Framework?

They complement each other. CSF 2.0 provides governance and a common language for the whole company [11]; 62443 provides the technical and organizational method for the plant. NIST SP 800-82 links the two, and its Rev. 4 draft is restructured around CSF 2.0 [10].

### What is the Purdue model and is it still relevant?

A layered reference architecture from field devices (level 0) to the enterprise (levels 4 and 5), often with an industrial DMZ at level 3.5. It remains a useful starting map for zones, but IIoT and cloud connections cut across its levels, so zones must follow real risk and data flows (section 7).

### How does zero trust apply to OT?

Zero trust means no implicit trust based on network location: every user, device and connection must be authorized for the specific access it needs. In a plant that matches what 62443 already asks for: zones with explicit, allow-listed conduits, individual accounts instead of shared logins, and brokered, approved remote access sessions (sections 7, 11, 12 and 14). Start at the conduits and with management functions such as engineering and vendor access, because many field protocols have no built-in authentication (section 2). The NIST SP 800-82 Rev. 4 draft adds guidance on zero trust principles for system management functions [10].

### Can you patch legacy PLCs without stopping production?

Often not right away, and you do not have to. First check whether the vulnerable function is used and reachable. If the firmware fix needs a restart or a functional test, apply compensating measures now: restrict the conduit, disable unused services such as an unused web server, keep the key switch in RUN where supported, and watch the asset more closely. Document and approve the residual risk, then install the fix at the next planned shutdown. Section 13 has a decision table and a worked example. Controllers that will never get a fix belong in a replacement plan, not in a permanent exception.

### Can you get certified to IEC 62443?

Yes. Products can be certified under ISASecure CSA (4-2), systems under SSA (3-3), development organizations under SDLA (4-1) [7], and since June 2025 operating sites under ISASecure ACSSA [8]. Other conformity assessment schemes also certify against 62443, above all the IEC's IECEE cybersecurity program [37].

### What is the difference between 62443-4-1 and 62443-4-2?

4-1 covers the supplier's secure development process (how products are built), 4-2 the technical security capabilities of a component (what the product can do) [1][7].

### How long does it take to implement IEC 62443 in a plant?

For a single mid-size site, a realistic path is 12 to 24 months for the core: inventory, zone model, IDMZ, remote access, backups, monitoring and incident response (section 20). The program then continues as an annual routine. This estimate is based on the roadmap in this guide, not on a published benchmark.

### What does OT security cost?

It depends on the number of zones, the age of the equipment and the chosen tools; we found no reliable public benchmark. Section 21 shows how to build the case with annualized loss expectancy and gives a worked example with assumptions.

### Does CMMC apply to OT systems?

CMMC applies to systems that process, store or transmit FCI or CUI. Machines that receive CUI-marked drawings or programs may be in scope; good segmentation keeps the assessment boundary small [28][29]. At Level 2, OT that can handle CUI but cannot be fully secured counts as a "Specialized Asset": documented in the inventory, SSP and network diagram, but not assessed against the other requirements [38].

### What did the Colonial Pipeline attack teach manufacturers?

That an IT incident can stop an OT operation. Colonial shut down pipeline operations after a ransomware attack on or about May 7, 2021, paid about 75 bitcoin, and the Department of Justice later seized 63.7 bitcoin [16]. Knowing your dependencies and having a degraded operating mode matter as much as protecting controllers.

### Do US companies with EU plants have to follow NIS2?

Their EU entities may have to, if they fall under the national implementation law's sectors and size thresholds. Germany, for example, applies NIS2 to listed entity types with at least 50 employees or more than EUR 10 million in both revenue and balance sheet total [25][40].

## Conclusion

OT cybersecurity is plant engineering with an attacker in mind. ISA/IEC 62443 gives manufacturers a method that fits the plant: know your assets, divide the plant into zones with controlled conduits, set target security levels by risk, close the gap with technical and organizational measures, and hold suppliers and integrators to the same rules. NIST CSF 2.0 and SP 800-82 connect that method to US governance, and CMMC, SEC rules, NIS2 and the CRA add obligations where they apply.

The documented incidents point to the same first steps: separate the plant from the enterprise through an industrial DMZ, control every remote access path, keep tested offline backups of controller logic and servers, and rehearse how the plant runs when IT is down. None of that requires waiting for a perfect architecture or the next edition of the standard. Start with the 30-day list in section 20, build the zone model in the first year, and measure progress with a handful of KPIs. Within two years, a mid-size plant can move from "we hope we are air-gapped" to a documented, tested and steadily improving security program.
