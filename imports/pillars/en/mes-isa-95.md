# Manufacturing Execution System (MES) and ISA-95: Functions, Levels, Selection and ROI

*Updated: November 2026 · Reading time: about 50 minutes (quick start: short answer, key takeaways, the comparison table in section 5 and the glossary)*

> **Short answer:** A manufacturing execution system (MES) is the software layer that manages, monitors and records the execution of production orders on the shop floor, in real time, between the ERP system that plans and the control systems that run the machines [8]. ISA-95 (ANSI/ISA-95, internationally IEC 62264) is the standard that defines where this layer sits (Level 3, manufacturing operations management), what activities it covers and what information it exchanges with ERP [1][3].

> **Key takeaways**
> - Gartner defines MES as production-oriented software that "manages, monitors and synchronizes the execution of real-time physical processes" and coordinates work orders with scheduling, ERP, PLM and quality systems [8].
> - ISA-95 has eight parts. Part 1 (models and terminology) was republished in 2025; Part 3 (activity models) dates from 2013 [1][2].
> - ISA-95 describes five levels. Levels 0 to 2 are the physical process and its control, Level 3 is manufacturing operations management (MES and related systems), Level 4 is business planning and logistics (ERP) [1].
> - The Part 3 activity model splits Level 3 into four categories (production, maintenance, quality test, inventory) and repeats the same eight activities in each: definition management, resource management, detailed scheduling, dispatching, execution management, data collection, tracking and performance analysis [3].
> - MESA International's 1997 model named 11 MES functions; ISA-95 later absorbed that thinking [9][12]. MESA ceased operations on June 30, 2026, and its content went to ISA [11].
> - A unified namespace (UNS) changes how data moves; it does not replace the MES logic or the ISA-95 vocabulary.
> - An MES business case usually stands or falls with one or two benefit lines. In the worked example below, the same project has a positive five-year NPV in the base case and a negative one in the conservative case.
> - Start with the data model and one value stream, not with the full feature list.

## What is a manufacturing execution system (MES)?

A manufacturing execution system is the software that turns a production order into finished, documented goods. ERP tells the plant *what* to make, how many and by when. Machines and their controllers make the parts. The MES sits in between: it releases and sequences work, tells operators and machines what to do next, collects what actually happened (quantities, times, parameters, materials, defects, who did it) and reports the result back.

Gartner's market definition is a good reference point because it is vendor-neutral and precise. It calls MES "a specialist class of production-oriented software" that "manages, monitors and synchronizes the execution of real-time physical processes involved in transforming raw materials into intermediate and/or finished goods." It adds that these systems coordinate work orders with production scheduling and with enterprise systems such as ERP, product lifecycle management and quality management, and that they provide feedback on process performance and support traceability and genealogy down to component and material level [8].

### The core idea in one sentence

An MES closes the loop between plan and reality, shift by shift, so that everybody from the operator to the controller works from the same, timely record of what happened on the floor.

### What an MES does in a typical shift

A concrete picture helps more than a feature list. Take a midsize plant that machines and assembles hydraulic valves on four lines:

1. **06:00, shift start.** The MES shows the supervisor the dispatch list for Line 2: twelve orders released from ERP, sequenced by the scheduler to minimize changeovers. Two orders are flagged because a gauge is overdue for calibration.
2. **06:10.** The operator scans the first order's traveler (or picks it on a terminal). The MES checks that the operator is qualified for this operation, that the program version on the CNC matches the released routing and that the raw material lot in the rack belongs to the order. It shows the current work instruction revision.
3. **06:12 to 13:30.** The machine reports cycles and states (running, idle, alarm) over OPC UA or MTConnect. The MES counts good and rejected parts, records downtime and asks the operator for a reason code when a stop exceeds two minutes. In-process measurements go into SPC charts; an out-of-control point stops the next operation until a technician signs off.
4. **11:45.** A seal lot turns out to be suspect. Because the MES recorded which lot went into which valve serial number, quality can isolate 140 valves instead of a whole week's production.
5. **14:00, shift handover.** The MES reports quantities, scrap, OEE and open issues to the next shift, and posts confirmed quantities and material consumption to ERP.

Without an MES, much of this happens on paper travelers, whiteboards and spreadsheets, typed into ERP hours or days later. That works, up to a point. The cost shows up as late or wrong data, slow recalls, quality escapes and long investigations. The [paperless manufacturing](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/shop-floor-management/paperless-manufacturing/) deep dive (planned) covers the step from paper travelers to digital work instructions, which is often the first MES module a plant uses.

### Where MES sits among the plant systems

ISA-95 places MES at **Level 3**, manufacturing operations management, between business planning and logistics at Level 4 (ERP) and the control layers at Levels 0 to 2 (sensors, PLCs, DCS, SCADA) [1]. Level 3 is not one system. The same layer holds laboratory information management (LIMS), warehouse management (WMS), maintenance management (CMMS) and others; together they are often called manufacturing operations management systems [14]. Section 5 compares them.

### Benefits of an MES, and what the evidence says

Most MES pages list benefits with impressive percentages and no source. Here is what you can state with care:

| Benefit | Mechanism | What to measure | Caveat |
|---|---|---|---|
| Faster, accurate production data | Data captured at the source, not re-typed | Data latency, manual entries per order | Only if master data (routings, BOMs) is correct |
| Shorter cycle times | Less waiting for paperwork, faster release and dispatch | Order lead time, queue time | *Control Engineering* reports that MESA International put the average reduction in manufacturing cycle times at 45 percent; treat it as an association claim, not a benchmark [9] |
| Fewer quality escapes | Enforced checks, SPC, interlocks before the next step | First pass yield, customer complaints | Depends on whether the checks are actually configured as hard stops |
| Faster, narrower recalls | Lot and serial genealogy | Time to identify affected units | Requires disciplined material scanning |
| Higher OEE on the constraint | Visible downtime reasons, faster response | OEE, MTTR | Only worth money if the extra output can be sold |
| Audit readiness | Electronic records, audit trails, e-signatures | Hours per audit, findings | Validation effort in regulated industries is substantial |

A 2006 *Automation World* article reported that more than 40 global manufacturers had used ISA-95 to define their MES requirements, and that the time to define requirements dropped from several months to four to six weeks [10]. That is a modest, plausible effect of a shared vocabulary, and a good reason to read the next sections before writing a requirements document.

## A short history: from AMR and MESA-11 to ISA-95 and beyond

The history explains why vendors, analysts and standards use slightly different words for the same thing.

### Early 1990s: the term MES

According to *Automation World*, the Boston analyst firm AMR Research established the MES concept in 1992 as the layer between enterprise systems and control systems, providing visibility and control [10]. Plants had used shop floor control, data collection and quality systems before, often built in-house for a single purpose; their functions overlapped and they rarely talked to each other [14].

### 1997: MESA-11

MESA International (Manufacturing Enterprise Solutions Association) published its white paper on MES in 1997 [15] and with it the functional model known as **MESA-11** [12]. The eleven functions were [9]:

1. Resource allocation and status
2. Operations/detailed scheduling
3. Dispatching production units
4. Document control
5. Data acquisition
6. Labor management
7. Quality management
8. Process management
9. Maintenance management
10. Product tracking and genealogy
11. Performance analysis

MESA's 1997 definition stressed that an MES should "deliver information that enables the optimization of production activities," a wording *Automation World* still called practical in 2006 [10]. Several of the eleven functions (maintenance, labor, document control) are now often handled by separate systems, which is why the model drew criticism as "too broad" even at the time [9].

### 2000 to 2013: ISA-95

The ISA95 committee built on the Purdue Reference Model for computer integrated manufacturing [1]. Part 1 appeared in 2000 and Part 3, with the Level 3 activity models, in 2005; secondary sources describe ISA-95 as having merged MESA's functional view with the Purdue hierarchy [12][14]. Further parts followed until 2020, and Part 1 was republished in 2025 [1][2]. Section 3 lists them.

### 2004 onward: MOM, collaborative MES and later models

Because ISA-95 named Level 3 "manufacturing operations management," the term MOM became common for the broader scope (production plus quality, maintenance and inventory operations), with MES as its production core [10]. MESA itself published several later models; the *Control Engineering* article on the original model notes it was one of eight MESA models at the time [9]. We could not verify the names and years of MESA's later models (often cited as c-MES in 2004 and the MESA model of 2008) from a primary source [unverified]. MESA's own model history page now redirects to ISA, and MESA's 2022 announcement of its latest model only says it had released "three (now four) MESA models" [43].

### 2026: MESA dissolves, ISA takes over its content

MESA International voted to cease operations effective June 30, 2026; ISA states that "MESA's content and educational programs will be assumed by ISA" [11]. This matters in practice because MESA maintained **B2MML**, the XML implementation of ISA-95 used in many ERP-MES interfaces [5]. Check with ISA where B2MML schemas and any future versions are hosted before you specify them in a contract. As of November 2026 the B2MML and BatchML schemas are still published in the MESAInternational repository on GitHub, whose README does not mention the transfer [36] [unverified: future hosting after the transfer is not documented].

### The 2020s: IIoT, cloud and composable MES

Three shifts shape current MES projects: machine data is cheaper to collect (OPC UA, MTConnect, MQTT), cloud and hybrid deployment has become normal, and vendors increasingly sell MES as a platform of smaller apps instead of a monolithic suite ("composable" or "next-generation" MES). Section 9 covers what that means for your architecture and contract.

## ISA-95 explained: the standard, its parts and its levels

ISA describes ISA-95, "also known as ANSI/ISA-95 or IEC 62264," as an international set of standards for integrating logistics systems with manufacturing control systems [1]. It organizes technology and business processes into layers defined by the activities that take place there and describes how an enterprise can build interfaces between those layers. ISA stresses that the architecture, based on the Purdue model, can be applied "regardless of the technology used," and that the equipment hierarchy works for discrete, continuous and logistics industries alike [1].

Two consequences follow. First, ISA-95 is **technology-neutral**: it says nothing about which database, protocol or vendor you use. Second, its main focus is the **interface between Level 3 and Level 4** [1], which is exactly where most MES projects get stuck (who owns the routing, when a quantity is "confirmed," how scrap is posted).

### ISA-95 and IEC 62264: same content, different numbering

ISA develops the series as ANSI/ISA-95; the IEC publishes it as IEC 62264. The 2025 edition of Part 1 carries the designation ANSI/ISA-95.00.01-2025 (IEC 62264-1 Mod), where "Mod" signals a modified adoption [1][2]. The editions are not always in sync. IEC 62264-1 and -2 were published in 2013, Part 3 in 2016, Part 4 in 2015 and Part 5 in 2016 [7]; IEC 62264-6, the messaging service model, appeared as edition 1.0 on June 26, 2020 [6]. When a contract or a requirements document cites "ISA-95," name the part and the edition.

### The eight parts of ISA-95

| Part | Title (ANSI/ISA-95) | Current edition per ISA | Scope in plain words |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Models and Terminology | 2025 (IEC 62264-1 Mod) | Scope of manufacturing operations and control, how physical assets are organized, interface functions, shared information |
| 2 | Objects and Attributes for Enterprise-Control System Integration | 2018 | The objects and attributes exchanged at the Level 3 / Level 4 interface |
| 3 | Activity Models of Manufacturing Operations Management | 2013 | The activities inside Level 3 and some of the data they exchange |
| 4 | Objects and Attributes for Manufacturing Operations Management Integration | 2018 | Object models for information flowing between Level 3 activities, independent of the degree of automation |
| 5 | Business-to-Manufacturing Transactions | 2018 | Transactions between Levels 3 and 4 (verbs such as get, process, sync) |
| 6 | Messaging Service Model | 2014 | Messaging across Levels 3 and 4 and within Level 3 |
| 7 | Alias Service Model | 2017 | Mapping of equivalent identifiers between manufacturing operations and other domains |
| 8 | Information Exchange Profiles | 2020 | Profiles for specific interfaces between Levels 4 and 3 or between Level 3 activities |

Source: ISA's ISA-95 page [1]. Note that ISA lists 2014 for Part 6 while the IEC edition of Part 6 is from 2020 [6]. 
For most MES projects, Parts 1 to 3 do the heavy lifting: they give you the vocabulary, the level model and the activity model you need for a requirements document. Parts 2, 4 and 5 matter when you design interfaces and data models. Parts 6 to 8 matter mainly to integration architects and software vendors.

### What changed in the 2025 edition of Part 1

ISA announced the new Part 1 on April 10, 2025. It updates the 2010 version, reflects specific enterprise functions, clarifies the boundary between the enterprise and the manufacturing/control domains and improves consistency with the other parts [2]. The chair of the ISA95 committee, Christian Monchinski, tied it to "increasingly modular architectures across business and manufacturing operations" and a shift to data-centric designs, and said the update "reaffirms the importance of standardized integration interfaces" [2]. For practitioners this is a signal: ISA-95 is meant to stay the shared vocabulary for modular, event-driven architectures, not to be replaced by them.

### The five ISA-95 levels

ISA-95 inherits its level model from Purdue. The levels describe *functions*, not computers or network segments [1][4].

| Level | Name | What happens here (our summary) | Typical systems | Typical time frame |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 4 | Business planning and logistics | Orders, material requirements, inventory, shipping, costing for the plant | ERP, supply chain planning | Months, weeks, days [4] |
| 3 | Manufacturing operations management | Workflow and recipe control to produce the end products; detailed scheduling, dispatching, records, quality, maintenance and inventory operations | MES, LIMS, WMS, CMMS, QMS, historian, APS (detailed scheduling) | Days, shifts, hours, minutes, seconds [4] |
| 2 | Monitoring and supervisory control | Monitoring and controlling the physical process | SCADA, HMI, DCS, PLC | Real-time responses measured in sub-seconds [4] |
| 1 | Sensing and manipulating | Sensors, actuators, smart devices, valves | Field devices | Not specified in the sources used here |
| 0 | Physical process | The machinery and the material being transformed | Machines, lines, process equipment | Not applicable |

Two remarks. ISA's own page places SCADA in the Level 3 row as an example of a Level 3 system next to MES [1], while the OPC Foundation's summary assigns automation and control to Levels 1 and 2 and MOM to Level 3 [4]. In practice SCADA spans the 2/3 boundary: its screens and alarm handling are supervisory control (Level 2), its historian and reporting functions often serve Level 3. Do not fight about the box; decide which system is the system of record for each piece of data. Second, the OPC Foundation stresses that "each level has its own definition for real-time" [4]. An MES that updates a dashboard every 30 seconds is real-time for a supervisor and far too slow for a machine interlock.

### Levels are not network zones

A common mistake is to treat ISA-95 levels and the Purdue levels used in OT security as the same thing. ISA-95 describes functions and information; network segmentation follows a different logic, now set out in ISA/IEC 62443 with zones and conduits [23]. ISA notes that ISA-95 relies on the Purdue reference model to describe network segmentation in industrial control systems [1], but a Level 3 *function* can run in a cloud tenant, while a Level 3 *server* in your plant sits in a specific security zone. The [OT cybersecurity and ISA/IEC 62443](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/ot-cybersecurity-iec-62443/) guide (planned) covers zones, conduits and the DMZ; NIST SP 800-82 Rev. 3 is the US guide to OT security [24].

### Is ISA-95 still relevant?

ISA's own answer: the levels still help describe system boundaries for Industry 4.0 and smart manufacturing, even though the model originated as a hierarchy [1]. That matches practice. The hierarchy as a *data path* (every value climbs level by level through point-to-point interfaces) has aged badly, and section 7 shows the alternatives. The hierarchy as a *map of responsibilities and vocabulary* has not.

## The ISA-95 models that matter for an MES project

ISA-95 contains several models. Four of them are worth knowing by name before you write an MES requirements document.

### 1. The functional hierarchy (levels)

Covered above. Use it to draw a system context diagram with every existing system placed at its level and every interface as a labeled arrow.

### 2. The equipment hierarchy

ISA-95 organizes the physical plant into a role-based equipment hierarchy: **enterprise, site, area, work center, work unit** (the names used by the ranking explainers and by the sister guide on Azure) [1][27]. Work centers are further typed, for example as process cells (batch), production units (continuous), production lines (discrete) and storage zones, with matching work units such as units, work cells and storage units. The OPC Foundation's ISA-95 information model lists exactly these level names [32], and the public 1999 draft of Part 1 assigns process cells and units to batch, production units to continuous and production lines and work cells to discrete manufacturing [33]. The OPC Foundation's mapping notes that equipment can be nested and that the model describes the *roles* equipment plays in production, separate from the physical assets that make it up [5].

Why it matters: the equipment hierarchy becomes your naming convention in the MES, the historian, the CMMS, the unified namespace topic tree and the data platform. A plant that names the same machine "L2-CNC3", "Line 2 Mazak" and "WC-4711" in three systems pays for that in every report and every integration.

**Example hierarchy for the valve plant:**

| Level | Example |
|---|---|
| Enterprise | Acme Fluid Power |
| Site | Dayton plant |
| Area | Machining; Assembly and test; Warehouse |
| Work center | Line 2 (production line); Test cell T1 (work cell group); Raw material store (storage zone) |
| Work unit | CNC 3; Wash station; Leak tester LT-1; Rack zone A |

### 3. The object models (Part 2 and Part 4)

ISA-95 describes the information exchanged between ERP and MES, and between MES activities, as object models with attributes. The OPC Foundation's companion specification names four resource models that ERP and MOM systems exchange: **material** (including material properties and lots), **equipment** (as it pertains to the operations performed), **physical assets** (the assets that make up the equipment) and **personnel** (roles and qualifications) [4][5]. Around them, ISA-95 organizes the exchange into four information categories that mirror the life of an order: **product definition** (how to make it), **production capability** (what resources are available), **production schedule** (what to make, when) and **production performance** (what was made). The categories match the Part 3 activity names [3]; the public 1999 draft of Part 1 groups schedule and performance as "production information" [33].

A practical rule from this model: ERP owns the "what and when," the MES owns the "how and what actually happened." When ERP holds a routing with operation times and the MES holds a more detailed work definition with parameters and instructions, decide explicitly which one is master for which attribute, and how changes flow.

### 4. The activity model (Part 3)

This is the most useful and least explained part of ISA-95. Part 3 divides manufacturing operations management into four categories: **production operations, maintenance operations, quality test operations and inventory operations**. Within each, it uses the same eight generic activities [3]:

| Generic activity | Production operations | Maintenance operations | Quality test operations | Inventory operations |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Definition management | Product definition management (routings, recipes, work instructions) | Maintenance definition management (task lists, procedures) | Quality test definition management (test specs, sampling plans) | Inventory definition management (storage and transfer rules) |
| Resource management | Production resource management (people, equipment, material availability and qualification) | Maintenance resource management (technicians, spare parts, tools) | Quality test resource management (lab equipment, analysts) | Inventory resource management (storage locations, handling equipment) |
| Detailed scheduling | Detailed production scheduling (sequence on resources) | Detailed maintenance scheduling | Detailed quality test scheduling | Detailed inventory scheduling (moves, replenishment) |
| Dispatching | Production dispatching (release work to stations) | Maintenance dispatching (work orders to technicians) | Quality test dispatching (samples to lab) | Inventory dispatching (transfer orders) |
| Execution management | Production execution management (guide and verify execution) | Maintenance execution management | Quality test execution management | Inventory execution management |
| Data collection | Production data collection (counts, times, parameters) | Maintenance data collection (findings, hours, parts) | Quality test data collection (results) | Inventory data collection (movements, counts) |
| Tracking | Production tracking (WIP, genealogy, reporting to ERP) | Maintenance tracking (history per asset) | Quality test tracking (results per lot or serial) | Inventory tracking (location and status of material) |
| Performance analysis | Production performance analysis (OEE, yield, cycle time) | Maintenance performance analysis (MTBF, MTTR) | Quality test performance analysis (capability, trends) | Inventory performance analysis (turns, accuracy) |

Row and column names follow the clause titles of ANSI/ISA-95.00.03-2013 (clauses 6 to 9) [3]; the examples in parentheses are ours. *Automation World* summarized the same split as "production, maintenance, quality and inventory" [13].

**How to use the matrix in a project:** Print it as a 4 × 8 grid. For each of the 32 cells, write which system does this today (paper, Excel, ERP, MES, CMMS, LIMS, WMS, nobody) and which system should do it after the project. The empty cells and the cells with three systems are your scope discussion. A typical result for a discrete plant: production column mostly MES, maintenance column in the CMMS, quality test split between MES (in-process checks) and LIMS or QMS (lab tests, CAPA), inventory split between ERP (stock levels) and WMS or MES (moves to the line). Detailed scheduling in the production column often goes to an APS (see [production scheduling and APS](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/production-scheduling-aps/), planned).

### A note on ISA-88 and VDI 5600

Two related frameworks show up in MES projects. **ISA-88** is the batch control standard; its procedural control model (procedures, unit procedures, operations, phases) was adopted by the IEC as IEC 61512-1 [34] and is the usual way to structure recipes in pharma, food and chemicals. BatchML, the companion of B2MML, implements ISA-88 [36], and many MES products implement electronic batch records on top of it [unverified: practice observation]. **VDI 5600**, a guideline of the Association of German Engineers (Part 1 dated October 2016), lists MES tasks in a way similar to MESA-11 and is common in German plants and RFPs from German parent companies [21].

## MES vs. ERP vs. MOM vs. SCADA and other plant systems

"What is the difference between MES and ERP?" is the most common MES question on google.com, followed by MES vs. SCADA. The short version: ERP plans and accounts, MES executes and records, SCADA supervises and controls machines. The longer version needs a table, because the boundaries depend on industry and on the products you already own.

### Comparison table

| Dimension | ERP (Level 4) | MES (Level 3) | MOM (Level 3, broader) | SCADA / HMI (Level 2, partly 3) | Historian |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Main question | What should we make, buy and ship, and what did it cost? | How exactly is this order made, and what actually happened? | How do production, quality, maintenance and inventory operations work together? | What is the machine or process doing right now, and how do I control it? | What were the process values over time? |
| Time frame | Months, weeks, days [4] | Shifts, hours, minutes, seconds [4] | Same as MES | Sub-seconds to seconds [4] | Seconds to years |
| Unit of work | Production order, purchase order, sales order | Operation, step, lot, serial number | Order plus test, maintenance work order, transfer order | Tag, alarm, setpoint | Time series tag |
| Typical data | BOM, routing, standard cost, stock, demand | Work instructions, actual times and quantities, parameters, genealogy, defects, sign-offs | MES data plus lab results, maintenance history, material moves | Live values, alarms, operator commands | Compressed time series |
| Users | Planning, purchasing, finance, sales | Operators, supervisors, quality, process engineers | Plant management, all operations functions | Control room operators, maintenance technicians | Engineers, analysts |
| Record of truth for | Financial postings, inventory value, demand | As-built record, execution history | Operations records across categories | Control states and alarms | Process history |
| Typical failure if misused | Shop floor confirmations typed in days late | Used as a planning tool or a data dump without process logic | Scope so broad that nothing goes live | Used as an order management system | Treated as the system of record for quantities |

MOM is the ISA-95 name for the whole of Level 3; MES is usually understood as its production core [10][14]. Many vendors sell "MOM suites" that include quality and maintenance modules. Whether you call it MES or MOM matters less than whether the activity matrix from section 4 has one owner per cell.

### Other Level 3 systems and where the MES boundary usually runs

| System | Core job | Typical boundary with MES |
|---|---|---|
| QMS (quality management system) | Nonconformance, CAPA, audits, document control, supplier quality | MES captures in-process results and holds; QMS manages the investigation and corrective action. Integration: nonconformance created in MES, worked in QMS |
| LIMS (laboratory information management) | Samples, lab tests, specifications, certificates of analysis | MES requests samples and receives release decisions; LIMS owns the test record |
| CMMS / EAM | Maintenance work orders, spare parts, asset history | MES reports machine states and counters; CMMS schedules maintenance. Shared asset IDs are essential |
| WMS | Storage, picking, warehouse moves | MES requests material to the line and confirms consumption; WMS executes the moves |
| APS | Finite capacity scheduling and sequencing | APS produces the sequence; MES dispatches and reports back actuals. Some MES products include an APS module |
| PLM | Product design, engineering BOM, change management | PLM releases the engineering definition; MES consumes the manufacturing definition (often via ERP or a manufacturing process planning tool) |
| Data platform / analytics | Cross-plant analysis, AI, long-term storage | MES is a source; it should not become the enterprise data lake. See [Manufacturing IT with Azure](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/manufacturing-it-azure/) (planned) |

### Is SAP an MES? Is ERP enough?

ERP vendors offer manufacturing execution functions, either inside ERP (shop floor confirmations, simple dispatch lists) or as separate products. SAP, for example, describes SAP Digital Manufacturing as a cloud-based manufacturing operations management (MOM) platform and tells customers of its older SAP Manufacturing Execution to move to it (vendor description) [35]. Whether ERP alone is enough depends on four questions:

1. **Granularity:** Do you need to record per operation and order, or per step, serial number and parameter?
2. **Latency:** Is end-of-shift confirmation good enough, or do decisions depend on minutes?
3. **Enforcement:** Must the system *prevent* a wrong step (wrong material, unqualified operator, missing check), or only record it?
4. **Regulation:** Do you need electronic batch records or device history records with audit trails and e-signatures?

**Signals that you have outgrown ERP-only execution:** confirmations typed in more than a shift late; more than one shadow spreadsheet per area; a customer or regulator asking for traceability you can only assemble in days; quality escapes caused by wrong revisions or materials; supervisors spending the first hour of each shift collecting numbers.

If all four answers are "coarse, end of shift, record only, no," ERP shop floor control plus good discipline can be enough, especially for small plants. If two or more answers point the other way, you need MES functions, whether from the ERP vendor, an MES vendor or a platform.

## MES functions in detail

The functions below follow the production column of the ISA-95 activity model [3], extended by the MESA-11 functions that most products still cover [9]. For each: what it does, what it needs and how it typically fails.

### 1. Product definition management (work definitions, routings, recipes)

**What it does:** Holds the manufacturing definition of each product: operations, steps, parameters, required materials, tools, skills, work instructions and checks. In batch industries this is the master recipe.

**What it needs:** A clear master data split with ERP and PLM, version control and an engineering change process that reaches the floor before the next order starts.

**Typical failure:** Two masters. ERP routing says 12 operations, the MES says 14, and nobody knows which times are used for costing.

### 2. Resource management (people, equipment, material)

**What it does:** Knows which resources are available and qualified: machine status, tool life, operator training and certification, material lots on hand at the line.

**What it needs:** Training records (often from HR or a learning system), equipment status from the CMMS and controls, material status from ERP or WMS.

**Typical failure:** The MES blocks an operator because the training record in HR is three months behind. Fix the process for qualification updates before you turn on hard enforcement. The [manufacturing workforce skills](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/operational-excellence/manufacturing-workforce-skills/) deep dive (planned) covers skills matrices.

### 3. Detailed scheduling

**What it does:** Turns released orders into a sequence per resource, respecting capacities, changeovers, material availability and due dates. MESA-11 calls it operations/detailed scheduling [9].

**What it needs:** Accurate run and changeover times, real resource calendars and actual progress from the floor.

**Typical failure:** A "schedule" that is really the ERP dispatch list sorted by due date. If scheduling is a real constraint in your plant, compare the MES module with a stand-alone APS.

### 4. Dispatching

**What it does:** Releases the next job to a work center or operator at the right moment, with all documents and materials ready. MESA-11: dispatching production units [9].

**Typical failure:** Dispatch lists that operators ignore because they do not reflect reality (missing material, machine down). The fix is closing the loop: dispatching must use live resource status.

### 5. Execution management and work instructions

**What it does:** Guides the operator or the machine through the steps, enforces sequence and checks, collects sign-offs, and stops the process when a required condition is not met. This is where an MES differs most from a reporting tool.

**What it needs:** Well-written, visual work instructions; decisions on which checks are hard stops and which are warnings; terminals or tablets at the station.

**Safety boundary:** Safety functions (guarding, emergency stops, safety interlocks) belong in safety-rated controls at Levels 1 and 2, never in the MES. The MES may block the *next order step*; it must not be the only thing that keeps a person safe.

**Typical failure:** Digitizing a 40-page paper procedure one to one. Rewrite instructions for the screen, test them with operators and keep them short. See [standard work](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/shop-floor-management/standard-work/) (planned) and [paperless manufacturing](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/shop-floor-management/paperless-manufacturing/) (planned).

### 6. Data collection (data acquisition)

**What it does:** Captures quantities, times, states, parameters and measurements, automatically from machines where possible and manually where not.

**What it needs:** Machine connectivity (OPC UA, MTConnect, vendor protocols, or simple I/O for counters and states), a time model (what counts as planned downtime) and reason code lists that operators can actually use.

**Typical failure:** 120 downtime reason codes. Operators pick the first one in the list. Start with eight to fifteen codes per area and refine them with the people who use them.

### 7. Quality management in execution

**What it does:** In-process inspections, SPC, sampling plans, holds and releases, nonconformance capture, links to the QMS for investigation.

**What it needs:** Control plans and characteristics from quality engineering (in automotive, the PFMEA and control plan), gauge integration, rules for what happens on a failed check.

**Typical failure:** Inspection results collected but never used to stop the process. A check that cannot stop the line is a record, not a control. Root cause work belongs in the quality system; see [Six Sigma and root cause analysis](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/six-sigma-root-cause-analysis/) (planned).

### 8. Tracking, traceability and genealogy

**What it does:** Tracks work in process and builds the as-built record: which material lots and components, from which suppliers, went into which serial numbers or batches, processed on which equipment with which parameters by whom. MESA-11: product tracking and genealogy [9].

**What it needs:** Unique identification (barcodes, 2D codes, RFID), disciplined scanning at every material consumption point, a clear rule for bulk materials (lot-level vs. serial-level).

**Typical failure:** Genealogy reconstructed after the fact from shipping documents. It must be captured in the process. A simple test: pick a random finished serial number and ask how long it takes to list all component lots. Then reverse it: pick a component lot and list all finished units that contain it.

### 9. Performance analysis

**What it does:** OEE, yield, scrap, cycle time, schedule adherence and downtime analysis by shift, line and product; the input for daily shop floor meetings. Section 14 covers the KPIs.

**Typical failure:** Dashboards no one discusses. Tie each KPI to a meeting and an owner, as described in [shop floor management](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/shop-floor-management/) (planned).

### 10. Document control

**What it does:** Delivers the current revision of drawings, instructions and specifications to the station and records which revision was used. Often shared with or delegated to a document management system or the QMS.

### 11. Labor management

**What it does:** Records who worked on which order for how long, for costing, qualification checks and payroll interfaces. Note the people side in section 15: labor tracking at operation level is sensitive and in unionized plants may be subject to bargaining.

### 12. Maintenance-related functions

**What it does:** Machine states, counters and alarms from the MES feed condition-based or usage-based maintenance in the CMMS. Full maintenance management usually stays in the CMMS. See [TPM and OEE](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/tpm-oee/) (planned) and [predictive maintenance and IIoT](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/predictive-maintenance-iiot/) (planned).

### 13. Inventory and material flow at the line

**What it does:** Requests material to the line, confirms consumption, tracks WIP buffers and line-side stock. Kanban signals can be triggered from MES consumption data; see [kanban and pull systems](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/kanban-pull-system/) (planned) and [intralogistics](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/production-scheduling-aps/intralogistics/) (planned).

### Discrete, process and batch: how the emphasis shifts

| Industry type | Typical MES emphasis | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Discrete, high volume (automotive, electronics) | Serial genealogy, interlocks, machine data, OEE, Andon | Body shop, PCB assembly |
| Discrete, high mix, low volume (machinery, aerospace) | Work instructions, travelers, nonconformance, operator qualification, as-built configuration | Assembly of machine tools, aircraft structures |
| Batch (pharma, food, specialty chemicals) | Electronic batch records, recipe execution, weigh and dispense, review by exception, compliance | Tablet manufacturing, sauces |
| Continuous (paper, steel, refining) | Production tracking by time and lot, quality by sample, energy, integration with DCS and LIMS | Paper machine, hot strip mill |

For aerospace specifics (AS9100, first article inspection, configuration control), see [aerospace production planning](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/aerospace-production-planning/) (planned).

## Integration: B2MML, OPC UA, MTConnect and the unified namespace

An MES is only as good as its interfaces. It needs orders and master data from above, machine data from below, and it must hand confirmed results back. Four building blocks recur.

### Upward: ERP integration and B2MML

The ERP-MES interface carries a small number of message types, which map neatly onto ISA-95's information categories:

| Direction | Message | Content | Trigger |
|---|---|---|---|
| ERP -> MES | Product definition / routing | Operations, BOM, work centers, standard times | Engineering change release |
| ERP -> MES | Production order (schedule) | Material, quantity, due date, routing reference | Order release |
| ERP -> MES | Material master, lots | Material IDs, batch characteristics | Change, receipt |
| MES -> ERP | Operation confirmation (performance) | Good and scrap quantities, times, labor | Operation end or periodic |
| MES -> ERP | Goods movements | Consumption, goods receipt from production | Consumption scan, order completion |
| MES -> ERP | Quality notification | Defects, holds | Nonconformance |
| MES <-> ERP | Capability / status | Resource availability, order status | Status change |

**B2MML** (Business To Manufacturing Markup Language) is MESA's XML implementation of the ISA-95 object models; the OPC Foundation's ISA-95 companion specification follows the B2MML mapping where possible to make translation easier [5]. Using B2MML or a JSON equivalent does not make an interface plug-and-play, but it gives both sides the same object names and structures, which shortens specification work. With MESA's dissolution, confirm where the schemas are maintained (see section 2) [11].

**Rules that save months:**

- Define for each attribute which system is master, and never let two systems write the same field.
- Agree on the *event* that triggers a confirmation (operation end, every 15 minutes, order end), not only the format.
- Make interfaces idempotent: a message sent twice must not post quantities twice.
- Plan for the ERP being unavailable. The MES must keep running and queue postings.

### Downward: machine and control integration with OPC UA and MTConnect

**OPC UA** is the dominant vendor-neutral interface between controls and Level 3 software. The OPC Foundation maintains companion specifications that define information models for specific domains, including OPC 10030, which maps the ISA-95 common object model into OPC UA [4][5]. In Microsoft's Azure IoT Operations, for example, the OPC UA connector reads configured data points and publishes them to an MQTT broker [26]; the sister guide [Manufacturing IT with Azure](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/manufacturing-it-azure/) (planned) covers that stack.

**MTConnect** is an open standard for machine tool and manufacturing equipment data, published as ANSI/MTC1.4-2018 [22]. Many US machine tool builders support it natively, which makes it attractive for job shops with CNC equipment.

For packaging lines, the PackML state model (from the ISA-TR88.00.02 technical report, current edition 2022) gives machines a common set of states and modes [37], which simplifies OEE calculation across different OEMs.

**What you need from the machine, at minimum:** a state (running, stopped, faulted, in setup), a good-part counter, a reject counter, the active program or recipe, and alarm codes. Everything else is a bonus for later use cases.

### Sideways and outward: the unified namespace (UNS)

A **unified namespace** is an architecture pattern, not a standard: systems publish their current state as events to a central broker, typically MQTT, organized in a hierarchical topic tree, and other systems subscribe to what they need. The topic tree usually follows the ISA-95 equipment hierarchy. The [Azure pillar](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/manufacturing-it-azure/) (planned) shows a full topic example and the Azure services that implement it; this section focuses on what a UNS means for the MES.

```
acme/dayton/machining/line-2/cnc-3/state
acme/dayton/machining/line-2/cnc-3/counts
acme/dayton/machining/line-2/order/current
acme/dayton/assembly/test-cell-t1/lt-1/result
```

**MQTT** is a lightweight publish/subscribe protocol; current brokers support versions 3.1.1 and 5, as Microsoft documents for the broker in Azure IoT Operations [25]. **Sparkplug**, maintained by the Eclipse Sparkplug Working Group, adds a payload and state convention on top of MQTT; version 3.0 is current. Its "birth and death certificates" tell subscribers whether a device's data is still valid [28]. Sparkplug's own topic structure (group, edge node, device) is not the ISA-95 hierarchy, so plants that use it typically map the hierarchy into the group ID (for example `spBv1.0/Plant1:Area3:Line4:Cell2/NDATA/<edge node>`) or split levels across brokers or a second, ISA-95-structured namespace; a broker vendor describes both patterns and warns that Sparkplug's limited number of topic elements makes the mapping tight [44].

**What a UNS changes for the MES:**

| Without UNS | With UNS |
|---|---|
| MES connects point-to-point to each machine, ERP, QMS, dashboard | MES subscribes to machine and order topics and publishes its own events (order started, operation completed, hold placed) |
| New consumers (a dashboard, an AI model) need a new interface to the MES | New consumers subscribe to existing topics |
| Current state is scattered across systems | Current state is visible in one place |

**What a UNS does not change:**

- **Business logic stays in the MES.** The broker moves messages; it does not enforce that the right material is used, that an operator is qualified or that a batch record is complete.
- **Records of truth stay in systems of record.** A broker holds current state, not an auditable history. Genealogy, batch records and confirmations must be stored transactionally in the MES (and in ERP), with audit trails where regulated.
- **Semantics still need a model.** A topic named `counts` is useless if one machine counts cycles and another counts good parts. ISA-95 objects and OPC UA companion specifications provide the payload semantics.
- **Security gets harder, not easier.** A broker that every system can write to is a high-value target; access control per topic, authenticated clients and segmentation per ISA/IEC 62443 are mandatory [23][24].

**Debate: "UNS replaces ISA-95."** You will read this claim. It confuses two things. ISA's own position is that the levels still help describe system boundaries even when data no longer flows level by level [1], and the 2025 Part 1 edition explicitly targets data-centric, modular architectures [2]. The workable synthesis: ISA-95 for the vocabulary, the equipment hierarchy and the responsibilities; UNS (or another event backbone) for transport; MES and ERP as transactional systems of record.

### Integration architecture: three patterns compared

| Pattern | How it works | Good for | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Point-to-point | Each system has direct interfaces to the others | Small plants with 3 to 5 systems | Up to n × (n − 1) ÷ 2 interfaces: 10 for 5 systems, 45 for 10; brittle upgrades |
| Integration middleware / ESB | A central integration layer transforms and routes messages | ERP-centric enterprises with IT integration teams | Can become a bottleneck owned by a distant team |
| Event backbone / UNS | Publish/subscribe broker with a shared topic and payload convention | Plants with many machines and many data consumers | Needs governance of topics, payloads and access; transactional flows still need guaranteed delivery |

Most plants end up with a mix: a UNS for machine and state data, a guaranteed-delivery integration for ERP transactions.

## MES in regulated industries

In regulated industries, the MES is part of the quality system, and its records are legal records. This section gives the US rules you must design for; it does not replace advice from your regulatory affairs team.

### FDA 21 CFR Part 11: electronic records and signatures

For closed systems, 21 CFR 11.10 requires, among other controls, validation of systems for accuracy, reliability and consistent intended performance; the ability to generate accurate and complete copies of records; protection of records throughout the retention period; limiting system access to authorized individuals; and "secure, computer-generated, time-stamped audit trails" that record the date and time of operator entries and actions that create, modify or delete records, without obscuring previously recorded information [19]. Other paragraphs cover operational and authority checks, device checks, training and written policies.

**What this means for an MES:** user accounts per person (no shared logins at the terminal), audit trails on every regulated record, e-signatures that are linked to the record and show the signer's name, date, time and meaning, and a validation package. Plan for validation work in time and budget; in pharma it is often a large share of the implementation effort [unverified: no public benchmark for the share].

### Pharmaceuticals: electronic batch records

Drug manufacturers must keep batch production and control records for each batch under the current good manufacturing practice regulations in 21 CFR Part 211; section 211.188 states that "batch production and control records shall be prepared for each batch of drug product produced" [38]. An MES with electronic batch records (eBR) enforces the recipe sequence, captures weights and parameters at the source and supports **review by exception**: the QA reviewer checks deviations flagged by the system instead of reading every page. That is one of the strongest MES business cases in any industry, because batch record review time often limits release.

### Medical devices: QMSR and device history records

Since February 2, 2026, FDA's Quality Management System Regulation (21 CFR Part 820) incorporates ISO 13485:2016 by reference, including its identification and traceability requirements [20]. The older term "device history record" (DHR) still appears in many MES product descriptions and plant procedures; FDA's QMSR page does not use the term, so our recommendation is to map your records to the ISO 13485:2016 requirements that the QMSR incorporates, rather than to the old Part 820 section numbers, and to confirm the mapping with regulatory affairs. The MES typically produces the as-built record per lot or serial number: components, operations, inspections, equipment and personnel.

### Food: FSMA 204 traceability

FDA's Food Traceability Rule (FSMA Section 204) requires key data elements for critical tracking events (receiving, transformation, shipping) linked to a traceability lot code, with records provided to FDA within 24 hours of a request; FDA will not enforce it before July 20, 2028 [29]. Transformation events happen in production, so the MES or a traceability module is the natural place to create the lot codes and link inputs to outputs.

### Automotive and aerospace

IATF 16949 and AS9100 add traceability, control plan and first article requirements; customer-specific requirements often go further. In IATF 16949, section 8.5.2.1 supplements ISO 9001's identification and traceability requirements with documented traceability plans based on "the levels of risk or failure severity for employees, customers, and consumers," according to a certification body's comparison matrix [39] (secondary source; the standard is a paid text). Both standards are paid texts; the [aerospace production planning](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/aerospace-production-planning/) guide (planned) covers AS9100 and AS9102 in depth.

### Validation approach

The FDA's final guidance *Computer Software Assurance for Production and Quality Management System Software* (CDRH and CBER, February 2026) describes a risk-based approach to establishing confidence in production and quality system software and to deciding where more rigor is needed [40]. It is written for medical device makers under 21 CFR Part 820; drug manufacturers can use it as orientation, not as their own rule. In practice: classify each MES function by risk, test high-risk functions (e-signatures, interlocks, calculations, genealogy) thoroughly with scripted tests, and use unscripted or vendor-supplied testing for low-risk configuration. Ask vendors for their validation accelerators and their own quality system certification.

## Deployment and architecture options

The market has moved from "one big MES suite per plant" to a wider set of options. They differ in where the software runs and in how it is structured.

### Where it runs: on-premises, cloud, hybrid

| Option | Description | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|---|
| On-premises | MES servers in the plant or the company data center | Runs when the WAN is down; full control; familiar to validation teams | Hardware, patching and upgrades are your job; upgrades often postponed for years |
| Cloud (SaaS) | Vendor runs the MES in its cloud; the plant connects through gateways | Faster start, vendor-managed upgrades, subscription cost | Dependence on WAN; latency for interlocks; data residency; forced upgrade cadence must fit validation |
| Hybrid / edge plus cloud | Time-critical execution on an edge node in the plant, analytics and management in the cloud | Keeps running during outages; scales analytics | Two environments to operate and secure; synchronization logic |

**Rule of thumb:** anything that must stop a wrong step or keep a line running must work when the internet connection is down. Ask every cloud vendor to demonstrate the offline behavior, not to describe it.

### How it is structured: suite, platform, ERP module

| Approach | What you get | Fits | Risks |
|---|---|---|---|
| Industry MES suite | Broad, pre-built functionality for one or several industries, often with regulatory features | Regulated, complex plants; multi-site standardization | Long projects, heavy customizing, vendor lock-in, high license cost |
| Composable / app platform ("next-generation MES") | A platform with low-code tools and connectors; the plant builds or configures apps for specific use cases | Plants that want to start small and expand; high-mix operations; strong internal teams | Many small apps without a shared data model; governance; validation of citizen-built apps |
| ERP-integrated MES | MES module or companion product from the ERP vendor | ERP-centric companies with moderate shop floor complexity | Shop floor usability and machine integration can lag specialist products |
| Custom build | Own software on a general platform | Unique processes, strong software team | You own maintenance forever (section 10) |

"Composable MES" is a vendor and analyst marketing term rather than a standard category. The underlying idea, small functions that share one data model and can be added over time, is sound and matches ISA-95's activity-by-activity view. The risk is the opposite of the monolith: twenty apps that each define "order" and "machine" differently. The ISA-95 equipment hierarchy and object models are the antidote.

### Multi-site templates

For companies with several plants, the question is less "which MES" than "how much standardization." A common approach is a **global template** (data model, core processes, interfaces, KPIs) with local extensions. Keep the template to what must be comparable across sites (KPI definitions per ISO 22400, genealogy, ERP interface); let sites differ where their processes differ.

## Build vs. buy

Most plants should buy (or subscribe) and configure. Building makes sense in fewer cases than engineering teams like to believe, but more than vendors admit.

### Decision factors

| Factor | Points toward buy | Points toward build or platform-build |
|---|---|---|
| Process uniqueness | Processes resemble industry practice | Processes are a real competitive differentiator and change often |
| Regulation | Validated, regulated environment (Part 11, GMP) | Unregulated or lightly regulated |
| Internal capability | No software team, or one fully occupied with ERP | Experienced team that can maintain software for 10+ years |
| Scope | Broad: execution, quality, genealogy, scheduling | Narrow: data collection and OEE, digital work instructions |
| Time | Need results in months | Can invest in a longer build |
| Total cost over 10 years | License plus configuration is lower than a team | Team cost is already sunk and stable |
| Key-person risk | Unacceptable | Mitigated by documentation and team size |

### The hidden cost of building

A custom MES has no license fee, but you pay for requirements, development, testing, documentation, hosting, security patching, upgrades of the underlying platform, support at 3 a.m. and, in regulated industries, validation of every change. The most common failure is not the first version; it is year five, when the original developer has left and the plant runs software nobody dares to change.

### A middle path

Many plants buy a platform or MES for the core (execution, genealogy, ERP interface) and build specific apps or dashboards on top using its APIs or low-code tools. That works when the purchased core owns the data model and the custom parts only read from it or use documented APIs.

- Write the request for proposal around use cases and the activity matrix, not a 600-line feature list. Feature lists get "yes" to every line.

- Let operators and supervisors score usability; let finance score cost; keep the scores separate until the end.
- For a vendor-neutral starting point, analyst reviews (Gartner Peer Insights lists the MES market [8]) and references from peer plants are more useful than vendor rankings published by vendors.

## MES cost and ROI with a worked example

"How much does an MES cost?" has no honest single number. Prices depend on scope, number of lines and users, deployment model, regulation and integration effort, and vendors rarely publish them. Some competitor pages quote ranges without a source; we do not repeat them. Instead, here is a structure for your own total cost of ownership and a worked example with **illustrative assumptions** that you replace with your quotes and baselines.

### Cost structure

| Cost block | One-time | Recurring | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Software | Perpetual license (if any) | Subscription or maintenance (often a percentage of license per year) | Check the metric: site, user, machine, consumption |
| Implementation services | Configuration, interfaces, data migration, project management | Change requests | Usually the largest one-time block |
| Hardware and network | Terminals, tablets, scanners, label printers, edge servers, Wi-Fi, cabling | Replacement cycle | Often underestimated in older plants |
| Machine connectivity | Gateways, PLC programming, retrofits for old machines | Maintenance | Price per machine type, not per machine |
| Internal team | Project team, key users, backfill for their normal jobs | MES support, admin, continuous improvement | Count loaded cost |
| Training | Training design and sessions | New hires, refreshers | |
| Validation (regulated) | Validation plan, protocols, execution | Revalidation after changes and upgrades | |
| Hosting and operations | Setup | Cloud fees or server operations, backups, security monitoring | |

**Capex vs. opex:** Subscription MES is generally opex; perpetual licenses, hardware and some implementation costs can be capitalized depending on your accounting policy. Under US GAAP, ASC 350-40 governs internal-use software. Since ASU 2018-15 (August 2018), implementation costs of a cloud arrangement that is a service contract follow the same capitalization stages, but the capitalized costs are presented like prepaid hosting fees, amortized over the hosting term and shown in the same income statement line as the fee [41]. ASU 2025-06 (September 2025) amends ASC 350-40 again, effective for annual periods beginning after December 15, 2027, with early adoption permitted [42]. Agree the treatment with your auditor. The accounting choice changes EBITDA and the timing of costs, not the economics.

### Benefit structure

| Benefit line | Formula | Data source |
|---|---|---|
| Additional throughput on the constraint | Added productive hours × contribution margin per hour | MES or manual downtime log, finance (only if demand exists) |
| Scrap and rework reduction | Annual scrap and rework cost × expected reduction | Quality cost reports |
| Labor time for paperwork and data entry | Hours saved × loaded rate × share actually redeployed | Time study, interviews |
| WIP and inventory reduction | One-time cash release; carrying cost × reduction per year | Inventory valuation |
| Faster batch or order release | Lead time reduction × value (inventory, on-time delivery) | QA review times, order data |
| Avoided compliance and recall cost | Probability × impact, usually not in the base case | Risk register |

### Worked example: a 220-employee valve plant

**Situation (all figures illustrative):** four lines, two shifts, revenue of $85 million. Line 2 is the constraint. Paper travelers, end-of-shift ERP confirmations, no automatic downtime capture.

**One-time costs (year 0):**

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Implementation services | $450,000 |
| Hardware, network, machine connectivity | $160,000 |
| Internal project team (2.5 FTE for 12 months at $120,000 loaded) | $300,000 |
| Training and backfill | $60,000 |
| **Total one-time** | **$970,000** |

**Recurring costs (from year 1):** subscription $180,000 + internal MES support 1 FTE $120,000 = **$300,000 per year**.

**Benefits at full effect (base case):**

| Benefit | Calculation | Per year |
|---|---|---|
| OEE on Line 2 from 62% to 67% | 4,000 planned hours × 5 points = 200 extra productive hours × $2,000 contribution margin per hour | $400,000 |
| Scrap reduction | $1.2 million scrap cost × 15% | $180,000 |
| Paperwork time | 4,000 hours × $45 loaded × 50% redeployed | $90,000 |
| WIP carrying cost | $3.0 million WIP × 10% reduction × 20% carrying cost rate | $60,000 |
| **Total annual benefit** | | **$730,000** |

Plus a one-time cash release from WIP: $3.0 million × 10% = $300,000 (assumed in year 2).

**Ramp-up:** year 1 delivers 40 percent of the annual benefit (pilot and rollout), years 2 to 5 deliver 100 percent.

**Cash flows (in $ thousands), discount rate 10%:**

| Year | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Base case | −970 | 730 × 0.4 − 300 = −8 | 730 − 300 + 300 = 730 | 430 | 430 | 430 |
| Cumulative | −970 | −978 | −248 | 182 | 612 | 1,042 |

NPV at 10% = −970 − 8/1.1 + 730/1.1² + 430/1.1³ + 430/1.1⁴ + 430/1.1⁵ ≈ **+$510,000**. Simple payback: cumulative cash turns positive during year 3, about 2.6 years after the start of the project (248 ÷ 430 = 0.58 of year 3).

**Conservative case:** the OEE gain is only half (or the extra output cannot all be sold): $200,000 instead of $400,000; scrap reduction 10 percent ($120,000) instead of 15 percent. Annual benefit $470,000, net $170,000 per year.

| Year | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Conservative case | −970 | 470 × 0.4 − 300 = −112 | 470 − 300 + 300 = 470 | 170 | 170 | 170 |
| Cumulative | −970 | −1,082 | −612 | −442 | −272 | −102 |

NPV at 10% ≈ **−$334,000**; no payback within five years.

**Sensitivity:** holding scrap, paperwork and WIP benefits at the base case, the project breaks even (NPV = 0 at 10%) with a throughput benefit of about $243,000 per year, which is about 121 extra productive hours or roughly 3 OEE points on Line 2 instead of 5. If only half the OEE gain materializes and everything else holds, NPV is about −$139,000. The sensitivity to that one assumption is the real finding.

**What the example teaches:**

1. **One line dominates.** The throughput benefit is more than half of the base case. Before you count it, confirm with sales that the plant is capacity-constrained on that line, or use the [theory of constraints](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/theory-of-constraints/) guide (planned) to find the real constraint first.
2. **Recurring cost matters more than the license line suggests.** Internal support is 40 percent of the recurring cost here and is often left out.
3. **Ramp-up kills NPV.** A pilot that drags on for 18 months instead of 12 moves the conservative case further into the red. Speed to the first stable line is a financial variable.
4. **Unquantified benefits are real but separate.** Recall scope reduction, audit readiness and customer requirements can justify an MES on their own in regulated or OEM-driven supply chains. Show them next to the numbers, not inside them.
5. **Track benefits after go-live** against the frozen baseline, with finance, for at least two years.

For the KPI and cost definitions behind these lines, see [manufacturing KPIs](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/manufacturing-kpis/) (planned).

## MES KPIs: what to measure and how

An MES produces the data for most shop floor KPIs. Two groups matter: the operational KPIs the MES calculates, and the KPIs that tell you whether the MES itself works.

### Standard definitions: ISO 22400

ISO 22400-2:2014 defines key performance indicators for manufacturing operations management, with formula, unit, timing and user group for each [16]; secondary sources count 34 KPIs in the standard [18], and a 2017 amendment added energy management KPIs [16]. Using its definitions makes KPIs comparable across lines and plants. NIST researchers published a hierarchical structure of these KPIs with formulas, which is freely available [17].

### Operational KPIs from the MES

| KPI | Formula (simplified) | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| OEE | Availability × performance × quality | Constraint and key equipment |
| Availability | Run time ÷ planned production time | Downtime focus |
| Performance | (Ideal cycle time × total count) ÷ run time | Speed losses, micro-stops |
| Quality (rate) | Good count ÷ total count | Scrap and rework |
| First pass yield | Units passing without rework ÷ units started | Process capability |
| Scrap ratio | Scrap quantity ÷ produced quantity | Material loss |
| Schedule adherence | Orders (or quantity) completed as scheduled ÷ scheduled | Plan stability |
| Throughput | Good units per hour | Capacity |
| WIP | Units or value between first and last operation | Flow |
| Order lead time | Completion time − release time | Customer responsiveness |
| MTBF / MTTR | Operating time ÷ failures; repair time ÷ failures | Maintenance |

**Worked OEE example (one shift on Line 2):** planned production time 450 minutes, unplanned downtime 60 minutes, so run time 390 minutes. Ideal cycle time 1.0 minute, total count 340, good count 323.

- Availability = 390 ÷ 450 = 86.7%
- Performance = (1.0 × 340) ÷ 390 = 87.2%
- Quality = 323 ÷ 340 = 95.0%
- OEE = 0.867 × 0.872 × 0.950 = **71.8%**

Cross-check: good count × ideal cycle time ÷ planned production time = 323 ÷ 450 = 71.8%. If your MES does not pass this check, its time model is inconsistent. Muchiri and Pintelon review the OEE definitions in use and their pitfalls [30]; the [TPM and OEE](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/tpm-oee/) guide (planned) goes deeper.

### KPIs for the MES itself

| KPI | Definition | Target direction |
|---|---|---|
| Data latency | Time from event on the floor to availability in MES / ERP | Down (minutes, not days) |
| Automatic capture rate | Share of quantities and states captured from machines vs. typed | Up |
| Manual correction rate | Corrections per 100 orders | Down |
| Reason code coverage | Share of downtime minutes with a valid reason | Up (above 90% is a common goal) |
| Traceability time | Minutes to produce a complete backward trace for one serial or lot | Down |
| Adoption | Share of orders run fully in the MES (no paper bypass, except the documented outage fallback) | Up (the end state is 100%; persistent bypasses point to a design problem, not an operator problem) |
| Interface error rate | Failed or rejected postings per day | Down |
| Availability of the MES | Uptime during production hours | Up |

## People and the shop floor

An MES changes how operators work every minute of the shift. Plants that treat it as an IT project tend to get workarounds; plants that treat it as a change in work get adoption.

**What operators gain:** current instructions at the station instead of a binder, fewer forms, faster help when something goes wrong (andon calls from the terminal; see the [andon](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/shop-floor-management/andon/) deep dive, planned), and less blame when the system shows that a stop was caused upstream.

**What operators fear, often with reason:** being monitored minute by minute, being blocked by a system that is wrong, and losing judgment to a screen. Address each directly:

- **Monitoring:** Decide and publish what labor data the MES records, who sees it and what it is used for. Use performance data to improve the process, not to rank individuals. In unionized plants, the introduction of electronic monitoring and changes in work rules can be a mandatory subject of bargaining; involve the union early and get labor counsel's view [unverified: legal assessment depends on the contract and current NLRB doctrine].
- **Being blocked by a wrong system:** Give operators a fast, visible way to report a wrong instruction or a wrong block, and fix those reports within days. Every unresolved report teaches the floor to find a workaround.
- **Judgment:** Use hard stops for safety- and quality-critical steps only. For the rest, warn and record.

**Involve operators in design.** Two or three operators per area in the design workshops, operators testing screens before go-live and operators as peer trainers are cheap and effective. Respect for people is a design input, not a change management slide; see [lean manufacturing](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/lean-manufacturing/) (planned) for the principle.

**Ergonomics and safety at terminals:** place terminals where the work is, at standing height, readable in the plant's lighting and usable with gloves. Scanners should not add walking. An MES that adds 30 seconds per operation to a 60-second takt has failed before go-live.

## Common MES implementation mistakes

| # | Mistake | What happens | Better |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Buying features before defining the data model | Interfaces break on mismatched IDs, KPIs differ by system | Equipment hierarchy, master data owners and time model first |
| 2 | Digitizing the current paper process one to one | Same waste, now on a screen | Simplify the process in the value stream walk first |
| 3 | Scope "everything in the MESA-11 list" | Multi-year project, nothing live | One value stream, three to five use cases, live in months |
| 4 | Customizing instead of configuring | Upgrades become projects; vendor support refuses | Accept standard processes where they are not a differentiator |
| 5 | No operator involvement | Workarounds, paper shadow systems | Operators in design, testing and training |
| 6 | ERP and MES both master the same data | Conflicting quantities and times | One master per attribute, documented |
| 7 | Hard stops everywhere | Lines stop for trivial reasons; trust collapses | Hard stops only where safety or quality require them |
| 8 | Ignoring the network and terminals | Slow screens, dropped scans | Site survey and network upgrade in phase 2 |
| 9 | No fallback for outages | Production stops when the MES or WAN fails | Tested paper fallback and offline edge capability |
| 10 | Weak security design | Flat network between office and machines | Zones and conduits per ISA/IEC 62443; least privilege [23][24] |
| 11 | No owner after go-live | MES decays; data quality drops | Plant-owned MES team and a KPI for adoption |
| 12 | Benefits never measured | Next site's business case has no evidence | Frozen baseline, finance tracks benefits for two years |
| 13 | Underestimated validation (regulated) | Delays of months | Risk-based validation planned from phase 1 |
| 14 | Treating the UNS or data platform as the MES | Data everywhere, no enforcement, no records | MES for execution and records; UNS for distribution |

## Limits of MES and ISA-95

Being honest about the limits helps you decide what not to buy.

### Limits of MES

1. **An MES does not fix a broken process.** If routings are wrong, materials are missing or the plant runs without standard work, the MES makes these problems visible, faster. That is useful, but it is not the fix. Lean basics first; see [lean manufacturing](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/lean-manufacturing/) and [5S and visual management](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/5s-visual-management/) (both planned).
2. **Data quality depends on people.** Manual entries, scans and reason codes are only as good as the discipline and the design of the screens.
3. **Rigid systems resist change.** A heavily customized MES can make product introductions and process changes slower, the opposite of what high-mix plants need.
4. **Cost and effort are high for small plants.** For a 30-person job shop, ERP shop floor control, a simple data collection tool and good visual management may deliver most of the value.
5. **Planning is not execution.** An MES with a scheduling module does not replace sales and operations planning or MRP; see [S&OP, MRP and production planning](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/sop-mrp-production-planning/) (planned).
6. **Analytics belong elsewhere at scale.** Cross-plant analytics, AI and long-term history usually belong in a data platform fed by the MES, not inside it. The [digital twin](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/digital-twin-manufacturing/) guide (planned) and the [process mining](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/digital-twin-manufacturing/process-mining-manufacturing/) deep dive (planned) show what the MES data can feed.

### Limits of ISA-95

1. **It is abstract.** ISA-95 is technology-agnostic by design [1]. It tells you what to name and exchange, not how to build it, so two "ISA-95 compliant" products can still be hard to integrate.
2. **The level model is not a data path.** Read literally, the hierarchy suggests data moves level by level through point-to-point interfaces. Modern architectures (UNS, event streaming, cloud) do not work that way; ISA itself now emphasizes the levels as boundaries, not as pipes [1][2].
3. **The standard texts are paid and long.** Many teams work from secondary summaries, which leads to inconsistent interpretations. Buy at least Parts 1 and 3 for the project library.
4. **Compliance is not certified.** There is no formal ISA-95 certification for products in the way ISA/IEC 62443 has product security certification schemes [unverified: no ISA-95 product certification program found]. "ISA-95 compliant" in a brochure is a vendor claim.
5. **Boundaries are drawn differently in practice.** Scheduling, quality and maintenance activities sit in different systems in every plant; the activity model accommodates that, but it does not decide it for you.

## FAQ

### What is a manufacturing execution system in simple terms?

It is the software that tells the shop floor what to do next and records what actually happened, order by order and step by step. Gartner defines it as production-oriented software that manages, monitors and synchronizes the execution of real-time physical processes that turn raw materials into products [8].

### What are the main functions of an MES?

The ISA-95 activity model lists eight generic activities for production operations: product definition management, resource management, detailed scheduling, dispatching, execution management, data collection, tracking and performance analysis [3]. MESA's 1997 model listed 11 functions, adding document control, labor management, quality management, process management and maintenance management [9].

### What are the benefits of an MES?

Production data captured at the source instead of re-typed, shorter order lead times through faster release and dispatch, fewer quality escapes through enforced checks, faster and narrower recalls through lot and serial genealogy, more visible downtime on the constraint, and audit-ready electronic records. Each benefit depends on a condition: correct master data, checks configured as hard stops, disciplined material scanning and output that can actually be sold. Section 1 lists what to measure for each one.

### What is ISA-95 in simple terms?

ISA-95 (IEC 62264) is the international standard for integrating enterprise systems like ERP with manufacturing control systems. It defines levels, an equipment hierarchy, object models and activity models so that IT, OT and the business use the same vocabulary [1].

### What are the five levels of ISA-95?

Level 0 is the physical process, Level 1 sensing and manipulating, Level 2 monitoring and supervisory control, Level 3 manufacturing operations management (MES and related systems) and Level 4 business planning and logistics (ERP) [1].

### Is ISA-95 the same as IEC 62264?

The content is aligned: ISA publishes ANSI/ISA-95, the IEC publishes IEC 62264. Editions and numbering differ; the 2025 ANSI/ISA-95 Part 1 is a modified adoption of IEC 62264-1 [1][2], and IEC 62264-6 appeared in 2020 [6]. Always cite part and edition.

### How many parts does ISA-95 have?

Eight: models and terminology; objects and attributes for enterprise-control integration; activity models of MOM; objects and attributes for MOM integration; business-to-manufacturing transactions; messaging service model; alias service model; information exchange profiles [1].

### What is Level 3 in ISA-95?

Level 3 is manufacturing operations management: the activities that turn plans into products, in time frames of days, shifts, hours, minutes and seconds [4]. It covers production, maintenance, quality test and inventory operations [3]. MES is the most common Level 3 system.

### What is the difference between MES and ERP?

ERP (Level 4) plans what to make and accounts for it in a time frame of months to days. The MES (Level 3) executes and records how each order is actually made, in shifts to seconds [4]. ERP holds the financial record; the MES holds the as-built record. They exchange orders, confirmations and material movements.

### Do I need an MES if I already have an ERP?

Not necessarily. Ask four questions: do you need to record per step, serial number and parameter rather than per operation; do decisions depend on minutes rather than end-of-shift confirmations; must the system prevent a wrong step rather than only record it; and do you need electronic batch or device history records? If all four answers are no, ERP shop floor control can be enough. If two or more are yes, you need MES functions, from the ERP vendor, an MES vendor or a platform (section 5).

### What is the difference between MES and SCADA?

SCADA supervises and controls machines and processes in real time (tags, alarms, setpoints). The MES manages orders, materials, people, quality and records. SCADA knows that a machine is running; the MES knows which order, which material lot and which operator it is running for.

### What is the difference between MES and MOM?

MOM (manufacturing operations management) is the ISA-95 term for all of Level 3, including production, quality, maintenance and inventory operations. MES usually refers to the production core; many "MOM suites" are MES products with extra modules [10].

### What is B2MML?

B2MML is an XML implementation of the ISA-95 object models, created by MESA International and used in many ERP-MES interfaces; OPC UA's ISA-95 mapping follows it where possible [5]. After MESA's dissolution on June 30, 2026, ISA took over MESA's content [11].

### How does ISA-95 integrate with ERP systems?

ISA-95 defines the interface between Level 4 (ERP) and Level 3 (MES and other operations systems). Parts 2 and 4 define the objects exchanged, and Part 5 the transactions. In practice ERP sends product definitions, production orders and material data down; the MES sends operation confirmations, goods movements, quality notifications and status back up. B2MML implements the ISA-95 object models in XML [5]. A shared model shortens specification work, but each project still has to decide which system is master for each field and which event triggers a posting (section 7).

### Does a unified namespace replace ISA-95 or the MES?

No. A UNS is a way to move and share current-state data through a broker. It needs ISA-95 (or a similar model) for naming and semantics, and it needs an MES and ERP for business logic and auditable records.

### How much does an MES cost?

It depends on scope, number of lines, deployment model, integration and regulation, and vendors rarely publish prices. Build your own five-year total cost of ownership with the cost blocks in section 13; in our illustrative 220-employee plant, one-time costs were $970,000 and recurring costs $300,000 per year.

### How long does an MES implementation take?

For one plant with a configured product, plan roughly six to twelve months to a stable pilot and further waves of three to six months [unverified: practitioner planning assumption]. Regulated plants and custom builds take longer.

### Is SAP an MES?

SAP's core ERP has shop floor control functions; SAP also sells separate manufacturing execution products. Whether ERP functions are enough depends on the granularity, latency, enforcement and regulatory needs described in section 5.

### Do small manufacturers need an MES?

Not always. A small job shop can often get most of the value from ERP shop floor confirmations, simple machine monitoring and good visual management. An MES becomes necessary when you need step enforcement, serial genealogy, electronic batch records or near-real-time decisions. NIST's Manufacturing Extension Partnership (MEP), which focuses on small and medium-sized manufacturers, is a vendor-neutral place to ask for an assessment [31].

### What is a cloud MES or composable MES?

A cloud MES runs as a service in the vendor's cloud; a composable MES is built from smaller apps on a common platform. Both can work; check offline behavior at the edge, the shared data model and, if regulated, how the vendor's release cadence fits your validation.

### Is ISA-95 still relevant with IIoT and cloud platforms?

Yes. ISA states that the levels still help describe system boundaries for Industry 4.0 [1], and the 2025 Part 1 edition was written for modular, data-centric architectures [2]. What has aged is the idea that data must climb the levels one by one.

### What are the downsides of ISA-95?

It is abstract and tells you what to name and exchange, not how to build it. Read literally, its level model suggests data must climb level by level, which modern event-driven architectures do not do. The standard texts are paid and long, so teams often work from inconsistent summaries. "ISA-95 compliant" is a vendor claim, not a certification. And it does not decide where your plant draws the boundary between MES, quality and maintenance systems (section 17).

### What KPIs should an MES track?

OEE and its components, first pass yield, scrap ratio, schedule adherence, throughput, WIP, lead time and MTBF/MTTR, defined per ISO 22400-2 [16][17]. Also track the MES itself: data latency, automatic capture rate, adoption and traceability time.

### What does 21 CFR Part 11 require from an MES?

For closed systems: validation, accurate copies of records, record protection, access limited to authorized individuals and secure, computer-generated, time-stamped audit trails that do not obscure earlier entries, among other controls [19]. E-signatures must be linked to their records.

## Conclusion and first steps

A manufacturing execution system is worth its cost when it closes the loop between plan and reality faster and more reliably than people with paper and spreadsheets can. ISA-95 gives you the map for that: the levels show where MES sits, the equipment hierarchy gives every system the same names, the object models define what ERP and MES exchange, and the activity model's 4 × 8 matrix shows which system should own which job. A unified namespace and cloud platforms change the plumbing, not those responsibilities.

The projects that succeed share a pattern: they start with a value stream and a data model, pilot one line with a frozen baseline, involve operators from the first workshop, keep hard stops for what truly matters, and measure benefits with finance for years. The worked example shows why: the same MES is a good or a bad investment depending on whether the constraint gain can be sold and how fast the pilot stabilizes.

**Your first 30 days:**

- [ ] Walk one value stream and mark every place where information is re-typed, delayed or missing
- [ ] Fill in the ISA-95 activity matrix (4 × 8) for that value stream: who does each activity today?
- [ ] Draft the equipment hierarchy for the pilot area and agree on names with maintenance and IT
- [ ] Freeze a baseline: OEE on the constraint, scrap cost, order lead time, traceability time
- [ ] Ask sales and finance whether extra output on the constraint can be sold, and at what contribution margin
- [ ] List your regulatory record requirements (Part 11, QMSR, FSMA 204, customer requirements)
- [ ] Talk to two peer plants that run an MES, without vendors present
- [ ] Buy ISA-95 Parts 1 and 3 for the project library
