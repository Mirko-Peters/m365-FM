# 5S and Visual Management: Steps, Audits, Examples, and How to Make It Stick

*Updated: November 2026 · Reading time: about 50 minutes (quick start: short answer, "Key takeaways" box, audit template and 30-day checklist)*

> **Short answer:** 5S is a five-step method for organizing a workplace: Sort, Set in order, Shine, Standardize and Sustain, from the Japanese seiri, seiton, seiso, seiketsu and shitsuke. Its purpose is a workplace where needed items are easy to find and abnormalities are obvious at a glance. That makes 5S the usual foundation of visual management, which puts tools, parts, work status and performance "in plain view" so everyone can see whether work is on plan [1][2][9].

> **Key takeaways**
> - 5S stands for Sort, Set in order, Shine, Standardize, Sustain. ASQ, the EPA and the Lean Enterprise Institute (LEI) describe it as the basis for a workplace suited to visual control and lean production [1][2][3].
> - The first English-language books on 5S appeared in the early 1990s: Takashi Osada (1991) and Hiroyuki Hirano's *5 Pillars of the Visual Workplace* (1995) [6][7][8].
> - Shine is inspection, not just cleaning. Cleaning machines counts as servicing under OSHA's lockout/tagout standard unless the minor-servicing exception applies [22].
> - OSHA does not prescribe a full floor-marking color scheme. It requires marked permanent aisles, red and yellow for specific hazards, and clear exit routes; an archived OSHA letter accepts aisle lines of any color at 2 inches or wider [14][15][17].
> - Standardize and Sustain are where most 5S programs fail. ASQ warns that without formalizing them, the initiative is "doomed to failure" [2].
> - Measure 5S with a scored audit (for example 25 items, 0 to 4 points) plus outcome metrics such as search time, red-tag closure, abnormalities found and changeover time.
> - 5S pays off through what it enables (faster changeovers, fewer defects, safer aisles, earlier detection of leaks), not through tidiness itself. Our worked example shows a negative first year if you count search time alone.
> - Visual management goes beyond 5S: hour-by-hour boards, kanban, andon and team boards turn an orderly workplace into one that shows deviations in real time [9][23][26].

## What is 5S? Definition and meaning

[5S](https://www.m365.fm/entity/5s/) is a method for creating and keeping a clean, orderly and safe workplace. ASQ defines it as a methodology that results in a workplace that is "clean, uncluttered, safe, and well organized" to reduce waste and improve productivity, and describes it as the basis for a workplace "suited for visual control and lean production" [2]. The EPA calls 5S "a system to reduce waste and optimize productivity through maintaining an orderly workplace" and notes that it is usually the first lean method a company introduces [3]. The LEI lexicon lists the same five steps [1]. Put simply, the 5S methodology (you will also see "5S system" or "5S method") is lean manufacturing's basic tool for workplace organization: a place for everything, and everything in its place.

The name comes from five Japanese words that all start with "S" in romanized form. English translations were chosen to keep the alliteration, which is why you will see several versions.

| Japanese | Common English term | Alternative English terms | What it means in practice |
|---|---|---|---|
| Seiri | Sort | Sift, Clear out | Separate what is needed from what is not, and remove what is not needed |
| Seiton | Set in order | Straighten, Simplify | Give every needed item a fixed, marked place that fits how often it is used |
| Seiso | Shine | Sweep, Scrub | Clean the area and equipment, and inspect while cleaning |
| Seiketsu | Standardize | Systematize | Make the first three steps routine through standards, visuals and assigned duties |
| Shitsuke | Sustain | Self-discipline | Build the habits, checks and leadership routines that keep the standard alive |

ASQ uses "sort, set in order, shine, standardize, sustain" [2]. The Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) uses "sort, straighten, scrub, standardize, sustain" in its health care workflow toolkit [4]. Vorne's leanproduction.com uses "Straighten" for seiton [8]. The words differ; the sequence and intent do not.

### The core idea in one sentence

5S makes the normal condition of a workplace so clear that any abnormality, such as a missing gauge, an oil puddle, an extra pallet or a box in an aisle, is visible to anyone within seconds. Tidiness is a side effect. The goal is a workplace that tells you what is wrong without anyone having to ask.

### Why 5S matters: benefits of 5S with evidence

Competitor pages list many 5S benefits, usually without sources, often as general promises to improve efficiency or lift the bottom line. The benefits below are the ones primary and peer-reviewed sources actually describe:

| Benefit | What the source says | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Less waste, higher productivity | 5S reduces waste and improves productivity by maintaining an orderly workplace | ASQ [2], EPA [3] |
| Earlier detection of equipment problems | A clean area makes it easier to spot leaks, vibration, breakage and misalignment before they cause failures | EPA [3] |
| Fewer errors, faster response, better morale | Listed as strengths of 5S in AHRQ's health care toolkit, together with improved workplace safety | AHRQ [4] |
| Environmental benefits | Easier spill and leak detection, fewer accidents that cause spills, less expired chemical stock, less floor space to heat and light | EPA [3] |
| Contribution to quality, safety and space use | A literature review of 5S research highlights contributions to production, quality, safety and effective use of workspace | Randhawa and Ahuja (2017) [12] |
| Foundation for other lean methods | Hirano argues that a company that cannot implement 5S has "little hope" of integrating larger changes such as just-in-time | Hirano (1995) via publisher [7] |

Two cautions belong next to that list. First, most published 5S results are single-company case studies, so the size of the effect varies widely and depends on the starting point. Second, 5S benefits arrive through other processes: a faster changeover, a defect found earlier, a forklift path that stays clear. If you want to see them in the numbers, you have to measure those processes, not just the audit score. The [KPI section](#5s-kpis-how-to-measure-results) shows how.

## 5S, visual management and the visual workplace: how they relate

The terms overlap and are often used interchangeably. They are not the same thing.

| Term | Definition used in this guide | Typical elements |
|---|---|---|
| 5S | Method for organizing a workplace in five steps | Red tags, shadow boards, floor marking, cleaning and inspection plans, 5S audits |
| Visual management | Placing tools, parts, production activities and performance indicators "in plain view" so the status of the system can be understood at a glance by everyone involved (LEI) [9] | 5S, hour-by-hour boards, team boards, kanban, andon, status lights |
| Visual workplace | A workplace designed so that information is built into the physical environment (Hirano's book title uses the term) [7] | Labels, borders, address systems, standard work at the point of use |
| Visual factory | The outcome of 5S done well: ASQ quotes *Quality Progress* that a properly implemented 5S "creates a visual factory that allows for quick determination of workplace status" [2] | All of the above across a whole site |
| Visual control | A visual that does more than inform: it limits what people can do or tells them what to do next (for example a min/max line on a rack or a shaped fixture) | Min/max markings, shaped cutouts, color-coded gauges |

Visual management research is fragmented. Tezel, Koskela and Tzortzopoulos reviewed the production management literature in 2016 and found overlapping terminology and no unified focus. They describe visual management as a close-range communication strategy that presents information so it is easy to process, and they propose a taxonomy of visual tools, an implementation framework for the visual workplace and nine conceptual functions of visual management [10].

Their nine functions are a useful checklist for what a visual should achieve [10]:

| Function (Tezel et al. 2016) | What it means on the floor |
|---|---|
| Process transparency | Flow and performance can be understood at a glance |
| Discipline | Visible standards make the correct procedure the easy one |
| Continuous improvement | Deviations and ideas surface and feed problem solving |
| Job facilitation | Visual aids reduce mental and physical effort in routine tasks |
| On-the-job training | People learn the standard at the point of work |
| Shared ownership and desired image | The area shows what the team stands for, to insiders and visitors |
| Management by facts | Shared, objective data builds trust |
| Simplification | Information is filtered so people are not overloaded |
| Unification and a boundaryless organization | Visuals create awareness across departments |

For practical purposes, think of it as a stack:

1. **5S** creates the physical order: everything has a place, and the place is marked.
2. **Visual controls** make the standard self-explaining: you can see where things go, how many there should be, and which way a valve should point.
3. **Visual performance management** shows whether the work is on plan: hour-by-hour boards, team boards and andon signals.
4. **Daily management** acts on what the visuals show: tier meetings, gemba walks and problem solving, covered in the [Shop Floor Management guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/shop-floor-management/).

Without step 1, the other steps have nothing reliable to show. Without step 4, the visuals are decoration.

## History of 5S: from Japan to the US shop floor

The exact origin of 5S is not well documented, and many web pages fill the gap with confident claims that contradict each other. One ranking page credits Hiroyuki Hirano with developing 5S in one section and Sakichi Toyoda with integrating it into the Toyota Production System in another. This is what the sources support.

### Japanese roots in the Toyota Production System era

5S developed in Japan in the context of just-in-time production and the Toyota Production System. Vorne's history page states that 5S "originated in Japan in conjunction with the Toyota Production System" [8], and the Wikipedia article describes it as one of the techniques that enabled just-in-time manufacturing, citing Hirano [5]. No primary source we could verify names a single inventor or a founding year. Treat any precise "5S was invented in 19xx by ..." claim with caution.

### The first English-language books (1991 and 1995)

- **Takashi Osada, *The 5S's: Five Keys to a Total Quality Environment*** was published in 1991 by the Asian Productivity Organization in Tokyo and distributed in the US by Quality Resources in White Plains, New York [6]. Vorne calls it "the first English-language book dedicated to explaining the 5S process by name" [8].
- **Hiroyuki Hirano, *5 Pillars of the Visual Workplace: The Sourcebook for 5S Implementation*** followed from Productivity Press in 1995, with 384 pages, case studies, illustrations and more than forty 5S forms [7]. Its title helped tie 5S to the idea of the visual workplace in the US.

Western companies used similar ideas before these books, under names such as "visual workplace" or housekeeping programs. The Wikipedia article mentions a 1986 example from Tennant Company described by Richard Schonberger, while flagging that the claim needs a clearer source [5]. Wikipedia also notes earlier "scientific management" ideas of workplace organization from Alexey Gastev's Central Institute of Labour in Moscow [5]. Both are interesting context; neither is evidence that 5S was invented outside Japan.

### 5S in its Japanese context

Gapp, Fisher and Kobayashi analyzed how Japanese companies that use 5S as a core management approach describe it. They found both visible, technical practices and less visible, philosophical elements for each S, and concluded that in its original context 5S works as an integrated management system and a starting point for lean management, not as a stand-alone housekeeping tool [11]. That finding matters for US plants: copying the visible part (tape, labels, shadow boards) without the management routines is the most common reason 5S fades.

### From 5S to 6S and 7S

"6S", which adds Safety as a sixth S, is widely used in US plants. The LEI notes the practice [1], and the Wikipedia article covers both 6S and 7S variants, the latter adding an element such as "spirit" or oversight [5]. The [safety section](#safety-and-5s-the-6s-question-and-what-osha-requires) discusses whether a separate S helps.

### A translation note: seiketsu

The Wikipedia article points out that seiketsu literally refers to cleanliness and hygiene, so "standardize" is an interpretation rather than a translation [5]. In practice, the English term describes what the step does in Western implementations: turning Sort, Set in order and Shine into a standard routine. ASQ describes seiketsu as performing seiri, seiton and seiso daily to keep the workplace in good condition [2], which bridges both readings.

### Where 5S sits in lean today

5S is often called the foundation of lean, and the EPA describes it as typically the first lean method a company implements [3]. The [Lean Manufacturing guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/lean-manufacturing/) places 5S at the base of the toolbox for the same reason: flow, pull and standard work all assume that materials and tools are where they should be and that abnormalities are visible. NIST's Manufacturing Extension Partnership (MEP) lists 5S among the lean methods its centers support [28], and the Tennessee MEP, for example, presents 5S as the first step toward a full lean transformation for its manufacturers [27].

## The 5S steps in detail

The five steps build on each other. You cannot set in order what you have not sorted, and you cannot standardize a condition you have not created. The EPA says explicitly that Set in order can only start after Sort [3]. Each step below has the same structure: purpose, how to do it, tools, a worked example and the mistakes to avoid.

The worked examples follow one fictional but realistic pilot area throughout this guide: **Cell 4**, a CNC machining cell in a midsize US job shop with three machines, one deburr bench and one inspection table on about 2,400 sq ft (about 223 m²), running two shifts with six operators per shift. All numbers in the Cell 4 examples are illustrative and labeled as such.

### Step 1: Sort (seiri)

**Purpose:** remove the unnecessary items the work area does not need for the current work, so that what remains can be organized and abnormalities can be seen.

**How to do it:**

1. Define the area and its boundaries on a map. Include walls, the tops of cabinets, the space under benches and drawers.
2. Agree on the decision rule before anyone touches anything (see the frequency table below).
3. Walk the area as a team, operators from every shift included (second and third shift use the area too, and their items are the ones most often tagged by mistake), and tag every item whose need is unclear with a red tag. The EPA describes the practice: unneeded, misplaced or excess items get red tags and move to a central holding area, where they are disposed of, recycled or reassigned [3].
4. Move tagged items to a marked red-tag holding area with a fixed review date, typically 30 days in practice.
5. Review each item with the owner of the decision (supervisor, maintenance, engineering, finance for inventory) and record the outcome.
6. Dispose of items properly. Involve EHS before discarding chemicals, coolants, aerosols, batteries or unlabeled containers. The EPA warns that the disposal wave after a 5S sort creates a short-term waste surge and that unlabeled wastes may be hazardous [3].

**Decision rule by frequency of use (common practice, adapt to your area):**

| Frequency of use | Decision | Where it goes |
|---|---|---|
| Every cycle or every hour | Keep | At the point of use, within reach |
| Daily | Keep | In the work area, within a few steps |
| Weekly | Keep, but not at the workstation | Area storage, cabinet or cart |
| Monthly or quarterly | Move out | Central storage, tool crib or stores |
| Less than once a year or never | Red tag | Holding area; return, transfer, sell, recycle or scrap |
| Unknown | Red tag | Holding area; decision by owner within the review period |

**Red-tag template (fields):**

| Field | Example entry |
|---|---|
| Tag number | C4-0087 |
| Date tagged / tagged by | October 6 / J. Ramirez, operator, 1st shift |
| Item description | Fixture plate, aluminum, part family 2200 (obsolete) |
| Category | Tooling / fixture / gauge / material / WIP / equipment / supplies / documents / other |
| Quantity and location found | 1, under machine 2 |
| Reason tagged | Not needed / defective / excess quantity / unknown owner / misplaced |
| Estimated value (if known) | Ask finance for book value of inventory items |
| Proposed disposition | Return to stores / transfer / sell / recycle / scrap / keep |
| Decision owner and due date | Manufacturing engineer, November 5 |
| Final disposition and date | Scrapped, October 28 (recycled aluminum) |

**Worked example: Cell 4 sort (illustrative)**

The team tagged 214 items in a two-day sort. After the 30-day review, the outcomes were:

| Outcome | Items | Share |
|---|---|---|
| Returned to stores or tool crib | 61 | 28.5% |
| Transferred to another department | 38 | 17.8% |
| Scrapped (incl. 9 items via EHS as hazardous waste) | 47 | 22.0% |
| Sold or recycled | 22 | 10.3% |
| Kept after review (moved back into the cell) | 46 | 21.5% |
| **Total** | **214** | **100.0%** |

Removing two surplus racks and an unused workbench freed about 180 sq ft, or 7.5 percent of the cell (180 / 2,400). The 46 items that came back are a normal result: the holding period exists because the team cannot always judge need on the spot.

**Common mistakes in Sort:**

- Sorting without operators, so items they need disappear and trust is lost on day one.
- No holding area or review date, so red-tagged items pile up in a corner and become the new clutter.
- Throwing away inventory that is still on the books without telling finance. Scrapping tagged material can mean a write-off; the controller needs to know (see the [business case](#the-business-case-costs-savings-and-payback)).
- Sorting only what is visible. Drawers, cabinet tops and the area behind machines are where most clutter lives.
- Treating "just in case" items as needed without asking how often "just in case" actually happened in the last year.

### Step 2: Set in order (seiton)

**Purpose:** give every needed item a fixed, marked home so that anyone can find it, use it and put it back without searching, and so that a missing item is obvious.

**How to do it:**

1. **Map the work.** Draw the current layout and trace the operator's path for a typical job (a spaghetti diagram). Note where tools and materials are used, not just where they are stored.
2. **Place by frequency and sequence.** Items used every cycle go within reach at the point of use, in the order of use. Items used rarely go further away. The goal is to cut walking, reaching and bending, which is also an ergonomics topic covered by OSHA's ergonomics resources [32].
3. **Make the home visible.** Use shadow boards, foam cutouts, labeled bins, outlines on the floor and address labels on racks. The EPA lists floor painting, labels, placards, outlined work zones and modular shelving as typical Set-in-order tools [3].
4. **Set quantities.** Mark minimum and maximum levels on racks and bins. When stock drops to the min line, that is the reorder signal; above the max line is overproduction or overbuying.
5. **Mark the floor.** Outline aisles, work cells, WIP locations, inspection holds and keep-clear zones. Permanent aisles must be marked under OSHA 1910.176(a) [14]; the [safety section](#safety-and-5s-the-6s-question-and-what-osha-requires) explains the rules and the conventions.
6. **Test with a stranger.** Ask someone from another area to find five items. If they need help, the labels are not clear enough.

**Address system for storage (example):**

| Element | Example | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Area | C4 | Cell 4 |
| Rack | R2 | Rack 2 |
| Shelf level | 3 | Third shelf from the floor |
| Position | 05 | Fifth position from the left |
| Full address | C4-R2-3-05 | Printed on the shelf label and in the bill of materials or ERP storage location |

The address belongs in the system, too. If ERP or the [MES](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/mes-isa-95/) (planned) still points to an old location, material handlers will keep delivering to the wrong place. Align the physical address with the storage location in your systems as part of Set in order; the [intralogistics guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/production-scheduling-aps/intralogistics/) (planned) covers point-of-use delivery and milk runs in detail.

**Worked example: Cell 4 search time (illustrative)**

Before 5S, a one-week observation in Cell 4 counted an average of 9 search events per operator per shift (gauges, inserts, wrenches, the right revision of the drawing), averaging 1.5 minutes each. After Set in order, a repeat observation found 2 search events at 0.5 minutes each.

- Before: 9 × 1.5 = 13.5 minutes per operator per shift
- After: 2 × 0.5 = 1.0 minute per operator per shift
- Saving: 12.5 minutes per operator per shift
- Per day: 12.5 × 6 operators × 2 shifts = 150 minutes = 2.5 hours
- Per year (240 working days): 2.5 × 240 = 600 hours

Six hundred hours is roughly a third of one full-time operator, assuming about 1,800 productive hours per year. The $42 per hour used below is an illustrative loaded labor rate (wage plus benefits and payroll taxes); use your plant's rate. Whether that saving becomes money depends on what the time is used for, which the [business case](#the-business-case-costs-savings-and-payback) addresses.

**Common mistakes in Set in order:**

- Labeling everything, including things that never move. Labels on a workbench that says "workbench" add noise, not information.
- Designing storage for the supervisor's sense of order instead of the operator's sequence of work.
- Shadow boards far from the point of use. A shadow board 40 feet from the machine still means walking.
- Fixed homes for items with no fixed quantity, so the min/max logic is missing and overflow returns.
- Forgetting the systems: the shelf moved, but the ERP storage location did not.

### Step 3: Shine (seiso)

**Purpose:** clean the work area and equipment and, while cleaning, inspect for abnormalities. A clean machine shows its leaks, cracks, loose fasteners and worn hoses; a dirty one hides them. The EPA notes that a clean area helps workers spot leaks, vibration, breakage and misalignment before they cause failures [3].

**How to do it:**

1. **Initial deep clean.** Clean everything once to "like new" condition so that any new dirt or leak stands out. Bring maintenance along; they will see things operators may not.
2. **Lock out before you clean inside guards.** OSHA's lockout/tagout standard, 29 CFR 1910.147, defines servicing and maintenance to include "lubrication, cleaning or unjamming of machines or equipment" [22]. Cleaning that requires removing guards, bypassing safety devices or placing a body part in a danger zone needs energy control under the standard. The standard exempts minor servicing during normal production only if it is routine, repetitive and integral to production and other measures give effective protection [22]. Decide which cleaning tasks fall where with EHS before the event, and write it on the cleaning standard.
3. **Tag abnormalities.** Every abnormality found while cleaning gets a tag (often a different color than red tags, for example a maintenance tag) and an entry in a list with owner and due date.
4. **Find and fix sources of contamination.** Cleaning the same chip pile every hour is not 5S; asking why chips land there is. Add chip guards, fix the coolant leak, change the hose routing.
5. **Make cleaning easy.** Improve access to hard-to-clean spots, mount cleaning tools at the point of use, and define how long each task should take.
6. **Write the cleaning and inspection plan** (template below) and move it into Standardize.

**Cleaning and inspection plan template:**

| Item / zone | Task | What to check while cleaning | Method and tools | Frequency | Time | Responsible | Energy control needed? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Machine 1 way covers | Wipe chips and coolant | Damaged wipers, chips under covers | Brush, lint-free cloth | Every shift | 3 min | Operator | No (outside guards) |
| Machine 1 coolant tank | Skim oil, check level | Smell, tramp oil, level below min line | Skimmer, sight glass | Daily | 5 min | Operator | No |
| Machine 1 chip conveyor | Clear jams, clean | Belt wear, jams, unusual noise | Hook tool | Weekly | 15 min | Operator + maintenance | Yes, LOTO procedure M1-03 |
| Hydraulic unit | Wipe, inspect | Leaks at fittings, hose wear, oil level | Cloth, flashlight | Weekly | 5 min | Operator | No |
| Floor around cell | Sweep, degrease | Oil spots (new leak?), trip hazards | Broom, absorbent | Every shift | 5 min | Last shift operator | No |

**Worked example: Cell 4 abnormalities found (illustrative)**

In the first four weeks of daily cleaning and inspection, Cell 4 operators tagged 14 abnormalities: 6 leaks (coolant and hydraulic fittings), 3 loose or damaged guards, 5 worn hoses or cables. Maintenance closed 11 within the month; 3 waited for parts. Each closed tag is a potential breakdown that did not happen, which is why Shine connects directly to autonomous maintenance in [TPM](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/tpm-oee/) (planned) and to the "abnormalities found" KPI below.

**Common mistakes in Shine:**

- Treating it as janitorial work. If the cleaning crew does it, operators lose the inspection effect.
- Cleaning without inspecting, or inspecting without a way to log and fix what was found.
- Ignoring energy control during cleaning. Cleaning and unjamming are classic lockout/tagout situations [22].
- Increasing chemical use. The EPA notes that frequent cleaning and painting can raise solvent use and emissions [3]; prefer dry methods, low-VOC products and source elimination.
- Polishing for audits instead of fixing the source of dirt.

### Step 4: Standardize (seiketsu)

**Purpose:** make the conditions created by the previous steps the normal, repeatable state, with clear standards, assigned duties and visuals that show at a glance whether the standard is met. The EPA describes Standardize as assigning 5S duties, integrating them into regular work and checking that they are maintained, with checklists, visual cues and short scheduled 5S periods as typical tools [3].

**How to do it:**

1. **Photograph the standard.** Post a "this is how it should look" photo for each zone, workbench and shadow board, with the date and the team's sign-off.
2. **Write a 5S map.** One page per area showing zones and the person or role responsible for each.
3. **Turn the cleaning and inspection plan into a daily routine.** Typically five minutes at the end of a shift, plus longer weekly tasks.
4. **Standardize visuals across the site.** One floor-marking color scheme, one label format, one tag system. Differences between areas confuse people who move between them, including forklift drivers, maintenance and new hires.
5. **Use one-point lessons.** A single page with one picture and a few words that teaches one standard (for example "how to return a gauge to the board").
6. **Prevent, do not just maintain.** The EPA includes prevention of clutter, breakdowns and dirt in this step [3]: ask how to keep the area from getting dirty or cluttered again.

**Site visual standard (example table):**

| Element | Standard | Owner |
|---|---|---|
| Floor marking colors | One site legend (see safety section), posted at every entrance | EHS + CI |
| Line width | Aisles 3 in, cell outlines 2 in, corners and T-marks for pallets | Facilities |
| Labels | Black on white or yellow, sans-serif, address code plus item name, readable at 5 ft | CI |
| Shadow boards | Color per area, tool name on the outline | Area team |
| Tags | Red = Sort, yellow = maintenance/abnormality, green = safety suggestion | CI + maintenance + EHS |
| Photos of standard | Updated when the layout changes, dated, signed by the team | Team lead |

**Common mistakes in Standardize:**

- Standards written by the CI office and handed down. Teams follow standards they helped create.
- Standards nobody can see. A binder in the supervisor's office does not standardize anything on the floor.
- Too many standards. If the 5S checklist for one workbench has 40 lines, it will not be used.
- No owner per zone, so "everybody" is responsible, which means nobody is.

### Step 5: Sustain (shitsuke)

**Purpose:** keep the standard alive through habits, leadership routines, audits and continuous improvement. The EPA calls Sustain the hardest pillar because people tend to revert to old methods [3]. ASQ warns that many companies implement only the first three steps and that without formalizing standardize and sustain, the initiative is "doomed to failure" [2].

**How to do it:**

1. **Audit on a fixed cadence**, scored with the same template (see the [audit section](#the-5s-audit-checklist-template-scoring-and-cadence)), and post the score on the area board.
2. **Put 5S into leader standard work.** Supervisors check one zone per day; managers check one area per week on their gemba walk; plant leadership reviews trends monthly. The [Shop Floor Management guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/shop-floor-management/) describes leader standard work and tiered meetings.
3. **Respond to findings within a day.** An audit finding that sits for weeks teaches people that audits do not matter.
4. **Improve the standard.** Every audit finding is a question: is the standard wrong, or was it not followed? Use [PDCA](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/lean-manufacturing/pdca-cycle/) (planned) to change the standard when it does not work.
5. **Make adherence visible and recognize effort and improvement**, not only top scores. Rotating the best area every month rewards the area that started tidy.
6. **Keep 5S in onboarding.** New hires learn the area standard in their first week. Over time this is how 5S becomes part of the work culture instead of a campaign: leaders foster it through what they check, ask about and fix.

**Common mistakes in Sustain:**

- No leadership presence. If managers never look, teams correctly conclude it does not matter.
- Audits used for blame. Low scores then get hidden instead of fixed.
- Monthly "5S days" instead of daily routines. Clutter builds every day; the routine has to as well.
- Contests that reward appearance before visits from customers or executives. That is theater, not 5S.

### The five steps at a glance: inputs and outputs

| Step | Key question | Main tools | Output you can check |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sort | Do we need this here? | Red tags, holding area, decision rule | Red-tag log with dispositions; freed space |
| Set in order | Can anyone find it and put it back in seconds? | Spaghetti diagram, shadow boards, labels, floor marking, min/max | Address system; marked locations; search time |
| Shine | Is it clean, and what did cleaning reveal? | Deep clean, cleaning and inspection plan, abnormality tags, LOTO | Abnormality log; cleaning plan with times |
| Standardize | How do we keep it this way? | Photo standards, 5S map, one-point lessons, site visual standard | Posted standards; assigned zones |
| Sustain | Do we keep it, and are we improving it? | Audits, leader standard work, PDCA, recognition | Audit trend; closed findings; updated standards |

## Safety and 5S: the 6S question and what OSHA requires

Housekeeping is a legal duty in the US, not only a lean practice. That gives EHS managers a natural stake in 5S and gives CI teams a set of hard requirements to design around. This section separates what federal OSHA requires from what is industry convention. State plans can add requirements; check your state.

### What OSHA requires (federal general industry, 29 CFR 1910)

| Topic | Requirement in short | Standard |
|---|---|---|
| Housekeeping | Places of employment, passageways, storerooms, service rooms and walking-working surfaces must be kept clean, orderly and sanitary; workroom floors clean and, where feasible, dry; surfaces free of hazards such as sharp or protruding objects, loose boards, leaks and spills | 1910.22(a) [13] |
| Inspection of walking-working surfaces | Inspected regularly and as necessary, hazards corrected or guarded before the surface is used again | 1910.22(d) [13] |
| Aisles and passageways | Sufficient safe clearance where mechanical handling equipment is used; aisles kept clear and in good repair; "Permanent aisles and passageways shall be appropriately marked" | 1910.176(a) [14] |
| Storage | Tiered storage stacked, blocked, interlocked and limited in height; storage areas free of accumulated materials that create tripping, fire, explosion or pest hazards | 1910.176(b), (c) [14] |
| Safety colors | Red for fire protection equipment, danger and emergency stops; yellow as the basic color for caution and for marking physical hazards such as striking against, stumbling, falling, tripping and being caught in between | 1910.144 [15] |
| Signs and tags | Danger signs (red, black, white), caution signs (yellow background, black panel), safety instruction signs (white background, green panel); accident prevention tags with signal words readable at a minimum of 5 feet; color specifications incorporated by reference from ANSI Z53.1-1967 or ANSI Z535.1-2006 (R2011) | 1910.145 [16] |
| Exit routes | Free and unobstructed; no materials or equipment, permanently or temporarily, in the exit route; exits marked with "Exit" signs in letters at least 6 inches high | 1910.37 [19] |
| Electrical working space | Working space around electric equipment must be kept clear and "may not be used for storage"; at least 30 inches wide or the width of the equipment, whichever is greater, with depth by voltage per Table S-1 | 1910.303(g)(1) [20] |
| Portable fire extinguishers | Mounted, located and identified so they are readily accessible to employees | 1910.157(c)(1) [21] |
| Energy control | Lockout/tagout for servicing and maintenance, which includes cleaning and unjamming, unless the minor-servicing exception applies | 1910.147 [22] |

Three practical consequences for 5S:

1. **Keep-clear zones are not optional.** Marking a hatched or outlined zone in front of electrical panels, extinguishers, eyewash stations and exit doors is one of the most useful things a 5S team can do. It turns three OSHA requirements into a visual that anyone can check in a second.
2. **Shine needs an energy-control decision for each cleaning task.** Write it on the cleaning plan (see the template in Step 3).
3. **Your 5S audit can carry the housekeeping checks.** That avoids a separate EHS walk for the same items and makes the audit more relevant to operators.

### Floor marking: requirement vs. convention

The most common US question about visual management is which floor-marking colors OSHA requires. The short answer: OSHA requires permanent aisles to be "appropriately marked" [14], assigns meaning to red and yellow for specific hazards [15], and otherwise leaves the scheme to the employer.

Two archived OSHA letters of interpretation, which OSHA notes may no longer represent current policy, give useful guidance:

- **May 15, 1972:** aisle lines "may be any color so long as they clearly define the area considered as aisle space"; lines may be dotted, segmented or continuous; a width of 2 to 6 inches is recommended and any width of 2 inches or more is acceptable; the letter also recommends aisles at least 3 feet wider than the largest equipment used, or a minimum of 4 feet [17].
- **February 14, 1977:** painted yellow lines are "usually recognized as the most convenient and inexpensive way to mark aisles and passageways," but other methods such as marking pillars, flags, traffic cones or barrels are acceptable if they are included in training; where markings are needed and missing, "citations will be issued" [18].

Beyond that, most US plants use a color scheme that floor-marking suppliers and lean practitioners have converged on. It is a convention, not a federal standard, and schemes vary. A typical legend:

| Color | Common use in many US plants | Regulatory anchor |
|---|---|---|
| Yellow | Aisles, traffic lanes, work cell outlines | Yellow = caution, physical hazards (1910.144) [15]; 1977 letter on yellow aisle lines [18] |
| White | Equipment, fixtures, racks, carts, workbench locations | None (convention) |
| Green | Raw material, finished goods, or sometimes safety equipment | None (convention) |
| Blue | Raw material or WIP (in some schemes) | None (convention) |
| Orange | Material on hold for inspection, temporary storage | None (convention) |
| Red | Defects, scrap, rework, red-tag area; fire equipment | Red = fire protection, danger, stop (1910.144) [15] |
| Black and yellow stripes | Physical hazards, areas that may expose workers to special hazards | Yellow = caution (1910.144) [15] |
| Red and white stripes | Keep-clear areas in front of electrical panels, fire equipment, exits | Supports 1910.37, 1910.157, 1910.303 requirements [19][20][21] |
| Black and white stripes | Areas to keep clear for operational reasons | None (convention) |

**Rules that matter more than the exact colors:**

- One legend for the whole site, posted at entrances and included in forklift and new-hire training (the 1977 letter makes training the condition for alternative markings [18]).
- Never use color as the only code. Some people have red-green color vision deficiency; add text, symbols or patterns.
- Durable materials for traffic areas. Tape that peels is a trip hazard of its own.
- Keep marking minimal. Every line should mean something someone needs to know.

### Do you need a sixth S for safety?

The 6S debate is mostly about emphasis.

**Arguments for 6S:** it gives safety its own name, own audit section and own owner, and it brings EHS into the program from the start. Several competitor pages and many US plants use it [1][5].

**Arguments against a separate S:** safety belongs in every step. Sort removes trip hazards and excess flammables; Set in order marks aisles and keep-clear zones; Shine finds leaks and damaged guards; Standardize writes energy control into cleaning; Sustain audits it daily. A separate "Safety" step can suggest that safety is an add-on that comes after the real work.

**Our recommendation:** call it what your plant prefers, but build safety into each S and into the audit template rather than appending it. If you use "6S", define Safety concretely, for example: keep-clear zones, signs, PPE stations, chemical labels, energy control on cleaning tasks, and walking-working surfaces. The audit template below includes these items under the five classic headings plus a safety check in each.

## Visual management: tools, design rules and examples

Once the workplace is orderly, visual management makes its status readable. The LEI defines visual management as placing tools, parts, production activities and performance indicators "in plain view," so that the status of the system can be understood at a glance by everyone involved [9]. That definition contains two tests for every visual: is it in plain view at the place where the decision is made, and can everyone understand it at a glance?

### Types of visual management: a ladder of visual devices

Practitioners often describe visual devices as a ladder from passive to preventive. The terms vary by author; the logic is consistent:

| Level | What the visual does | Example | Strength |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Visual indicator | Tells: informs anyone who looks | Label, sign, address, photo standard | Low: depends on people looking |
| 2. Visual signal | Grabs attention when something changes | Andon light, stack light, flashing beacon, alarm | Medium: pushes the information out |
| 3. Visual control | Limits or guides behavior | Min/max line on a rack, shaped foam cutout, color band on a gauge, floor outline sized for exactly one pallet | Higher: the standard is built in |
| 4. Mistake-proofing | Makes the error impossible or stops the process | Fixture that accepts the part only one way, sensor that stops the cycle | Highest: does not depend on attention |

The higher the level, the less the visual depends on attention and discipline. Level 4 is the domain of [poka-yoke](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/lean-manufacturing/poka-yoke/) (planned), and level 2 includes [andon](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/shop-floor-management/andon/) (planned). The LEI describes andon as a visual management tool that shows the status of operations at a glance and signals whenever an abnormality occurs [23].

### Visual management toolbox

| Tool | Purpose | Where | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Floor marking | Define aisles, cells, WIP locations, keep-clear zones | Whole site | See OSHA rules above [14][15][17] |
| Shadow board / foam cutout | Show where each tool belongs and which one is missing | Point of use | Keep within reach of the work |
| Labels and address system | Identify items and storage locations | Racks, bins, cabinets | Match ERP/MES storage locations |
| Min/max markings | Show when to replenish and when there is too much | Racks, bins, supermarkets | First step toward kanban |
| Kanban cards and signals | Authorize production or replenishment | Supermarkets, lines | LEI: a signal that triggers and controls the production or movement of parts in a pull system [26]; see [Kanban and Pull Systems](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/kanban-pull-system/) (planned) |
| Andon | Signal abnormalities and call for help | Workstations, lines | Needs a response standard, not only lights [23] |
| Stack lights on machines | Show machine status (running, stopped, waiting) | Machines | Define colors site-wide |
| Hour-by-hour board (production control board) | Compare plan and actual every hour, record reasons for gaps | End of line or cell | Example below |
| Team board (SQDCP or huddle board) | Show safety, quality, delivery, cost and people key performance indicators (KPIs) and actions | Team area | See the [Shop Floor Management guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/shop-floor-management/) |
| Standard work at the point of use | Show sequence, takt and key points | Workstation | LEI: standardized work has three elements, takt time, work sequence and standard inventory [25]; see [Standard Work](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/shop-floor-management/standard-work/) (planned) |
| Color-coded gauges and dials | Show normal operating range at a glance | Pressure gauges, thermometers | Green band = normal range |
| Match marks | Show whether a bolt has loosened | Critical fasteners | A painted line across nut and bolt |
| Valve position tags | Show normal open/closed position | Process lines | Supports lockout/tagout checks |
| Heijunka box / scheduling board | Show the leveled production sequence | Pacemaker process | See the [Production Scheduling guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/production-scheduling-aps/) |
| Quality alert boards | Show current defects, containment and checks | Entry to area | Remove when closed |

### Worked example: hour-by-hour board in Cell 4 (illustrative)

Cell 4 runs a repeat part with a planned output of 30 parts per hour on the bottleneck machine. The first hour is planned at 25 because of the start-of-shift meeting and warm-up. The team lead writes the actual count at the end of each hour and a reason for every gap.

| Hour | Plan | Actual | Cum. plan | Cum. actual | Gap (cum.) | Reason / action |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 6:00–7:00 | 25 | 24 | 25 | 24 | −1 | Warm-up cycle longer, probe recalibrated |
| 7:00–8:00 | 30 | 30 | 55 | 54 | −1 | |
| 8:00–9:00 | 30 | 22 | 85 | 76 | −9 | Insert broke, no spare at shadow board (missing since 2nd shift); tag C4-M-031 |
| 9:00–10:00 | 30 | 29 | 115 | 105 | −10 | |
| 10:00–11:00 | 30 | 31 | 145 | 136 | −9 | |
| 11:00–12:00 | 20 | 20 | 165 | 156 | −9 | Lunch break |
| 12:00–13:00 | 30 | 30 | 195 | 186 | −9 | |
| 13:00–14:00 | 30 | 30 | 225 | 216 | −9 | |

The board did two things. It made a 9-part loss visible within the hour, so the team lead could react, and it connected the loss to a 5S failure: a spare insert was not back on the shadow board. The countermeasure (a min/max of two spares with a reorder card) is a Set-in-order fix. That loop, from a visual signal to a 5S standard, is what visual management is for. Hour-by-hour data also feeds the availability and performance factors of OEE, which the [Manufacturing KPIs guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/manufacturing-kpis/) explains.

### Benefits of visual management

The benefits of visual management follow from the nine functions listed earlier [10]: everyone in the organization has access to the same information at the point of work, deviations show up in near real time instead of in next week's report, standards are easier to follow because they are built into the workplace, and discussions start from shared facts. The hour-by-hour board above is a small example of all four. None of this needs software; a whiteboard and a marker are often the most effective visual management tools in the first year.

### Design rules for effective visual management

Implementing visual management follows the same order as 5S: start small in one area, prove that people use the visuals, then standardize them across the site. These rules separate boards that get used from boards that get ignored:

1. **Place the visual where the decision is made.** A board in the office does not help the operator at the machine.
2. **Make it readable from where people stand.** For tags, OSHA requires signal words readable at a minimum of 5 feet [16]; use that as a floor, not a target. Exit sign letters must be at least 6 inches high [19], a useful reference for overhead area signs.
3. **One meaning per color, site-wide.** Red on the floor, red on a stack light and red on a board should all mean "problem" or "danger".
4. **Do not rely on color alone.** Pair colors with text, shapes or patterns.
5. **Show normal vs. abnormal, not just data.** A number without a target is information; a number with a target and a color is a signal.
6. **Keep it current.** A board that shows last week's numbers trains people to ignore it. If nobody updates it, remove it.
7. **Fewer visuals, more meaning.** Too many signs and screens create visual noise; people stop seeing them. Tulip's visual management guide lists the risk of too much visual information among its FAQs [39].
8. **Build visuals with the people who use them.** Operators know which information they need at which moment.
9. **Every visual needs a response.** An andon light without a defined responder and response time is decoration [23].

### Visual management in offices and support functions

The same logic applies off the shop floor: a planning board that shows which orders are late, kanban boards that track progress of requests in engineering or IT, a maintenance backlog board, a quality complaint tracker on the wall or on a shared screen. The test is unchanged: can a visitor tell within a minute whether the team is on plan, and what the biggest problem is?

## How to implement 5S: a 12-month roadmap

5S looks simple, which is why plants underestimate it. The steps themselves can be done in a week; making them the normal state takes months. The roadmap below starts with one pilot area, proves the routines, then scales. The time frames are practitioner guidance for a midsize plant (here: roughly 100 to 500 employees), not a published standard; adjust them to your size and starting point [unverified].

### Phase 0: preparation (weeks 1 to 3)

- **Decide why.** Tie 5S to a business problem: long changeovers, safety findings, quality escapes from mixed parts, searching, or a planned layout change. "Because lean says so" is not a reason operators will accept.
- **Name the roles.** A plant sponsor (plant manager or operations director), a 5S coordinator (often from CI), area owners (supervisors), EHS and maintenance as permanent members, finance for inventory decisions.
- **Talk to the workforce first.** Explain goals, what will change and what will not. In unionized plants, involve union representatives early; see the [respect-for-people section](#5s-and-respect-for-people).
- **Agree on the site standards before the pilot** where possible: floor-marking legend, tag colors, label format. Changing them after five areas are done is expensive.
- **Collect the baseline.** Photos of the current state, a first audit score, a search-time observation, open safety findings, changeover times, and a red-tag area location.

### Phase 1: pilot (weeks 4 to 12)

Start small. One well-chosen pilot teaches more than a site-wide launch, and it gives the rest of the plant something real to look at.

**Choosing the pilot area:**

| Criterion | Good pilot | Poor pilot |
|---|---|---|
| Visibility | On the main walking route; people see the change | Hidden storeroom nobody visits |
| Business link | Bottleneck machine, frequent changeovers, quality issues | Area with no measurable problem |
| Team | Supervisor willing, operators open to try | Area in conflict or with high turnover |
| Size | One cell or line, 5 to 15 people per shift | Whole department at once |
| Support | Maintenance and EHS available | Area waiting for a major move |

**Pilot sequence (example for Cell 4):**

| Week | Activity | Who | Output |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4 | Introduction to 5S training (2 to 4 hours), baseline photos and audit | All Cell 4 operators, supervisor, coordinator | Trained team, baseline score |
| 5 | Sort event (2 days), red-tag area set up | Team + maintenance + EHS | Red-tag log |
| 6 | Set in order (3 days): layout, shadow boards, labels, floor marking | Team + facilities | Marked locations, address system |
| 7 | Shine: deep clean with maintenance; abnormality tags; cleaning plan | Team + maintenance | Abnormality log, cleaning plan |
| 8 | Standardize: photo standards, 5S map, daily routine | Team + supervisor | Posted standards |
| 8 | Red-tag review (30 days after Sort) | Supervisor, engineering, finance | Dispositions |
| 9–12 | Sustain: weekly audit, daily 5-minute routine, leader checks | Team, supervisor, manager | Audit trend, closed findings |
| 12 | Pilot review: results, lessons, standards to adopt site-wide | Sponsor, coordinator, team | Go/no-go for scaling |

### Phase 2: scale (months 4 to 9)

- Roll out area by area, two to four areas at a time, using pilot team members as co-facilitators.
- Adopt the pilot's site standards (legend, tags, labels, audit template) everywhere.
- Add 5S to tiered meetings: area score at tier 1, trend and open findings at tier 2, site trend at tier 3.
- Start cross-area audits: supervisors audit a neighboring area once a month, which spreads good ideas and reduces scoring bias.
- Include offices, maintenance shops and warehouses. They often hold the most clutter and set an example for the floor.

### Phase 3: integrate (months 10 to 12 and beyond)

- Connect 5S to the next lean steps: [SMED](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/smed-changeover-reduction/) (planned) uses shadow boards and point-of-use tooling, [TPM](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/tpm-oee/) (planned) builds autonomous maintenance on Shine, and [kanban](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/kanban-pull-system/) (planned) builds on min/max markings.
- Raise the bar: move from level 1 visuals (labels) to level 3 and 4 (visual controls and mistake-proofing).
- Make 5S part of onboarding, leader standard work and the annual plan.
- Run a 12-month review: audit trend, KPI results, costs and the next targets.

### Kickoff checklist

| # | Item | Done |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Business reason for 5S written in one paragraph and shared | ☐ |
| 2 | Sponsor, coordinator, area owners, EHS, maintenance, finance named | ☐ |
| 3 | Workforce (and union, where applicable) briefed | ☐ |
| 4 | Site floor-marking legend, tag colors and label format agreed | ☐ |
| 5 | Pilot area chosen with the criteria above | ☐ |
| 6 | Baseline photos, audit, search time and changeover times recorded | ☐ |
| 7 | Red-tag holding area marked, review date and decision owners set | ☐ |
| 8 | Energy-control decisions for cleaning tasks reviewed with EHS | ☐ |
| 9 | Budget for materials (labels, tape or paint, shadow boards, bins) approved | ☐ |
| 10 | Time for the team protected in the production schedule | ☐ |

The last item is where many pilots fail. If the schedule does not release people for the sort and set-in-order days, the event becomes overtime or gets canceled. Plan the event like a planned downtime, with the [production schedule](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/production-scheduling-aps/) adjusted in advance.

### Roles during implementation

| Role | Responsibilities |
|---|---|
| Plant sponsor | Sets the reason, protects time, attends the kickoff and the pilot review, audits personally once a month |
| 5S coordinator | Trains, facilitates events, owns the site standards and audit template, reports the trend |
| Area owner (supervisor) | Owns the area standard, runs the daily routine, audits weekly, closes findings |
| Operators | Do the work, propose locations and visuals, own their zones, audit their own area |
| Maintenance | Supports Shine, closes abnormality tags, designs access for cleaning |
| EHS | Defines safety requirements, energy control for cleaning, chemical disposal, sign and floor-marking legend |
| Finance | Decides on inventory dispositions, write-offs and the business case |
| IT / OT | Aligns storage locations in ERP/MES, supports digital audits or boards if used |

## The 5S audit checklist: template, scoring and cadence

An audit is how you evaluate an area against its 5S standards. It makes the state of an area comparable over time and between areas. It also gives the team a checklist for daily self-checks. The best audits are short, specific and scored the same way every time.

### Audit template (25 items, 0 to 4 points)

Each item is scored on a five-point scale: **0** = not in place, **1** = started, major gaps, **2** = partly in place, several gaps, **3** = in place, minor gaps, **4** = fully in place and maintained. Maximum: 25 items × 4 points = 100 points.

| # | Category | Audit item | Score (0–4) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Sort | No unneeded items, materials or equipment in the area | |
| 2 | Sort | No unneeded items in drawers, cabinets, on top of cabinets or behind machines | |
| 3 | Sort | Red-tag area in use; no tags older than the review period | |
| 4 | Sort | Only the current revision of drawings and instructions at the workstation | |
| 5 | Sort (safety) | No excess flammables or chemicals beyond the shift's need | |
| 6 | Set in order | Every tool, gauge and fixture has a marked home and is in it (or in use) | |
| 7 | Set in order | Storage locations labeled with address and item; labels match ERP/MES | |
| 8 | Set in order | Min/max levels marked and respected | |
| 9 | Set in order | Aisles, cells and WIP locations marked per site legend; markings intact | |
| 10 | Set in order (safety) | Keep-clear zones in front of panels, extinguishers, eyewash and exits clear and marked | |
| 11 | Shine | Floor clean and dry; no oil, coolant or chips on walking surfaces | |
| 12 | Shine | Machines and equipment clean; no new leaks | |
| 13 | Shine | Abnormality tags logged; none overdue | |
| 14 | Shine | Cleaning tools at the point of use and in good condition | |
| 15 | Shine (safety) | Cleaning tasks that need energy control are marked on the plan and followed | |
| 16 | Standardize | Photo standards posted and match the current state | |
| 17 | Standardize | 5S map with zone owners posted and current | |
| 18 | Standardize | Cleaning and inspection plan posted with frequencies and times | |
| 19 | Standardize | Visuals (colors, labels, tags) follow the site standard | |
| 20 | Standardize (safety) | Signs and chemical container labels present and legible | |
| 21 | Sustain | Daily 5S routine done (checklist signed or recorded) | |
| 22 | Sustain | Last audit findings closed or with owner and date | |
| 23 | Sustain | Audit score and trend posted on the area board | |
| 24 | Sustain | At least one 5S improvement implemented since the last audit | |
| 25 | Sustain | Team members can explain the area standard when asked | |

Item 20 relates to OSHA's Hazard Communication Standard (29 CFR 1910.1200), which governs labels on workplace chemical containers. Paragraph (f)(6) requires each workplace container of a hazardous chemical to carry either the shipped-container label information or the product identifier plus general hazard information; paragraph (f)(8) exempts only portable containers intended for the immediate use of the employee who made the transfer [46]. Ask EHS how these rules apply to your secondary containers before relabeling during Set in order.

### Worked example: scoring Cell 4 (illustrative)

| Category | Items | Max | Baseline (week 4) | Week 12 | Month 12 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sort | 1–5 | 20 | 7 | 16 | 17 |
| Set in order | 6–10 | 20 | 5 | 13 | 17 |
| Shine | 11–15 | 20 | 8 | 15 | 16 |
| Standardize | 16–20 | 20 | 2 | 9 | 15 |
| Sustain | 21–25 | 20 | 1 | 7 | 14 |
| **Total** | 25 | **100** | **23** | **60** | **79** |

Two patterns are typical. Sort and Shine improve fast because they are events. Standardize and Sustain lag because they need routines and leadership time, which is exactly ASQ's warning [2]. A radar chart of the five category scores shows the pattern at a glance and is a good visual for the area board.

### Audit cadence and layered audits

| Level | Who | Frequency | Scope | Time |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Self-check | Operator / zone owner | Daily, end of shift | Own zone, short checklist (5 to 8 items) | 5 min |
| Area audit | Supervisor or team lead | Weekly | Full 25-item audit | 20–30 min |
| Cross audit | Supervisor of another area | Monthly | Full audit | 30 min |
| Leadership audit | Plant manager, operations director | Monthly, rotating areas | Full audit plus conversation with the team | 30–45 min |

**Rules that keep audits honest:**

- Score what you see, with a photo for every finding scored 0 or 1.
- Rotate auditors to avoid "home bias" and calibrate once a quarter by having two auditors score the same area.
- Every finding gets an owner and a date; the next audit starts by checking them.
- Do not tie individual pay to audit scores. It invites gaming and conflicts with OSHA's position that incentive programs must not discourage reporting where they touch safety [30].
- Change the template when an item is always 4 everywhere; replace it with the next level of difficulty.

## 5S KPIs: how to measure results

The audit score measures the condition of the area. It does not show whether the plant performs better. Use a small set of both: one or two condition metrics and three or four outcome metrics linked to the business reason for 5S. The [Manufacturing KPIs guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/manufacturing-kpis/) covers the outcome metrics in depth.

| KPI | Formula | Example (Cell 4, illustrative) | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5S audit score | Points scored / maximum points × 100 | 60 / 100 = 60% | Condition of the area |
| Sustain rate | Areas at or above target in each of the last 3 audits / audited areas × 100 | 7 / 12 = 58.3% | Whether improvements stick across the site |
| Red-tag closure rate | Tags with final disposition within review period / tags issued × 100 | 196 / 214 = 91.6% | Discipline of the Sort process |
| Abnormality tag closure | Abnormality tags closed within target time / tags issued × 100 | 11 / 14 = 78.6% | Whether Shine findings get fixed |
| Abnormalities found per month | Count of abnormality tags | 14 in month 1 | Inspection effect of Shine (expect a peak, then decline) |
| Search time | Search events × average duration, per operator per shift | 13.5 → 1.0 min | Direct effect of Set in order |
| Floor space freed | Freed sq ft / area sq ft × 100 | 180 / 2,400 = 7.5% | Effect of Sort |
| Changeover time | Last good part to first good part of next job | 48 → 40 min | Effect of point-of-use tooling, link to SMED |
| Housekeeping findings | EHS or OSHA-related housekeeping findings per audit | 6 → 1 | Safety effect |
| OEE (bottleneck) | Availability × performance × quality | see KPI guide | Combined effect on the machine that limits output |

**How to read the abnormality metric:** a rising count in the first months is good news; it means people are finding problems while they are still small. It should fall later as sources are eliminated. Do not set a target of "fewer abnormalities" in the first quarter, or people will stop tagging.

## The business case: costs, savings and payback

Competitor pages claim that 5S pays back "within a few months" without showing a calculation. Here is one, with every assumption visible. All figures are illustrative for Cell 4; replace them with your plant's numbers.

### Costs in year 1

| Cost item | Calculation | Amount |
|---|---|---|
| Training | 12 operators × 4 h × $42/h (loaded labor rate) | $2,016 |
| Event time (sort, set in order, shine) | 6 operators × 3 days × 8 h × $42/h | $6,048 |
| Materials (labels, floor tape and paint, shadow boards, bins, cleaning tools) | Quote from suppliers | $3,500 |
| Facilitation | 40 h × $60/h (internal CI coordinator) | $2,400 |
| Daily routine | 5 min × 12 operator-shifts/day × 240 days = 240 h × $42/h | $10,080 |
| Weekly audits | 1 h × 52 weeks × $55/h (supervisor) | $2,860 |
| **Total year 1** | | **$26,904** |

### Benefits in year 1

| Benefit | Calculation | Amount |
|---|---|---|
| Search time | 600 h/year (from the Set-in-order example) × $42/h | $25,200 |
| Floor space | 180 sq ft × $9/sq ft/year (internal occupancy cost) | $1,620 |
| Changeover time at the bottleneck | 8 min saved × 3 changeovers/day × 240 days = 96 h × $180/h contribution margin of lost bottleneck output | $17,280 |
| **Total year 1** | | **$44,100** |

**Result:** net benefit in year 1 = $44,100 − $26,904 = $17,196. Simple payback = $26,904 / ($44,100 / 12 months) = about 7.3 months. In year 2, the one-time costs drop out ($2,016 + $6,048 + $3,500 + $2,400 = $13,964), leaving $12,940 of running cost against the same benefits.

### What the calculation teaches

1. **Search time alone does not pay for 5S.** Without the changeover benefit, year 1 is $26,820 of benefits against $26,904 of cost, slightly negative. And the search-time saving is only real cash if the time goes into output that is needed, or if overtime or hiring is avoided. A plant controller will ask that question; answer it before they do.
2. **The big lever is the bottleneck.** Time saved at a constraint adds sellable output, but only if there is demand for it; the $180 per hour of contribution margin in the table assumes the bottleneck is sold out. Time saved at a non-constraint mostly adds idle time. The planned [Theory of Constraints guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/theory-of-constraints/) explains why. Pick a pilot area where 5S touches the bottleneck or a recurring quality or safety problem.
3. **Do not turn search-time savings into layoffs.** If the first result of 5S is a headcount cut, people will stop finding waste, and Sustain will fail. Redeploy the time into training, improvement work or growth, and say so up front. The [Lean Manufacturing guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/lean-manufacturing/) recommends briefing employees on employment security before a lean rollout for the same reason.
4. **Some benefits are real but hard to price.** Avoided injuries, avoided breakdowns from leaks found early and fewer mixed-part defects can outweigh everything in the table. Track them as counts (abnormalities found, housekeeping findings, defects from wrong parts) rather than inventing dollar values.
5. **Inventory has an accounting side.** Red-tagged material that is scrapped or sold below book value can mean a write-down or a charge against an obsolescence reserve. The loss stems from past purchasing and production decisions that 5S only makes visible, yet it hits the P&L in the month of disposal. Agree with finance in advance how dispositions are booked [unverified: specific accounting treatment depends on your policies and auditors].
6. **Materials are mostly opex.** Labels, tape and bins are typically expensed; durable items such as a new rack system or modular workbenches may be capitalized under your plant's capitalization threshold. Ask the controller.

## 5S beyond the assembly line

5S was described for factories, but the logic travels. What changes is what "needed", "in place" and "clean" mean.

| Environment | What to adapt | Typical visuals | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|
| High-mix job shop | Sort by part family and job, not by single product; mobile tool carts per family; staging areas for the next job | Job staging outlines, fixture racks by family, "next job" board | Over-fixing locations for tools that move between machines |
| Process industries (chemicals, food, pharma) | Shine linked to sanitation and contamination control; validated cleaning procedures may already exist | Color-coded cleaning tools by zone, valve position tags, line labels with flow direction | Conflicting with validated procedures; align with QA |
| Warehouse and distribution | Sort obsolete stock and empty pallets; Set in order with slotting by velocity | Location labels, pallet outlines, aisle marking, dock door status boards | Forklift traffic; keep marking durable and consistent with 1910.176 [14] |
| Maintenance shop and tool crib | Shadow boards, kitting for planned jobs, spare parts with min/max | Kit carts, parts labels with criticality, backlog board | The maintenance shop is often the most cluttered area in a plant |
| Laboratory and inspection | Gauge control: calibrated gauges in marked homes, out-of-calibration quarantine | Calibration status labels, quarantine area | Mixing calibrated and uncalibrated gauges |
| Quality hold and MRB areas | Nonconforming product clearly identified and physically separated until a disposition is made; ISO 9001:2015 clause 8.7 requires that outputs that do not conform are identified and controlled to prevent their unintended use or delivery [47]; AS9100 and IATF 16949 plants work to the same requirement plus sector additions [unverified: sector-specific clause text not opened] | Red or orange hold outlines, hold tags with disposition fields, locked cages for high-risk parts | Hold areas that become long-term storage; tags without a disposition date |
| Aerospace and defense | Foreign object debris (FOD) prevention, tool control and accountability | Tool control boards with check-out tags, FOD walk routes | Requirements from customers and programs; see the [Aerospace Production Planning guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/aerospace-production-planning/) |
| Health care | Supply rooms, carts and patient areas; AHRQ includes 5S in its workflow toolkit [4] | Par levels, labeled bins, crash-cart checklists | Clinical priorities over appearance |
| Offices and engineering | Sort files and documents; Set in order shared drives; Standardize naming | Team boards, request queues, naming conventions | Over-policing personal desks (see criticism) |

### Digital 5S for files and data

The ASQ tutorial includes an example from Kaiser Permanente Colorado, where a Medicaid enrollment team applied 5S to its enrollment template and process: fields were sorted and standardized, and a database was built to prevent duplicate entries [2]. The Wikipedia article notes that 5S is applied to knowledge work with information and software in place of physical products [5]. In practice, "digital 5S" means:

| S | Shared drives, SharePoint and Teams | Machine programs and engineering data |
|---|---|---|
| Sort | Archive or delete obsolete files; remove duplicate copies; close unused team sites | Remove obsolete NC programs and old revisions from machine controls |
| Set in order | One folder structure per site or process; links instead of copies | One program library with a defined revision status; programs named by part and revision |
| Shine | Fix broken links, outdated owners and permissions | Check that the program on the control matches the released revision |
| Standardize | Naming convention, retention labels, templates | Release process and naming standard for programs and setups |
| Sustain | Quarterly owner review; reports on stale content | Periodic check of control memory against the library |

Digital 5S has real consequences. An old program revision on a machine control is a quality risk, and duplicate files with different content are a classic root cause of errors. Changing or deleting programs on machine controls is a change to a production system: run it through your engineering change and OT change-management process, not as a cleanup action. For programs and data on OT systems, coordinate with the OT and security teams; see the [OT cybersecurity guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/ot-cybersecurity-iec-62443/) (planned).

## 5S and respect for people

5S changes people's immediate workspace, often the one place on the floor that feels like theirs. Done badly, it feels like an outside team taking away their tools and taping their coffee mug to a marked spot. Done well, it is the first lean method where operators decide how their own workplace is organized.

**Principles that work:**

- **Operators decide locations.** The people who use the tools know the sequence and the reach distances. The coordinator facilitates; the team decides.
- **Ask before you tag personal items.** Agree on a place for personal belongings (lockers, a marked shelf) instead of red-tagging them.
- **Make the work easier first.** If the first visible result of 5S is less walking, less bending and fewer searches, the method sells itself. OSHA's ergonomics resources are a useful starting point for designing point-of-use storage that reduces reaching and lifting [32].
- **Protect time.** 5S on top of a full production target, without time released, is work intensification. The daily five minutes must be in the standard, not squeezed out of breaks.
- **Use audits for learning, not blame.** Post area scores, not individual names.

**US labor and safety context:**

- **Worker participation in safety is an OSHA recommended practice.** OSHA's guidance on safety management calls for workers to participate in all aspects of the safety program, including hazard identification [29]. Building safety items into 5S audits that operators run is a direct way to do that.
- **Incentives must not discourage reporting.** OSHA's 2018 memorandum on safety incentive programs clarifies that such programs are acceptable unless they are used to penalize or discourage injury and illness reporting [30]. If you reward "accident-free 5S areas", make sure the reward does not create pressure to hide incidents.
- **Union plants:** layout, job content and time standards may be subjects of bargaining under the contract. Involve the union before the pilot.
- **Standing 5S committees in non-union plants:** under the National Labor Relations Act, employer-dominated committees that deal with terms and conditions of employment can raise legal questions; *Electromation* (7th Cir. 1994) is the leading case [31]. Teams focused on organizing their own workplace are usually set up differently, but have employment counsel review the charter of any standing committee.

## Common 5S mistakes

| Mistake | Why it happens | What to do instead |
|---|---|---|
| 5S as a one-time cleanup day | Seen as housekeeping, not as a management routine | Plan Standardize and Sustain from the start; ASQ: otherwise "doomed to failure" [2] |
| Starting with tape and labels | Visible quick wins look good in photos | Sort first; the EPA notes that Set in order can only start after Sort [3] |
| CI team does the work for the operators | Faster in the short term | Operators decide; CI facilitates |
| Labeling everything | Equating more labels with more order | Label what moves, what is shared and what is critical |
| No red-tag holding area or review date | Sort done in a hurry | Marked area, fixed review date, decision owners |
| Shine without inspection | Cleaning delegated to janitorial staff | Operators clean their equipment and log abnormalities |
| Ignoring energy control during cleaning | Cleaning seen as harmless | Lockout/tagout decision per task [22] |
| Different color codes in every area | Areas implemented one by one without a site standard | One legend for the site, posted and trained |
| Audits used for ranking and blame | Scores tied to supervisor evaluations | Scores for learning; trend over rank |
| No link to business results | 5S treated as an end in itself | Tie to changeover, quality, safety or space goals |
| 5S before every customer visit | Show for visitors | Daily routine; leaders look on random days |
| Over-policing offices and desks | Copying the factory approach literally | Focus on shared spaces, shared files and team boards |
| Ignoring the systems | Physical changes only | Update ERP/MES storage locations and program libraries |

## Limits and criticism of 5S

5S has critics, and some of the criticism is fair. Taking it seriously makes a 5S program better.

### 1. Cosmetic 5S and "5S police"

The most common failure is 5S that looks good and changes nothing. Taped outlines for staplers and mugs on office desks are a typical example: the method is copied without its purpose, and the people affected experience it as control rather than help. When audits become inspections by a "5S police", teams comply on audit day and drift back the rest of the week. The remedy is to tie each standard to a reason the team accepts, and to judge 5S by what it reveals and enables, not by how it looks.

### 2. The method is easy to strip of its management system

Gapp, Fisher and Kobayashi found that in Japanese companies, 5S combines visible practices with less visible philosophical and managerial elements and functions as an integrated management system [11]. Western implementations often copy only the visible part. That is a criticism of how 5S is implemented, not of 5S itself, but it explains why so many programs fade after a year.

### 3. Standardize is a loose translation

As noted in the history section, seiketsu literally refers to cleanliness and hygiene; reading it as "standardize" can push teams toward uniformity where work is not uniform [5]. In high-mix or creative work, a rigid standard can get in the way. Standardize the conditions that matter (where critical tools go, what normal looks like) and leave room for variation where the work varies.

### 4. Environmental side effects

The EPA lists potential shortcomings of 5S: frequent painting and cleaning may increase solvent and chemical use and emissions; disposing of unneeded items creates a short-term waste surge; unlabeled wastes may be hazardous; and excluding environmental staff can lead to improper disposal or missed recycling [3]. Involve EHS, choose low-VOC products and plan the disposal wave.

### 5. The evidence base is mostly case studies

There is a body of literature on 5S, reviewed for example by Randhawa and Ahuja (2017) [12], but much of it consists of single-company case studies with different starting points and measures. Few studies isolate the effect of 5S from other changes made at the same time. Be careful with benefit percentages quoted without a source, including on vendor pages, and measure your own baseline.

### 6. Time cost

AHRQ names the time required for full implementation as the main disadvantage of 5S [4]. The daily routine and audits are a permanent cost, as the business case shows. If the benefits do not justify that time in a particular area, a lighter standard is the right answer.

### 7. 5S does not fix the process

5S makes problems visible. It does not solve a bad layout, an unstable process or a capacity gap. If the same clutter reappears every week, the cause is often upstream: overproduction, batch sizes, missing kanban, or a schedule that pushes work into the area. That calls for [value stream mapping](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/value-stream-mapping/) and pull, not more tape.

## Digital 5S and digital visual management

Most of 5S is physical and should stay that way: a shadow board works without power and without login. Digital tools help where paper does not scale: audit data across many areas, trends over months, alerts when a machine stops, and boards that have to show data from systems.

### What digital tools can do

| Need | Paper solution | Digital option | When digital pays off |
|---|---|---|---|
| Daily 5S self-check | Laminated checklist, signed | Mobile checklist in a frontline app, for example a task list published to teams | Many areas; need to see completion across the site |
| Weekly audit and trend | Paper audit, hand-drawn trend | Audit form on a tablet with photos; list or database; trend chart | More than about 10 areas, or cross-site comparison |
| Finding and tag tracking | Tag board with paper tags | Digital list with owner, due date, photo, status | Many open findings; maintenance work orders in a CMMS |
| Hour-by-hour board | Whiteboard | Screen with counts from the machine or MES | Automated counts available; several lines |
| Andon | Lights and cord | IIoT-based andon with notifications to responders | Responders not always in sight of the line |
| Team board KPIs | Whiteboard and printed charts | Dashboard in the team's workspace | Data already in systems; manual transcription costs time |

**Microsoft 365 as an example.** Many plants already have Microsoft 365, so it is a common first platform for digital 5S. Microsoft documents task publishing in Planner, where operations managers publish task lists to teams in a defined hierarchy and track completion through reports, with checklists of up to 20 items per task [33]. That fits a daily 5S self-check across many areas. Microsoft Lists can hold audit results and findings [34], Power BI reports can be shared in Teams for trends [35], and Microsoft's frontline guidance for manufacturing describes further scenarios [36]. Product names and features change often; check the current documentation before you design a solution. The [Manufacturing IT with Azure guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/manufacturing-it-azure/) covers the broader architecture.

### Rules for digital visual management

1. **Start on paper.** Prove the routine and the content of the board on paper first; digitize once the team agrees on what it needs.
2. **Keep the gemba in the loop.** A dashboard in the office is not a substitute for looking at the area. The data shows that something is wrong; the floor shows why.
3. **Automate data, not judgment.** Counts, stops and audit completion can be automated. The reason for a gap on an hour-by-hour board still comes from the team.
4. **Respect OT boundaries.** Connecting machines for automated andon or counts touches the OT network. Follow your site's architecture, for example ISA-95 levels [38] and ISA/IEC 62443 zones and conduits [37][45]; see the planned [MES and ISA-95](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/mes-isa-95/) and [OT cybersecurity](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/ot-cybersecurity-iec-62443/) guides.
5. **Be careful with monitoring.** Screens that show individual performance can be read as surveillance. Show team and process data, agree on it with the workforce and, where applicable, the union.
6. **Paperless needs the same 5S discipline.** Digital work instructions must be current and easy to find, or they are clutter on a screen; see the planned [paperless manufacturing guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/shop-floor-management/paperless-manufacturing/).

### 5S, digital twins and simulation

When a plant plans a new layout, the 5S questions (what is needed, where should it go, how far will people walk) can be answered in a layout model or a [digital twin](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/digital-twin-manufacturing/) before anything is moved. Walking distances, point-of-use storage and aisle widths are classic inputs for layout and material flow simulation; see the planned [manufacturing simulation guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/digital-twin-manufacturing/manufacturing-simulation/). The model is only as good as the observed data, which is why the gemba observation comes first.

### 5S vs. kaizen

Kaizen means continuous improvement; it describes the mindset and the practice of improving a process, in the LEI's words through small, incremental improvements [43]. 5S is one specific method for workplace organization. A 5S event is often a team's first kaizen, and Sustain turns 5S into a continuous improvement loop. Kaizen is the broader concept; 5S is one of its tools.

### 5S vs. Six Sigma

Six Sigma is a data-driven method for reducing variation and defects, usually run as DMAIC projects by trained belts [44]. 5S is a workplace method run by every team, with no statistics required. They complement each other: a stable, orderly workplace reduces variation from wrong tools, mixed parts and contamination, and Six Sigma projects often find 5S issues among their root causes. The [Operational Excellence guide](https://www.m365.fm/en/learn/operational-excellence/) discusses how both fit into a broader improvement system.

## FAQ: 5S and visual management

### What does 5S stand for?

5S stands for five Japanese words and their English equivalents: seiri (Sort), seiton (Set in order), seiso (Shine), seiketsu (Standardize) and shitsuke (Sustain) [1][2]. Some sources use Straighten for seiton or Scrub for seiso [4][8].

### What are the 5S of lean?

They are the same five steps. In lean manufacturing, 5S is usually the first method a plant introduces because flow, pull and standard work depend on an organized workplace [3].

### What is the purpose of 5S?

To create a workplace where needed items are easy to find and use, and where abnormalities are visible at a glance. Productivity, quality and safety gains follow from that [2][3].

### Who invented 5S?

No single inventor is documented. 5S developed in Japan in the context of the Toyota Production System and just-in-time production [5][8]. The first English-language books were written by Takashi Osada (1991) and Hiroyuki Hirano (1995) [6][7].

### What is the difference between 5S, 6S and 7S?

6S adds Safety as a sixth step [1][5]; 7S variants add a further element such as "spirit" or oversight [5]. Many plants instead build safety checks into each of the five steps and into the audit, which avoids treating safety as an add-on.

### What do 1S, 2S, 3S, 4S and 5S mean in industry?

They are shorthand for the individual steps in their fixed order: 1S is Sort, 2S Set in order, 3S Shine, 4S Standardize and 5S Sustain. The numbering matters because each step builds on the previous steps; the EPA notes that Set in order can only start after Sort [3].

### What does 5S stand for in the medical field?

The same five steps, sometimes with different English words. AHRQ's health care workflow toolkit uses sort, straighten, scrub, standardize and sustain [4]. In hospitals the method typically covers supply rooms, carts and patient care areas.

### What is visual management?

The LEI defines it as placing tools, parts, production activities and performance indicators in plain view so that everyone can understand the status of the system at a glance [9]. 5S is its foundation; hour-by-hour boards, kanban, andon and team boards are typical tools.

### What is the difference between visual management and visual controls?

Visual management is the overall system of making status visible and acting on it. Visual controls are individual devices that guide or limit behavior, such as min/max lines, shaped tool cutouts or color bands on gauges.

### What colors does OSHA require for floor marking?

OSHA requires permanent aisles to be appropriately marked [14] and assigns red to fire protection equipment, danger and emergency stops and yellow to caution and physical hazards [15]. An archived OSHA letter states that aisle lines may be any color that clearly defines the aisle and recommends a width of 2 to 6 inches [17]. Full color schemes beyond that are industry conventions.

### How do you decide what is needed during Sort?

Use a frequency rule agreed before the event: items used every cycle or daily stay at the point of use, weekly items in area storage, monthly or rarer items move out, and items used less than once a year or with unknown need get a red tag and a review date.

### How long does it take to implement 5S?

A pilot area can complete Sort, Set in order and Shine in a few weeks; making the routines stick usually takes several months, and a site-wide rollout often a year or more [unverified: practitioner estimate]. AHRQ names the time required as the main disadvantage of 5S [4].

### How do you sustain 5S?

With a short daily routine, scored audits on a fixed cadence, leaders checking areas as part of their standard work, quick response to findings and regular updates of the standard. ASQ warns that without formalizing Standardize and Sustain, the initiative fails [2].

### How do you score a 5S audit?

Score a fixed set of items (for example 25, five per S) on a 0-to-4 scale, add the points and divide by the maximum. Track the category scores, not only the total: Standardize and Sustain usually lag behind Sort and Shine.

### What is the difference between 5S and kaizen?

Kaizen is continuous improvement as a mindset and practice [43]; 5S is one specific method for organizing a workplace. A 5S event is often a team's first kaizen.

### What is the difference between 5S and Six Sigma?

Six Sigma reduces variation and defects through data-driven DMAIC projects [44]; 5S organizes the workplace through team routines. They complement each other.

### Can 5S be applied in offices or to digital files?

Yes. ASQ describes a 5S example applied to an enrollment template and process at Kaiser Permanente Colorado [2], and digital 5S covers shared drives, naming conventions and machine program libraries. Focus on shared resources rather than policing personal desks.

### Is 5S only for large manufacturers?

No. A single cell, a small shop or an office can use the same five steps, and NIST's Manufacturing Extension Partnership centers support small and midsize manufacturers with lean methods including 5S [28].

### Who should participate in 5S?

Everyone who works in the area, plus maintenance, EHS and finance for their decisions. Operators should decide on locations and visuals; managers should audit and remove obstacles.

### Does 5S really save money?

It can, but mainly through what it enables. In our worked example, search time alone barely covered the cost in year 1; adding a changeover saving at the bottleneck gave a payback of about seven months. Measure your own baseline.

### How often should visual boards be updated?

As often as the decision they support. An hour-by-hour board is updated every hour, a team board daily, a 5S audit trend weekly. A visual that is not kept current should be removed.

## Conclusion and first steps

5S is simple to explain and hard to sustain. Its five steps turn a workplace into one where the normal state is obvious and abnormalities stand out: Sort removes what is not needed, Set in order gives everything a marked home, Shine cleans and inspects, Standardize turns this into routine, and Sustain keeps it alive through audits and leadership. Visual management builds on that foundation and shows, hour by hour, whether work is on plan.

Lasting 5S differs from a one-time cleanup through the daily routine, the audit that leads to action, the link to a business problem and the respect for the people whose workspace is being changed. In the US, it also means designing 5S around OSHA's housekeeping, marking, exit route and lockout/tagout rules, which turns a lean method into a practical tool for both efficiency and safety.

**Your first steps in the next 30 days**

- [ ] Choose one pilot area with a clear business problem (changeover, quality, safety, searching)
- [ ] Take baseline photos, run a first audit with the 25-item template and observe search time for one week
- [ ] Agree a site floor-marking legend and tag colors with EHS, based on the OSHA rules in this guide
- [ ] Mark a red-tag holding area and name the decision owners, including finance
- [ ] Brief the team, and the union where applicable, and protect time in the production schedule
- [ ] Run the Sort event with the operators, then Set in order and Shine with maintenance and EHS
- [ ] Decide energy control for every cleaning task and write it on the cleaning plan
- [ ] Start the daily five-minute routine and a weekly audit, and post the score on the area board
