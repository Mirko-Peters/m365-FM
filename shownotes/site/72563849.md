## Key takeaways

- Alert fatigue is a system design problem: agents should become the orchestration layer so humans supervise decisions instead of clicking through every alert.
- Dwell time and true positives are better SOC metrics than MTTR, and multi-agent validation cuts false positives more than bigger models do.
- Agents need their own identities, least privilege, guardrails, audit trails and a phased rollout from assistive to semi-autonomous before they gain more autonomy.

## FAQ

**How does an AI phishing triage agent handle user-reported emails?**

It investigates each report in parallel and classifies it as malicious, benign or uncertain. The agent checks headers, sender reputation, URLs, attachment hashes and the wording of the message, then explains its verdict in plain language. Clear benign cases close automatically, clear malicious ones are escalated, and ambiguous ones go to an analyst whose overrides it learns from.

**How should security teams govern autonomous AI agents?**

Treat every agent as a privileged identity, not a software tool. Mirko Peters argues each agent needs its own Entra ID identity, least privilege scoped to its job, guardrails that decide what it may do alone, and complete audit logging. Analyst overrides should be captured and reviewed, because a high override rate signals bad guardrails or an unready agent.

**How long does it take to roll out agentic defense in a SOC?**

The episode puts a realistic timeline at 12 to 18 months across three phases. First, assistive agents suggest verdicts on low-risk workflows like phishing. Second, more agents are added for EDR, cloud and identity alerts, still with humans approving. Third, semi-autonomous agents execute reversible, low-risk actions while governance rules are tightened.
