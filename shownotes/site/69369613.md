## Key takeaways

- AI amplifies the existing operating model, so ownership of truth, access approvals and cost must be decided before a pilot scales.
- Identity, classification and lineage, and semantic contracts must be deterministic layers so probabilistic AI components stay within a bounded blast radius.
- Steer AI with unit economics such as cost per decision, each with a named business owner, plus time-bound, recorded exception pathways.

## FAQ

**Why do enterprise AI pilots stall when they move into production?**

They stall because production exposes operating model gaps the pilot avoided. Pilots run on curated documents, temporary access and relaxed policies. At scale, conflicting definitions, missing owners and access drift surface, so outputs become inconsistent, costs spike and compliance steps in. The model usually did what it was built to do with ambiguous inputs.

**How do you calculate cost per decision for an AI workflow?**

Divide the total monthly cost of the AI workflow by the decisions it produces. In the episode's example, $120,000 per month and 60,000 decisions give $2 per decision, compared with an $18 human baseline per case. The cost should include governance work such as evaluation, logging and access reviews, and a named business owner.

**What should an exception pathway record for AI and data access?**

Every exception should record who approved it, what it grants, why it exists and when it expires. The episode adds a fifth attribute: the compensating control active meanwhile, such as extra logging, reduced scope or monitoring. Without a recorded pathway, exceptions still happen through informal channels and quietly become permanent drift.
